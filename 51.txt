Netflix’s New Movie Is Being Compared to Parasite, but It Resembles Other Masterpieces Even More

====================================================================================================

As if the tale of Nathan Fielder and Elizabeth Holmes weren’t warning enough about the dangers of
letting a documentary filmmaker into your life, Netflix’s _Possible Love_, the new movie from
_Burning_director Lee Chang-dong, charts the increasingly tangled relationship between a
well-meaning documentarian and the working-class couple she’s chosen to put in her viewfinder.
Ho-seok (Sul Kyung-gu) is a welder whose company was bought and stripped for parts by a private
equity firm, still reeling two years later from his union’s brutal confrontation with the police.
Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong) is a novice filmmaker intent on capturing the state of South Korea’s factory
workers. It’s not, perhaps, the most groundbreaking topic—early on, she applies for a grant to
finance the project, and one of the people on the funding panel asks if she shouldn’t be focusing
on something more current, like A.I. But she argues, and Lee and co-screenwriter Oh Jung-mi would
seem to agree, that the fallout of corporate restructuring is no less devastating because it’s
less novel.

Ye-ji meets Ho-seok at a funeral for one of his former colleagues who has died by suicide—or
rather she meets his wife, Mi-ok (Jeon Do-yeon), because Ho-seok, a muscled knot of a man, is still
so shaken up by the strike and its aftermath that he can’t bear to go inside. He’s unable, in
fact, to talk about much of anything at all. Even in the group therapy sessions for laid-off
workers, many of whom have still not found new jobs, he maintains a stony silence and the posture of
someone who might sprint for the door at any second. Given that his mouth might as well be wired
shut, he’s hardly an ideal subject for a documentary, but Ye-ji fixates on him, perhaps because
she sees his deep pain and wants to help him confront it, or perhaps because she’s got a strong
instinct it will play well on camera.

While she’s waiting for him to crack, she grows close to Mi-ok, who seems as quiet and
inward-looking as Ye-ji is upbeat and outgoing. The two develop a real friendship, and Lee and
Jung-mi don’t flatten the two women’s dynamic into a simple matter of a predatory filmmaker and
her unsuspecting subject. (The movie’s nearly three-hour length gives it room to follow its
developments without pushing us to draw easy conclusions.) But Ye-ji’s camera both figuratively
and literally comes between them. Once, while sitting outside one of Ho-seok’s therapy sessions,
they’re chatting, and Mi-ok relates a story from her childhood about how her mother, who worked in
a car wash, grew upset when she saw her playing with a hose, because she didn’t want her daughter
to live the same difficult life she had. Ye-ji listens to her, but we can feel a growing anxiety as
Mi-ok keeps talking, and finally she breaks: That’s a great story, she tells Mi-ok. Can you just
wait for me to get my camera, so you can tell it again? While Ye-ji is sprinting back to her car,
Mi-ok furtively snaps a picture of her fancy leather backpack and searches the image to find out how
much it costs—a reminder of the gulf that exists between them. (Later, Mi-ok discreetly advises
Ye-ji that it might not be such a good idea to be interviewing laid-off factory workers in her
expensive clothes, and Ye-ji abruptly starts showing up to work in worn t-shirts.)

As Mi-ok re-tells her story, _Possible Love_ jumps back in time to show us the moment she’s
thinking about, although it’s not clear if we’re meant to be in her memory or the images that
her words are creating in Ye-ji’s mind. (She’s already revealed that, while she’s making
documentaries “for the moment,” she expects to eventually move into fictional narrative.) In
fact, for a movie that explicitly deals with the way the presence of a camera affects how we see our
own lives, the passage from present to past happens with a surprising absence of fanfare, so
seamlessly it may take you a moment to notice it’s even happened. Lee does it a few more times
over the course of the movie—to show us, for example, an episode from early in Mi-ok and
Ho-seok’s relationship, when she was working in a factory and he was finishing his military
service. But they don’t seem to be evolving toward any particular end, so much as reminding us
that there are pieces of these characters’ lives that we will never see or entirely understand.

Where Mi-ok and Ho-seok live, those who still work at Ho-seok’s company are called “the
living,” and former workers are called “the dead.” Ho-seok’s co-workers still describe the
sense of solidarity they felt banding together against the company’s restructuring, and Mi-ok
refers to it as an “_okswae_”strike, roughly equivalent to “all or nothing.” But nothing is
what Ho-seok feels like he’s been left with. Sul played the man whose mysterious suicide sent
Lee’s 1999 movie _Peppermint Candy_ spiraling backward in time in an attempt to explain it, and he
carries a similar sense of grief with him here, but it’s mixed with rage and a profound sense of
betrayal—not to mention a fear, as much on the audience’s part as his, of what might emerge if
the walls protecting him from his own emotions were ever to crack. As their wives grow closer, he
and Ye-ji’s husband, Sang-woo (Zo In-sung), end up spending more time together, but Sang-woo is
tall and handsome and comes from money that he’s made into even more money, and he moves through a
world that seems impossible for Ho-seok to imagine.

_Possible Love_ isn’t interested in condemning Ye-ji and Sang-woo, or holding up Ho-seok and Mi-ok
as exemplars of blue-collar stoicism. And though the movie’s nationality and the common presence
of Cho Yeo-jeong has drawn some comparisons to _Parasite_, I’m put more in mind of the movies of
Ryusuke Hamaguchi, which often trace how economic disruptions ripple through the fabric of a
community, or even Krzysztof Kieslowski’s _The Decalogue_, which it’s semi-officially adjacent
to. (Kieslowski’s frequent collaborator Krzysztof Piesiewicz is an executive producer, and the
movie opens with the text of the Tenth Commandment: Thou shalt not covet.) Lee isn’t going for
razor-wire satire; when we find out Sang-woo makes his money in private equity, the same field that
led to the destruction of Ho-seok’s job, it’s not so we can quickly judge him—he still seems
like a nice enough guy. But it wants to insert the idea that when there are larger forces at play,
individual characteristics get swept away. Toward the end of the movie, Sang-woo triumphantly closes
a deal he’s been working on for a long time, but we’re so distant, physically and otherwise,
from wherever it’s taking place that we have no way of understanding its consequences, and neither
does he.

There are no easy outs in the movie’s performances, either. Lee’s stellar quartet of leads give
us complicated humans rather than representative figures, people who may do things for good reasons
or bad but rarely for only one, whose best intentions are tempered by failure and self-interest,
whose feelings come nearer to the surface but rarely break all the way through. Ye-ji’s filmmaking
brings them closer together, at least for a time, but as with the solidarity between Ho-seok and his
former comrades, that closeness is born of circumstances that are intense because they’re
temporary. Larger, more lasting change stays just out of reach: possible, but never quite real.
