Young Men Gave Us Trump 2.0. Now They’ve Taken It Away. No One’s Talking About the Reason Why.

====================================================================================================

Here is a question I have asked myself with increasing urgency over the last decade: Am Inormal?
I’m a 35-year-old man with a wife, a career, and a bountiful network of friends. My lifestyle
overlaps with every stereotype attached to my demographic. I play video games on weeknights and
watch football on Sundays. I am known to make the occasional $20 wager; I wear a lot of J. Crew; I
order Jameson at bars. I have rolled my eyes at some of the more exhausting overreaches of wokeness,
but I’m directionally progressive in nearly all political lanes. I listen to every episode of _The
Bill Simmons Podcast_; I do arms and shoulders at the gym. These are the rituals that have put me in
touch with my masculinity—taught me to appreciate my inner brawn, to learn to love what it means
to be a dude. And for a very long time, I assumed that most of my fellow men knew themselves in the
exact same way that I did. But then along came the shambolic reign of Trump, and his total capture
of my identity.

How did Trump storm back into office? Or more to the point, how did he get there in the first place?
Too many reasons to count, but one storyline reigned supreme in the wreckage of 2024: The president
cultivated a coalition of disaffected bros, whom he expertly managed both at the ballot box and in
the zeitgeist. MAGAdom enthralled all avenues of male interest and culture, infusing them with
Trump’s unique brand of grievance politics. Sports media? That’s now dominated by Barstool
oafs—in garish ballcaps—handing out doomed parlays in moldering basement podcast studios. Video
games? YouTube is awash with a class of snarling incels warding consumers away from _Grand Theft
Auto VI_ because, surely, controlling a female protagonist with a joystick is evidence of a
cuckolded mind. The list goes on and on. You can’t go see the summer’s hottest blockbuster
because it is insufficiently deferential toward Western sovereignty. You can’t download a dating
app because all women are conniving and evil—and furthermore, enjoying sex makes you gay.

Trump alone did not engineer the rot eating away at American manhood—tremors of this generational
backslide were already rumbling underneath our feet. But he capitalized on it with aplomb. That is
why Logan Paul gets invited to the White House; that is why J.D. Vance retweets the
white-supremacist _World of Warcraft_ personality Asmongold; that is why we’ve all been
propagandized with the faulty idea that Elon Musk—who has one of the strangest bodies the world
has ever seen—is a Chad.

And so, I ask again: Am I normal? Or is my station in life—a straight dude who relishes his
masculinity without being a total freak about it—in rapid decline? Am _I_ the outlier? For a while
there, I really thought I might be. The MAGA takeover of the things I hold dear seemed too
comprehensive, too airtight, too meticulously planned. Its virulent worldview was drilled into the
basic fundamentals of manhood, online and elsewhere. I had resigned myself to the rare life of an
adult male liberal; the last of a dying breed.

Then came the midterm rebuke, in which young men backed the Democrats by a 60 percent
margin—moving further left than any other demographic—injecting fresh life into my flagging
caucus. Innumerable factors contributed to the Republican demise—the skyrocketing cost of living,
yet another Middle East adventure gone wrong—but when the dust settled, and the scale of the
defeat became clear, some analysts floated the term “chud fatigue” to articulate what had
happened. Chud fatigue is essentially the theory that voters were exasperated beyond repair by the
figures who gained prominence on the back of the Trump wave. That maybe society was better off when
a boring liberal consensus rested at the center of our nation. Undoubtedly, chud fatigue has existed
for ages within the party’s most reliable constituencies—women and people of color, who never
needed to grow tired of the Paul brothers in order to despise them. But at long last, it seems like
chud fatigue has crossed over into the black box of the swing-voting white male. That means a new
political dawn is upon us; men are ready to be normal again.

I say all of this because I saw it coming. Weeks before the midterms, the chud fatigue reckoning
came to me in a sibylline vision; a flash of light portending a future in which men returned to the
Democratic Party en masse. Where was this prophecy unveiled? In one of the great melting pots of
male culture: the Instagram comments for ESPN’s _College GameDay_.

Just go with me for a minute. _College GameDay_ is an ESPN show that airs before the weekend slate
of college football games from late summer to early winter. It’s been around since 1987, and has
traditionally starred a roundtable of grizzled former coaches offering their insight on, say,
LSU’s rushing attack. _GameDay_ is genuinely beloved, more so than other pregame shows. If you are
a man under the age of 50, there is a good chance you spent much of your youth watching Lee Corso
rev up the Michigan State Spartans on an otherwise dreary campus in mid-November. However, in 2022,
ESPN changed the nature of _GameDay._ The broadcaster turned over one of its marquee panel slots to
Pat McAfee—an NFL punter turned sports-media mogul with a distinct MAGA-ish gestalt. McAfee
isn’t explicitly partisan, but he does fit a chud-shaped mold. He is perpetually sleeveless,
making many of his public appearances in a black tank top and a crimson sunburn. His artistic
interests do not extend beyond WWE and remedial country music. And yes, he has run into several
baffling political controversies that seem to indicate where he stands on the culture war, such as
allowing Pittsburgh Steelers quarterback—and hardcore MAHA truther—Aaron Rodgers to promote
vaccine disinformation on his show.

Nevertheless, everyone implicitly understood why ESPN made this move. McAfee’s ascension was
designed to defang the network’s buttoned-up aesthetic, making it more palatable to this profane
horde of Trumpified young men who—especially during the bleary-eyed miasma of 2025—were
understood to represent a new cultural mainstream. That worked for a while, but in 2026, the worm
began to turn.

Here’s what I mean by that. If you scroll through the _College GameDay_ Instagram posts featuring
McAfee, you will find a ton of people trolling the host in the exact same way. Their thesis? McAfee
is cringe, and his shtick is tired. This point is expressed through an online dialect that
derisively mimics his brand of Barstool-tinged masculinity. For instance: McAfee is known for taking
his shirt off and flexing for the crowd on the _GameDay_ set, a macho non sequitur that—I
guess?—the audience is supposed to find endearing. This routine, more than any other, is savagely
mocked by the commenters. They do this under the guise of a cloying tribute. Here is what one user
left on a video of McAfee stripping down at a Utah Utes game in late October: “NO WAY! Pat took
his shirt off again! I can’t stop belly laughing,” reads the post, studded with cry-laughing
emojis. “I can’t wait to bet on all the games on Kalshi, Polymarket, Fanduel, Draft Kings! And
the one where Sydney Sweeney has no clothes! I love you Pat, I’m going to start drinking again!”

That comment racked up over 16,000 likes. Hundreds more just like them are spread across
_GameDay_’s social media accounts, and all of them share the same diction. They are scripted in
capital letters, and are smothered with emojis emblematic of a certain type of chud—fires, pint
glasses, eggplants. They often make reference to the rapidly ballooning college football postseason
bracket, perhaps the most visible example of enshittification eating away at male culture.
(“Expand the playoff to 1,000 teams!” reads one.) Some comments sarcastically beg for the
country-rap star Jelly Roll—perhaps the most overexposed MAGA-friendly celebrity of this era—to
appear on the _GameDay_ desk; always they are threaded with a rabid distaste for the suffocating
array of sports-betting commercials. My favorites are the ones punctuated with a biting
characterization of the sort of person who would ever find McAfee charming. (“I don’t care that
it’s 9am! No weekend custody, am I right Pat!”) And it is especially illuminating that so many
of these _GameDay_ comments scornfully quote the Shaboozey song “Tipsy”—ground zero for the
conservative takeover of the Billboard charts—and pop culture as a whole: “Pour me up a double
shot of whiskey!”

You might be wondering why I am drawing parallels between an embattled _GameDay_ host and a
gender-wide political earthquake. That’s easy. College football fans are not a reliably liberal
voting bloc, to say the least. That they are also broadly fed up with McAfee signals, to me, a vibe
shift that transcends party lines. When I read those Instagram comments, what I see is a contrived,
fratty, 2020s-era right-wing machismo that is officially past its sell-by date. A critical mass of
men no longer subscribe to this way of being—if they ever did in the first place. (It is telling
that according to polling, manosphere figures like Joe Rogan and Andrew Tate have rapidly ceded
popularity from their mid-decade highs, with many of them being held accountable in ways that have
felt elusive for years.) Why is it all coming to a head now? For my money, I think men are simply
tired of being treated like they’re idiots, which was the only thing the MAGA blueprint could ever
offer.

What do boys want? According to the White House, it is A.I.-generated anime ICE agents, and mashups
of Iran war footage with _Call of Duty_ iconography. It is Tony Hinchcliffe making low-effort jokes
about Puerto Ricans, and RFK Jr. holding court with Russell Brand. It is a horrifically chintzy
worldview that hems in all frontiers of the mind; one where you are assumed to retain the exact same
taste you did as a high-schooler—where irrelevant ploys for your nostalgia, like a _Legend of
Zelda_ edit starring Donald Trump, are expected to leave you clapping like a seal. It is, most
succinctly, Pat McAfee ripping off his shirt on the set of _College GameDay._ But last week, along
with the rest of the country, young men rejected that vision. I imagine they were lured by promises
of more accessible healthcare and cheaper gas, and were disgusted by the institutional right-wing
hubris that they could be kept in line by yet more racist tweets.

MAGAdom never did respect the intelligence of the men it so desperately wished to court. It coasted
along on swagger alone, stoking conspiratorial fears that the sanctity of masculinity was under
threat. Well, now it is 2026, and after enduring a decade’s worth of the garish Trump ethos—this
onslaught of sky-darkening Kalshi commercials, and awful Morgan Wallen chart-toppers, and
mind-numbing arguments about the ethnicity of Athena, all of which reached a keening crescendo these
past two years—I, and so many other men, have concluded that enough is enough. Please, for the
love of god, can we just be normal?
