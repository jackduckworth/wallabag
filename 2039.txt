The Great British Bake Off Is Supposed to Be an Escape. One Nagging Complaint Is Ruining It This Year.

====================================================================================================

It’s been a tough time to be a woman lately. Consequently, I, a noted woman, have chosen to seek
refuge elsewhere—namely, _The Great British Bake Off_, a beautiful escape where nothing and no one
can hurt you.

A _Bake Off_ (known as _The Great British Baking Show_ in the U.S., where it streams on Netflix) is
always a reliably pleasant watch. In fact, you can usually anticipate exactly how it’s going to
play out each year: A prodigious student baker wows the tent with talent beyond her years, an
affable gay twentysomething dutifully plays along with hosts Noel Fielding and Alison Hammond’s
innuendo, and there’s one contestant who is not good but manages to squeak through week after week
anyway. As judge Paul Hollywood would agree, consistency is key, and even though this season saw a
shake-up in the form of new judge Nigella Lawson, to her credit, it feels as if she’s been there
for much longer than just a few weeks.

So imagine my dismay when what should have been a promising distraction from the horrors of life
instead felt like a bizarre perpetuation of them. Early reactions to Lawson’s debut focused on one
thing: how much she ate. Social media posts and headlines zeroed in on the generous bites she took
when tasting each dessert; “Woman Shovels Garbage Slop Into Hog Mouth,” those posts may as well
have read. Her mouthfuls were too big, apparently, her enthusiasm for chocolate cake simply _too_
strong, such that it warranted numerous videos imitating and montaging her eating style.

Lawson is one of the most accomplished cookbook authors and food writers in the U.K. She has
published bestselling books, hosted numerous TV programs, and was even supposed to be awarded an
Order of the British Empire for her contributions to journalism and cookery. (She apparently turned
it down, our humble queen.) There are a dozen other things you could first talk about when it comes
to Lawson and food, but instead, all we got was mockery and policing of how a woman eats on camera.

One reason for this might be thanks to her _Bake Off_ predecessor, Prue Leith. While the owner of
countless admittedly delightful necklaces, the former judge’s attitude toward food could sometimes
feel out of step with a show about sweet treats. “Worth the calories” or “not worth the
calories” were questionable phrases she often employed when judging bakes, which is something that
Lawson almost immediately nipped in the bud when her turn came.

“A cookie should be quite indulgent and naughty,” Hollywood said in the second episode of the
new season, channeling some of Leith’s projections of illicitness surrounding desserts.

“I don’t approve of talking about food as naughty,” Lawson cut in. “We need to take our
pleasures where we can in life.”

It’s this mindset—which, yes, includes the way she eats without restrictions—and willingness
to push back that makes Lawson great for the show, and helps keep the long-running series fresh.
Instead of yet another season devoted to Paul Hollywood the God with his signature handshakes,
we’re getting a rapport more like divorced co-parents trying to have one good night with their
kids. Lawson’s not afraid to disagree with Hollywood, which means the judging itself is much more
dynamic. I’m hearing feedback I’ve never heard before—what does it mean to be “too heavy on
the bicarb”? And am I doing it, too?

As for Lawson’s response to the discourse, it’s quite simple: “I like to be able to taste
things properly,” she told Woman’s Weekly. “I’m an eater.” Which, as a person whose job is
to judge the quality of food, makes sense.

Lawson’s unabashed love of food has always felt radical, in a world that often tries to shrink
appetites, especially when it comes to women. “No one should feel guilty about what they eat, or
the pleasure they get from eating,” she wrote in her 2020 cookbook _Cook, Eat, Repeat_. “The
only thing to feel guilty about (and even then I don’t recommend it) is the failure to be grateful
for that pleasure.” Although the response to this attitude has ranged from cruel to perversely
sexual, Lawson has shown no signs of backing off of it, and for that I thank her.

In fact, in order for _Bake Off_ to continue to be my form of comfort and escapism, I’m going to
need Lawson to turn up the very thing she’s being criticized for. Start treating the bakers’
submissions like smash cakes. Lick the batter out of their bowls once they’ve put their bake in
the oven. And the next time Paul Hollywood advises her to take smaller bites—a real thing that
happened, according to her interview with Woman’s Weekly—turn around and eat him whole, too.
