El-Sayed’s Victory in Michigan Was Much Closer Than Anyone Predicted. What Does That Mean for What Comes Next?

====================================================================================================

Abdul El-Sayed, a public health official, narrowly defeated four-term Rep. Haley Stevens in the
Michigan Democratic Senate primary. The vote proved too close to call on Tuesday evening, but by
Wednesday morning, El-Sayed had claimed the win, and Stevens had phoned him to concede. The race was
called by about 10 a.m.

It’s a watershed moment in Democratic politics. No other recent instance of a left-wing insurgent
defeating the Democratic establishment candidate in a swing-state Senate primary comes to mind.
Stevens and her camp threw everything they had into the race. She enjoyed the support of both the
Washington and Michigan Democratic establishments. Super PACs spent tens of millions of dollars,
dwarfing the money spent on El-Sayed’s behalf, attempting to get Stevens across the finish line.
But El-Sayed ably weaponized these resources against Stevens, making her financial superiority the
core of his argument against her.

And yet the victory comes with more of a whimper, given expectations heading into the night. Despite
public and private polling showing double-digit leads for El-Sayed heading into the night, he
defeated Stevens by just 1 percentage point. Stevens put up strong numbers in rural parts of the
state to supplement her leads in metro Detroit, though that wasn’t quite enough to offset the
margins El-Sayed posted in Grand Rapids and Ann Arbor. The closeness of the race only underscores
one of El-Sayed’s challenges going ahead: reuniting a fractured party after a bitter primary
campaign. This was not a resounding verdict.

The most watched Senate primary in the country now becomes the most watched Senate election in the
country, as El-Sayed will face former GOP Rep. Mike Rogers in November. The matchup gives the
progressive movement that Sen. Bernie Sanders ignited 10 years ago a chance to try its case: that
left-wing populism is just as, if not more, “electable” in a marquee toss-up election than the
cautious moderation offered by the Democratic establishment.

The stakes are high not just for the people of Michigan, or for the candidate who wins control of
the Senate in the midterms. What happens in this state could color the Democratic presidential
primary in 2028 too.

The Michigan primary was a three-way race for most of its duration. State Sen. Mallory McMorrow, a
rising star in the party with a history of viral speeches, positioned herself as a middle-ground
option between Stevens’ institutional candidacy and El-Sayed’s fiery progressivism. It’s a
strategy that often works better on paper than in reality. Finding the perfect position appeasing
those on either side of an issue is exhausting work and prone to error. Eventually, Stevens
consolidated establishment and centrist support, while El-Sayed consolidated progressives. McMorrow
was squeezed out and quit the race in early July.

That created a true left–vs.–center battle in the final month of the race, as Stevens worked to
close El-Sayed’s polling lead. Stevens’ supportive super PACs—most notably the United
Democracy Project, a group backed by the American Israel Public Affairs Committee—opened the
spigots with both pro-Stevens and anti-El-Sayed television ads. El-Sayed, whose campaign wasn’t
poor but couldn’t keep up with Stevens’, countered that the candidate was bought and paid for.

The contest turned deeply nasty in the closing weeks. El-Sayed at one point had to direct his
supporters not to mock Stevens in gross personal terms online. But El-Sayed, who does not lack
confidence, didn’t always keep his tongue in check. He described Stevens as “a suit with a large
AIPAC bank account” who can’t “string together two coherent sentences.” He called her the
“least capable candidate in America.” Stevens wasn’t as personally hostile toward
El-Sayed—his (attempted) destruction was largely outsourced to super PACs—but that last comment
set her off. “Everyone in America understands you want to blame all of your problems on Jewish
Americans,” Stevens posted in response to El-Sayed’s provocation.

That post was one of many Stevens tells in the campaign’s final weeks demonstrating that she was
the one trying to play catch-up. Neither Michigan Sen. Gary Peters nor Gov. Gretchen Whitmer—the
latter Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer’s first attempted recruit for the Senate race—would
have endorsed in the race unless they felt they had to. Stevens’ campaign, meanwhile, went all in
on portraying El-Sayed as unelectable. El-Sayed, in the final week of the campaign, began to turn
his attention to Mike Rogers.

El-Sayed is eager to face Rogers, predicting, with characteristic moxie, that in the future, if
“you say ‘Mike Rogers,’ it’ll be like: ‘Remember when Abdul just completely demolished
that man’s political future?’ ” Well, it’s important to maintain a positive attitude. But
Rogers and his allies will be taking on El-Sayed with a nine-figure assault, addressed to a more
moderate electorate and without concern for maintaining a baseline level of cordiality.

Much of the Democratic Party’s future depends on whether El-Sayed’s impressive speaking ability
and quickness on his feet allow him to withstand the Rogers team’s onslaught. If a blue wave
materializes in November but a Michigan Senate seat flips red—and helps the Senate stay
Republican—the Democratic establishment might find it much easier to get its preferred candidates
nominated the next time. But if El-Sayed can defeat Rogers, the appetite for new kinds of candidates
will only spread further.

We’re not just talking about Senate races either. El-Sayed’s ability to defeat the
“unelectable” tag under such a bright spotlight could substantially affect whom Democrats
nominate for president in 2028. Progressive leaders, eager to put one of their own atop the ticket
after the Biden debacle and Harris loss in 2024, don’t just know this—they’re counting on it.
In both 2016 and 2020, “electability” was a fatal blow to Sanders’ presidential campaigns, and
any successor trying to fill his “lane” in 2028 would have to contend with the same issue. What
an asset it would be for that candidate to be able to argue, _They said the same thing in the
biggest Senate race of 2026, and he won_. And what a liability it would be if that candidate
couldn’t.
