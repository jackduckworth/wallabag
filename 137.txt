A Beloved Sci-Fi Franchise Has a New TV Sequel. Surprisingly, It Works.

====================================================================================================

A _Blade Runner_ story, all atmospherics aside, is always about the violent police enforcement of
boundaries between humans and human-created intelligent beings, called replicants. In Ridley
Scott’s 1982 cult favorite _Blade Runner_, based on Philip K. Dick’s _Do Androids Dream of
Electric Sheep?_, Rick Deckard (Harrison Ford) is a “blade runner,” tasked with finding and
“retiring” rogue replicants. An enforcer in a world where replicant labor is subjugated to human
will, his mission is to find and “retire” four replicants who have rebelled against their
masters on an off-world colony and returned to Earth to try to force their creator to lengthen their
lifespan. In Denis Villeneuve’s gloomy, beautiful 2017 sequel, _Blade Runner 2049_, Ryan
Gosling’s replicant character K is also a blade runner, working for a human boss hunting down
fellow replicants. K becomes involved in the uncovering of a secret: A replicant has given birth,
something that’s not supposed to happen.

In each of these movies, the humans act none too admirably, and the mood has been one of moral
ambiguity. Now, in the new Prime Video miniseries _Blade Runner 2099_, the humans are getting their
comeuppance. This latest addition to the franchise, created by Silka Luisa, is set in the same world
as the two movies. But there’s something remarkably different here: There’s been a replicant
uprising, and in a major reversal of the dynamic _Blade Runner_ is known for, the humanoids have the
upper hand, and are making humans do all the dirty work they once reserved for artificial beings.
“Blade runner,” in an act of reclamation, is now the name given to replicant police officers who
track down and arrest rebellious humans. Michelle Yeoh plays Olwen, a blade runner. _Euphoria_’s
Hunter Schafer is Cora, a human who’s pretending to be a replicant while in pursuit of yet another
secret that will shift the balance of power between the two parties: In _2099_, replicants, while
dominant, still have the vulnerability of built-in expiration dates—but what if they didn’t?

It’s hard not to sigh when you hear of a beloved movie franchise becoming television, but this
show works more often than it doesn’t. Here we have eight hours of classic _Blade Runner_ vibes,
with the show heavily indebted in its approach to the production designers and cinematographers of
the first two films. Bleak landscapes, monumental architecture, flying cars, rain in Chinatown,
replicants born out of oversize envelopes in a gush of goop—this show will make you swear off LED
lighting for good, recalling this degraded world’s tech-y pinks and oranges shining through the
smog. The show’s composer, _Shōgun_’s Leopold Ross, follows Vangelis’ score for the original
film, using moody synthesizers and heavy hits of saxophone, for the noir. The costumes play
along—nobody will ever cut a better figure than Schafer does in a wide-shouldered, padded jacket
and puffed pants that nip in at the ankle. Every scene brings a new look: the replicants staffing a
training center for human children who are learning to administer Voight-Kampff empathy tests are
dressed in jackets with epaulets that jut outward, making for ’80s silhouettes approaching
“David Byrne in _Stop Making Sense_” territory.

Eight hours of this admittedly puts the show at the risk of becoming too much. That’s a problem,
because _Blade Runner_, as a universe, is at its best when it withholds, then reveals. Ryan
Gosling’s K busting through a wall while fleeing his fellow blade runners in Villeneuve’s film
is a good example—of course a replicant has that ability, but the movie lets you forget he’s got
this power, get lulled by his apparent humanity, then be bowled over in the moment when it happens.
_Blade Runner 2099_, by comparison, has a replicant straight-up manhandle a bench anchored in
concrete in Episode 1, and by the end of the season, you’re watching a battle between replicants
where walls are coming down left and right, like it’s an HGTV home renovation show.

Likewise, the two films were spare in their delivery of emotional moments, reflecting both the dire
nature of the environment and the fact that half the people onscreen were replicants whose
questionable ability to feel and express emotion is part of the plot. Here comes a nine-year-old
spoiler for _Blade Runner 2049_, which you really should watch: In that film, there’s a
devastating scene when K realizes that his hopeful suspicion that he might, himself, be the one
replicant child who was born instead of made, was incorrect. In _2099_, often to the show’s
detriment, we’re getting a big revelation like this every episode or so. Who’s a replicant,
who’s a human, who’s an old-style replicant with an expiration date, and who’s something new;
this is television, and things must move along.

Still, _2099_’s actors commit and ultimately pull this off. Schafer has the perfect balance of
delicacy and strength—she’s fun to watch in the action scenes and moving in the emotional ones.
Yeoh, a trench-coated and severe figure, is, by turns, convincingly maternal. Matthew Needham, who
plays the unctuous and scheming Larys Strong in _House of the Dragon_, is excellent as a fellow
blade runner who’s far too effective at his job, and becomes a danger to the undercover Cora. A
character who appears in the latter episodes, Clothilde (Amy Lennox)—a replicant working with the
show’s primary antagonist—is manic and dangerous, like Daryl Hannah’s Pris character in the
1982 original, and offers some moments of actual comic relief. Humor, in Los Angeles, in the year
2099? It’s a rare, and welcome, thing, a little bit like this series.
