Slow Horses season six review – it gets harder to enjoy Gary Oldman's spy thriller every year

====================================================================================================

This is a new Slow Horses: season six is the first not to be guided by its creator, Will Smith, a
writer whose previous work on The Thick of It and Veep explained why this espionage saga had an
unusually sharp comic tongue. The absence of Smith's drollery is discernible in a more
straight-faced new story, but the bigger problem is one of a creative work naturally eroding over
time. Slow Horses is now old.

It's always been a difficult, delicate proposition. The staff of Slough House, a purgatorial
spin-off of MI5 where misfits and screw-ups are given boring work when it would make more sense to
just fire them, regularly outstrip supposedly elite spies despite being shambling losers. Leading
the field agents is River Cartwright (Jack Lowden), who has the good looks of a spy hero and some of
the skills, but is also a brittle, luckless and rather whiny wretch, who regularly has to be saved
from ignominious death. The jokes have helped us suspend our disbelief that River and the rest
haven't yet been riddled with bullets, and the relocation of traditional spy thrills to decaying
urban settings is still a tonic, but enjoying this conjuring trick gets harder every year.

[Kristin Scott Thomas in Slow Horses.]
The crisis descended in season five, Smith's last, with a flat story powered by generic terrorists
delivering blowback for British crimes abroad. Slow Horses also began to suffer from the curse of
the celebrated supporting character: when you know you have failsafe comic relief in your cast, the
temptation to promote them can be irresistible, but they don't always survive the exposure. That
happened to showboating virgin Roddy Ho (Christopher Chung), who had previously snarked from in
front of his computer but was now given a pivotal role, in which he soon became unbearable. Even the
star of the show Gary Oldman might have been better staying in the background as the physically
disgusting but intellectually unbeatable Slough House boss, Jackson Lamb: he's a brilliantly
conceived and perfectly performed cartoon, but in season five he started to burn out.

So, new lead writer Gaby Chiappe – who did The Level and The Beast Must Die, two lean and
underrated dramas – is pushing buttons on a desk that has several red lights blinking. She
responds by making the new Slow Horses more of a conventional thriller, with complex geopolitical
machinations almost entirely on mute; for the Slough House mob themselves, old friends unexpectedly
reappear.

Someone is bumping off past team members, which at first means a cruelly brief comeback for Struan
Loy (Paul Higgins), who meets a horrible end in the typically desolate Slow Horses milieu of a
shipping container parked on a dull expanse of estuary mud. When it becomes clear that the two
frighteningly confident, black-clad killers – played by MyAnna Buring and Kyle Soller – are not
going to be satisfied with slaughtering spooks from season one whom we barely remember, the whole
gang ends up on the run.

[Lenny Rush (left) and Christopher Chung in Slow Horses season six.]
As well as the re-emergence of a more important character from long ago, recent apparent departures
are reversed: Rosalind Eleazar is back as Louisa, Joanna Scanlan is back as Moira, and Hugo Weaving
is back as Frank, the bleakly amoral mercenary who is also, a bit implausibly, River's dad, and
whose narrative seemed to have played out perfectly well back in season four. None of them makes an
unwelcome return, but cycling back to familiar faces does feel like something that might be done by
a show that finds itself short of ideas.

The main issue, though, is the plot, which boils down to a long chase. The killers keep coming and
the slow horses, mainly River, chaotically run away – which often requires the murderers to
suddenly become incompetent in order for the story not to end prematurely. With the vicious banter
not quite as piercing as it used to be and the parallels to real political issues also not as
piquant – besides some stuff about Russia – it becomes more noticeable when tropes like trained
killers who can't shoot straight creep in. Having cast actors of the calibre of Buring and Soller to
play the new foes, the show inexplicably never gets round to getting to know them. Also struggling
to make an impact is Harry Lloyd, who looks as if he could be superb as a malevolent media mogul who
is blackmailing MI5 boss Diana Taverner (Kristin Scott Thomas), but who never gets a proper chance
to show us.

Slow Horses is still slick and confident enough, and it helps itself by casting Lenny Rush, an actor
who immediately improves anything he's in, as a civilian caught in this year's crossfire. But it
might have run its course.
