

====================================================================================================

Ghosts: The Possession of Button House review – new levels of giddiness unleashed in spooky spin-off

====================================================================================================

What on earth is happening at Button House? Kitty has tasered a police officer. Fanny is pulling
down her capacious Victorian drawers to show off her bottom. Robin the caveman has fallen in love
with a crusty eco activist (to be fair she looks quite a bit like him). If you've never watched the
BBC's haunted house comedy Ghosts – one of the finest family television shows in recent memory –
none of the above will make the slightest bit of sense. Neither perhaps will this glorious new film,
which picks up with the ghosts not long after the events of the final episode in 2023.

Often when British sitcoms get a spin-off what happens is the gag-to-runtime ratio dips. But
breaking out of the 30-minute episode structure seems to have freed up the writers to unleash new
levels of giddiness with added spoonfuls of silliness. The jokes keep coming, and it's every bit as
warm and comforting as on TV.

It's been a few months since Alison and Mike (Charlotte Ritchie and Kiell Smith-Bynoe) left Button
House, selling the crumbling stately pile to a hotel chain. But work on the refurb has stalled, and
the ghosts are pining after Alison, the only living person who can see them (long story). One of the
joys of Ghosts is the competing main character energy of Button House's deceased residents, played
to perfection by an unassailable cast. There's the sleazy Tory MP with no trousers, Julian Fawcett
(Simon Farnaby), forever caught with his pants down. And scoutmaster Pat (Jim Howick) with an arrow
through his neck. It feels mean to leave out the rest; each and every ghost could single-handedly
power a 90-minute script.

To say anything about the plot would ruin the fun. (I watched with a nine-year-old who hooted
throughout.) It really is a delight. My only complaint is the timing: if the BBC was serious about
public service it would have waited a couple of months and put this on telly at Christmas for a bit
of genuine festive cheer.
