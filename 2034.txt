Kelly Clarkson’s Meltdown Over a $15,000 Fake Bird Is Embarrassing for Exactly One Person

====================================================================================================

For the past 20-plus years right up until a few days ago, Kelly Clarkson, the pop star, former
talk-show host, and first and best _American Idol_ winner, was more or less regarded as a national
treasure. Now, in a story that has dominated celebrity news all week, a Brooklyn gift shop called
Art of Play is accusing Clarkson of putting its staff and neighbors “in danger.” Who could have
predicted that the young woman who performed “A Moment Like This” on the _Idol_ finale all those
years ago would one day be embroiled in a moment like _this_, involving the online outrage machine
and a $15,000 animatronic bird? Definitely not me.

We are now moving into Day Five of the Clarkson–Art of Play affair, and there are a few facts that
both sides seem to agree on. Neither party disputes that on Saturday afternoon, Clarkson visited the
Brooklyn Heights shop. Nor does either party dispute that, during her visit, Clarkson inquired about
the aforementioned pricey animatronic bird that was on display in the shop. Beyond that, the two
accounts diverge. Clarkson shared her version of events online shortly after the visit:

According to Clarkson, she “drove all the way to Brooklyn” because she wanted to check out a
store that she “thought looked really rad.” She “had a few things in mind to buy and literally
was looked at as if I couldn’t afford it and was told he could find something comparable for
me,” she wrote. “Wow, dude. You’re an idiot and just lost a huge sale. But y’all should go
check out Art of Play in Brooklyn if you enjoy being treated like Julia Roberts character in Pretty
Woman.”

The store followed up by posting its own version of the story on Instagram, where it said that
Clarkson had asked about “a one-of-a-kind singing and dancing bird automaton” named Molly, which
it said was attached to the store’s wall and was not for sale. Incredibly, Clarkson volleyed back,
pointing out that the item had a price tag that said $15,000 next to it, and that when she asked
about it, a store employee “elevator eyed” her and told her in a “condescending” tone that
he could find something comparable for her.

From there, a series of predictable events unfurled: The business’s social media accounts received
a groundswell of negative comments, and people started showing up in person to harass employees and
customers at the shop. One of Art of Play’s owners, Adam Rubin, who is also, incidentally, the
author of the bestselling children’s book _Dragons Love Tacos_, started speaking to the media
about the negative impact of Clarkson’s posts, and he did not mince words: “Kelly Clarkson put
us in danger,” he told New York magazine and other outlets. “People are getting threatened on
the street. … They’re calling. They’re emailing. They’re leaving reviews on every platform,
they’re review-bombing, they’re attacking anyone who mentions us on social media.” He called
the whole situation “an insane misunderstanding,” but one he could have let go. “You know,
everybody has a bad day. But things changed when she used her platform to attack us. It’s
asymmetrical social warfare.” Was he being a tad bit dramatic? Sure. “Aymmetrical social
warfare” is quite the phrase from the owner of a twee magic-themed store in Brooklyn. But he is
also more or less right—there are perhaps a few justifiable reasons for a celebrity to attack a
small business on social media, but Clarkson had no righteous cause to stand on.

Things might have ended there … if the security footage from the incident didn’t emerge.
Investigative journalist Pablo Torre obtained the footage and shared it on his podcast Tuesday
evening. The footage doesn’t have sound, so we’ll never know what truly happened, but Torre used
it as grist to make the point that Clarkson was the one who was more at fault: She was only in the
store for a few minutes, and only had brief interactions with its employees—it looks like she did
indeed ask an employee about Molly, and after she seemed not to like his answer, she left, saying
something else, likely a complaint about her interaction with the first employee, to a different
employee at the cash register near the front of the store on her way out. Footage of her leaving the
store shows she whipped out her phone almost immediately. The tweet that kicked all this off went up
about 15 minutes later.

Here’s what I think happened: Clarkson had a confusing interaction with a salesperson, because she
asked about an item that had a price tag on it that she was told wasn’t for sale for reasons she
didn’t understand. She interpreted this to be about her, and either took some kind of special
umbrage as a celebrity or had some kind of flashback to her working-class roots in Texas or both.
(It’s worth noting that she looked exactly like all the other patrons in the store that day,
according to the footage, but whatever.) Meanwhile, Art of Play seemed to have a bizarre policy of
displaying an item that it hadn’t really decided whether it was selling or not. I visited the
store myself to get a sense of the scene, and it absolutely seemed like an item one could buy,
though its price tag has passive-aggressively been changed to $15 million, which actually still
might not be an impediment for Clarkson, who according to online estimates has a net worth exceeding
$50 million.

Should Clarkson have fired off a tweet about this? Absolutely not, not ever, but certainly not in
the year 2026, when we’ve spent the past 10 to 15 years learning about the corrosive effects
social media has on basically every situation. Everyone likes to joke about how you don’t go up
against Clarkson in a singing competition and expect to win, but it goes without saying that you
shouldn’t face off against anyone with 8 million followers. It seems like it was genuinely a
misunderstanding, owing in no small part to Art of Play’s own confusing decisions, but even if it
weren’t and they were really in the wrong, what good could come of her attacking a small business?
Art of Play is not, as Torre pointed out, an airline with an entire customer service department to
monitor its mentions. It’s a store whose clientele includes a lot of children and their parents
who would probably prefer if their faces didn’t appear in social media videos that could lead to
them being doxed.

Clarkson’s outrage at being treated like someone who can’t afford a $15,000 bird is also pretty
wild: If you have enough money to get mad about being perceived as poorer than you are, you’re way
ahead of most people. Most of us aren’t mad that we can’t afford $15,000 birds, but we do wonder
why so much of our society seems to be oriented around people who can.

Those are my rapid-fire verdicts: Clarkson guilty, Art of Play merely annoying. And now we are left
to wonder why this whole thing blew up as much as it did. Clarkson’s original tweet has been
viewed _46.2 million_ times. That’s more than the population of many countries. The controversy
made _The View_, _Good Morning America_, and the New York Times. Torre, who is a Pulitzer
Prize–winning journalist, took it upon himself to investigate. In all likelihood, he was doing so
partly because of how silly it was, sort of as a lark, but nevertheless: Why are we all talking
about this? The simplest explanation is that it’s very, very funny. Do we long for low-stakes,
viral COVID-conscious lesbian bar–style drama to distract us from all the ways the world feels
immovably bad? Yes, but I suppose we have to remember that what’s low stakes to us is always high
stakes to _someone_, even if that someone happens to be a pretentious magic-shop owner or a pop star
fuming about not getting to buy an exorbitantly expensive bird.
