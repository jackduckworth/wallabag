Katie Miller’s MAGA Podcast Officially Too Embarrassing to Continue

Stephen Miller’s promotion to White House chief of staff will bring with it one small blessing: It
will mean the end of his wife’s mind-numbingly boring podcast, and, if we’re really lucky, we
can maybe even take it as a signal that she’s abandoning her plan to become a conservative
lifestyle influencer.

Over the weekend, Katie Miller announced on X that she had made the difficult decision to stop
recording new podcast episodes. With her husband’s important new job, she simply won’t have the
time for it, she claimed: “Nothing is more important than raising our four kids. While I’ve
loved the podcast, the time commitment is too great and now is the time to step away.”

There’s reason to be a little dubious about her explanation. How much time could she really be
spending prepping for friendly guests like RFK Jr. and recording this show each week? Given her
resources, it seems clear she could continue the show if she wanted to. So we can take her leaving
as a sign that, well, she doesn’t really want to. If anything, this timing is convenient: If she
was looking for an exit strategy out of _The Katie Miller Podcast_, her husband’s promotion
provides a perfect one, in that now she never has to admit what a flop the show was. Now she gets to
sound like a dutiful wife in line with her movement’s expectations of young mothers like her.

The podcast launched in August 2025 and has not been a resounding hit. While a few episodes have
featured notable guests and scored respectable viewership numbers, they were nestled between
episodes like the one from June that featured an interview with the founder of Drybar and netted
just 4,200 views on YouTube, and an April appearance from the U.S. energy secretary and his wife
that had an audience of just 6,200. Though Miller’s connections to the Trump administration no
doubt helped her score guests like Elon Musk and Nicki Minaj, she also frequently hosted people I
actively don’t want to hear from on a lifestyle podcast, like Federal Communications Commission
Chairman Brendan Carr.

If you try to watch an episode, you’ll quickly get a sense of why the show failed to catch on: I
turned on a July episode featuring Kelsey Grammer, during which the pair spent much of the early
part of the interview cooing over their new babies. Miller, who is 35, really thinks people should
be having more babies, so she shares how happy she is that Grammer has one, no matter that he is 71.
As other journalists have noted, Miller is not especially charismatic or adept at interviewing, and
revelations from the podcast rarely received much attention or made headlines. It’s easy to see
why: No one cares to hear how J.D. Vance keeps his marriage strong, to the extent that he’s even
telling the truth when he speaks about it, or that Musk loves cheeseburgers. No one wants to see the
softer side of these figures, because it turns out that in addition to carrying out abhorrent
policies, they’re often quite boring.

When Miller launched the show, she made a big deal about how conservative women needed more spaces
to gather online. “There isn’t a place for a mom like me, mom of three young kids, 4, 3, and
almost 2, and a wife, and trying to do a career, eat healthy, work out—there isn’t a place for a
mom like me,” she said at the time. (She now has four children.) Does the end of her podcast
definitively prove that … she was wrong? Unfortunately, no, because there are still plenty of
conservative women speaking out online to huge numbers (if not to huge midterms results). But Miller
may well have been wrong that she was the person to be the voice of the movement, and her ending the
podcast is at least a tacit admission of that. This leaves us with one less atrocious podcast in the
world (after she burns off the remaining two episodes) and one less aspiring content creator sitting
in front of a stupid neon sign. It’s not much, but it’s something.
