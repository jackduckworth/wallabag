A blue wave could take out Susie Wiles

====================================================================================================

Donald Trump relished delivering two devastating words — “You’re fired” — in his role on
the reality show “The Apprentice,” and years later, he repeated them incessantly on the campaign
trail, much to the delight of his audiences. But the president has a dirty little secret: He does
not like confrontation. In real life, he prefers to delegate the unpleasant task to an underling. 

This emerged as a pattern during his first term, when personnel churn was a constant. Trump often
had his chiefs of staff — there were several — deliver the bad news, and sometimes he would
even fire people on social media rather than confront in person. Rex Tillerson, Trump’s first
secretary of state, famously learned of his dismissal from his staff via a printed tweet after
returning from a diplomatic trip to Africa. FBI Director James Comey saw the news of his firing on
TV news reports. Once the person was gone, Trump often publicly insulted them, portraying them as
“lowlifes,” “nutjobs” and worse, never seeming to realize the glaring contradiction
— that a man with his alleged business savvy should have had better judgment than to hire such
people in the first place. According to a Brookings analysis, the president had a 92% turnover rate
among his “A team” during his first administration, the highest of any president in history.

Trump’s second term has been much more stable. Where before Trump had hired qualified people
— aside from his daughter Ivanka and son-in-law, Jared Kushner — recommended by experienced
Washington hands, this time around he has surrounded himself with nothing but the most loyal
sycophants. As a result, there has been much less personnel drama. Still, there have been a few
notable departures. 

The first was the richest man in the world, Elon Musk. After a bromance budded between the
billionaire and Trump during the transition period, when Musk essentially moved into Mar-a-Lago, the
president appointed him to head the Department of Government Efficiency and take a chainsaw to the
federal government. Then there was a rupture in the relationship and Musk departed, although they
seem to have mended fences. Since then, Trump has only fired a few people: Attorney General Pam
Bondi, Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, Centers for Disease Control Director
Susan Monarez, Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer, Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard
and Ambassador to Greece Kimberly Guilfoyle. (The latter three “resigned” under duress.) On
Friday afternoon, after only a few days on the job and widespread criticism of her tenure, Trump
dismissed White House press secretary Katie Zacharia.

The White House has insisted it’s entirely coincidental that all the firings have been women. But
as the New York Times reported on Wednesday, “In many instances, the female officials who departed
appeared to have failed some kind of loyalty test to Mr. Trump.” (You know how women are.) The
departures of these women isn’t because none of the administration’s men have been implicated in
misconduct or corrupt dealings. To the contrary: Some of Trump’s male officials and staffers are
stunningly incompetent and dishonest, but their behavior has either been overlooked or rewarded with
promotions and transfers.

So when reports surfaced that another high-profile female staffer may be on the chopping block, it
would have come as no surprise — if it weren’t for the fact that the woman in question is the
single most powerful female in the administration. 

Susie Wiles has earned the distinction of being Trump’s longest tenured chief of staff, and with
good reason: She has managed to stay on the president’s good side by enabling him and never
telling him what he doesn’t want to hear. She has admitted that herself, saying, “These seminal
moments where you have to go in and tell the president what he wants to do is unconstitutional or
will cost lives. I don’t have that.” 

One would certainly think that would guarantee her job security. But ahead of the monumental loss
Republicans are expected to endure during the upcoming midterm elections, Trump is reportedly raging
— and looking for a scapegoat. Apparently, it’s going to be Wiles. According to recent reports,
the president has been calling around to friends to see what they think of his chief of staff — a
common precursor to a Trump firing. According to POLITICO, plenty of people have their knives out
for Wiles, with one person close to the White House saying, “She’s burned a lot of people.” 

Since Wiles, who guided Trump to his historic 2024 comeback, has also been tasked with doing the
same in 2026, she is in the presidential crosshairs, even though there is little room to address
Trump’s overwhelming unpopularity because he won’t listen to anyone. 

This, though, is an occupational hazard for any Trump chief of staff. It’s also not unusual for
turnover following a midterm election. But the GOP is facing what looks to be a massive loss,
attributed to the president’s historically low approval ratings among the American people. Since
Wiles, who guided Trump to his historic 2024 comeback, has also been tasked with doing the same in
2026 — along with her partners James Blair and Chris LaCivita — she is in the presidential
crosshairs, even though there is little room to address Trump’s overwhelming unpopularity because
he won’t listen to anyone. 

The big question now centers on Wiles’ replacement. POLITICO reported there are three likely
candidates. One is Bill Pulte, the head of the Federal Housing Finance Agency and chairman of Fannie
Mae and Freddie Mac, a fierce Trump loyalist known most recently for spending as much time as
possible with the president, feeding him memes and ideas on big white poster boards. Another is
Kevin McCarthy, the former House Speaker who once told an aide to fill a jar with only the red
Starburst candies for him to give to the president because he had noticed that Trump preferred them,
earning him the presidential sobriquet “My Kevin.” Then there is Stephen Miller, the notorious
senior adviser of everything.

The choice of Pulte or McCarthy would signal a continuation of current practices, albeit with less
discipline and adult behavior in the White House, which may be inevitable no matter who wins the
job. Pulte is dramatically unqualified, but since Trump once made him acting director of national
intelligence, that distinction is obviously not an impediment.

McCarthy is one of the most incompetent and ignorant leaders in GOP history, but this is likely a
plus, since Trump likes people who are just like him. If anyone expects that the president is
looking for someone who is stronger than Wiles, the fact that McCarthy is being considered should
lay those thoughts to rest.

The most frightening prospect is, of course, Miller, who is already serving as the shadow president
and would, as chief of staff, essentially have the full power of the presidency at his fingertips.
Trump is obsessed with his legacy projects and making as much money as possible, and the only other
thing he cares about is vengeance on his enemies, a desire he shares in equal fervor with Miller. If
Miller has the full power of chief of staff, we can assume that he will be ordering and approving
the most authoritarian policies the United States has ever seen, and Trump may not even know it’s
been done. That is clearly Miller’s mission, and given the power to enact such policies, he will
not hesitate. 

Being far more ruthless and determined, Miller would no doubt run a tighter ship than Pulte or
McCarthy. Trump, though, will still be in charge, and it’s easy to imagine that Miller’s actions
would make him even more unpopular than he is now. That could make the legacy-concerned president
uncomfortable as he confronts the possibility that he will leave office with a personal reputation
far worse than Richard Nixon’s and a record of failure lower than Jimmy Carter’s.

It’s not too early to start blaming Miller for Trump’s unpopularity, and Democrats would be wise
to start doing that publicly as soon as possible. If he becomes the chief of staff wielding power
with fascist fervor, it may just be too late.
