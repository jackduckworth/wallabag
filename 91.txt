Netflix Just Released the Ultimate Show for Cinephiles. Its Host Is As Dumb As a Brick.

In the history of jobs, no one has ever been as bad at theirs as Philomena Cunk. In the spirit of
Diane Morgan’s fictitious TV host, who has presented herself as an expert on everything from
Shakespeare to the _Mona Lisa_, I have not fact-checked that claim, trusting that an authoritative
tone is enough to establish its veracity, or at least to keep my audience from scrunching up their
foreheads and lunging for their phones. Cunk began life as a character on _Black Mirror_ creator
Charlie Brooker’s week-in-review series _Weekly Wipe_, where her ill-informed musings jostled with
the pungent commentary of a blokey comedian named Barry Shitpeas. But the people demanded more Cunk,
and so she spun off into a series of specials and limited series: _Cunk on Britain_, _Cunk on
Earth_, _Cunk on Life_—you get the idea.

In a now-iconic blue dress shirt and blazer, Philomena Cunk might have looked the part of a studied
authority, but she sure didn’t sound it: Morgan hails from just outside Manchester, and her native
accent, sometimes thickened for effect, deliberately subverts the received pronunciation once
synonymous with British broadcasting. The trick is that sounding like she couldn’t find Cambridge
on a map allows her to play the fool with real-life experts who have no choice but to smile
uncomfortably as she asks whether it’s true that “King Arthur came a lot.”

The Cunk persona allows the shows to send up the shopworn conventions of the TV documentary form, as
when Philomena stands among a series of low, ancient stone walls, and intones, “It’s hard to
believe I’m walking through the ruins of the first-ever city—because I’m not. That’s in
Iraq, which is miles away, and fucking dangerous.” Then again, why did we ever think that simply
standing near the place where something happened gives a person any special insight?

The newest contribution to Philomena Cunk’s catalog of anti-knowledge is _Cunk on Cinema_, a
six-part series that arrives on Netflix on Sunday. Cunk corners a cavalcade of movie experts,
including director Michael Bay, _Die Hard_ screenwriter Steven E. de Souza, _Project Hail Mary_
composer Daniel Pemberton, and several prominent British film critics, as she works her way from the
Lumière brothers’ cinematograph all the way up to the dawn of generative A.I. And yet there’s
something a little different about this installment, which is that, try as the series might to get
everything wrong, it actually ends up being a tiny bit educational.

Philomena Cunk might think that the name of Hollywood’s biggest Western star was “Wayne
George,” but the series, perhaps in spite of itself, traces the same path as a decently
informative survey class. Turn off the sound and ignore the puzzled faces of her interview subjects,
and you might as well be watching a non-parodic recap of cinema’s first century. There’s a keen
irony in the fact that a series whose host is concerned she won’t understand _2001_ if she
hasn’t seen the first 2,000 movies also likely represents the first time many of the movies it
cites have appeared on Netflix, a service whose conception of movie history otherwise peters out
somewhere in the 1980s.

_Cunk on Cinema_’s interviews are a different story. The character thrives in interaction with
subjects who don’t seem like they’re on to her, duped by Morgan’s slow-witted persona into
thinking she must be the one who doesn’t know what’s going on. (The historian who balks at
Cunk’s insistence that the ancient Romans invented anal bleaching looks as if he can’t decide
whether to storm out or lose his mind on the spot.) But considering that every film critic I know
lost their mind when Philomena Cunk’s next subject was announced, it seems safe to assume that
_Cunk on Cinema_’s participants knew exactly what they were in for. They’re pretty good at
playing it straight, but that delicious bafflement is nowhere to be found.

In Bay and de Souza, Cunk appears to have found genuinely unsuspecting marks, but either Hollywood
types have less tolerance for bullshit or they’re simply less painfully polite. Bay corrects Cunk
when she asks why directors say a C-word at the end of every take (hint: it’s not _cut_) but
eventually seems so confused he simply doesn’t know how to proceed. “These are strange
questions,” he observes. De Souza simply starts to seem angry, refusing to humor Cunk when she
tries to show him the A.I. video of Buster Keaton she made on her iPhone—proof positive, as far as
she’s concerned, that the Kansas-born legend spoke with a Cornish accent. (Apparently Slate’s
Dana Stevens was not consulted.)

At barely 90 minutes (each mini-episode is about a quarter of an hour), _Cunk on Cinema_ runs the
length of a fluffy rom-com, and yet it touches a surprisingly number of the requisite bases—any
time you think, _Oh, I guess they’re not doing X_, it shows up a few minutes later. And for all
its studied stupidity, the series doubles as a pretty good look at the history of movies. Sure,
Philomena Cunk refers to Russian pioneer Sergei Eisenstein’s most influential film as “Batshit
Pumpkin”—and does it while standing beneath a marquee that spells out _Battleship
Potemkin_—but at least she gets the clip right.
