The Pitt Prides Itself on Accuracy. So How Did It Mess This Up?

====================================================================================================

In the last moments of the finale of _The Pitt_’s second season, which aired Thursday night,
Dr. Robby (Noah Wyle) is alone in a quiet peds (pediatric) room with a baby who was left in the ER
waiting area early that morning. It’s an outrageously tender scene, and any watcher is hoping that
this little baby girl, abandoned by her parents, will somehow enchant Robby enough to ditch his plan
to drive his motorcycle, helmetless, on what sounds like a literal suicide mission across the
country and take her home with him as a foster parent. Perhaps everyone could win here: Robby will
have someone to love, and this perfect baby will have a guardian.

It’s very touching. But the scene also features a pretty wild mistake: Dr. Robby cannot swaddle a
baby to save his life.

This wouldn’t matter, except for two things. The first is simply that Dr. Robby says to the baby:
“Why are you crying? Why are you crying, little one? You’re safe. You’re not alone. You’re
OK. Do you need to be swaddled again?” He then starts limply folding the blanket over the baby in
a loose and haphazard way. This would be fine if he were just putting the blanket around the baby to
pick her up. But he said he was going to swaddle her. This is not what he does!

If you are a parent who has had a kid in the past 20 years (or maybe earlier—I can’t speak to
whether the craze goes back that far), you know the difference between a loose wrap and a real, bona
fide swaddle. When my older friends had kids in the early aughts, they were all reading _The
Happiest Baby on the Block_, a book that endorsed the “Five S’s” to calm your baby to sleep,
one of which was swaddling. Swaddling is no joke; you have to put your tiny, tiny baby in a
straitjacket of your own design. You are origami-ing them into submission. Experts tell you that
this is good, but it initially seems bad. It is scary putting their little arms by their sides and
immobilizing them with a pretty muslin cloth pulled tighter than seems OK, and then extremely
satisfying when you master it and your baby conks out. I took a lot of pride in my extremely snug
swaddles—this skill was overshadowed only by my ability to cut my baby’s fingernails without
nicking her. My ex-husband was even better. We were sad when our little daughter was finally strong
enough to break out of our custom cocoon; it was harder for everyone to sleep, and it was a goodbye
to her earliest weeks and months. (She’s 14 now and sleeps with a “burrito blanket” and buried
in fluffy throws, which I guess is like a teen swaddle.)

Why does this matter in the context of _The Pitt_? Why am I beating up on the near-unimpeachable
Wyle? Because this is a show that prides itself on accuracy, crows about it, lives by it. Earlier in
the episode, Robby performs a C-section on a mom whose life—as well as that of her baby—is in
danger. We see it all; every move, instruction, and gruesome slice is precise. Even medical
professionals have praised the series for how accurately it depicts everyday and extraordinary
traumas in the ER. Nothing on this jargon-filled, extremely graphic show is sloppy, ever. So why, in
the big send-off to the season, did _The Pitt_ whiff it on the swaddle? C’mon!

My ex-husband texted me this morning, in all caps: “THE SWADDLE WAS BAD” and surmised that
Wyle’s wife (and the partners of the other men at the helm of the show) must have taken care of
the kids, at least when they were babies. A co-worker texted that her husband immediately noted the
“shit swaddle.” I really hope that when we see Robby again, he’s 1) alive and 2) caring for
this baby. But I also hope, in the meantime, he has learned how to properly swaddle. And maybe
he’ll get swaddled himself—he needs it.
