

====================================================================================================

The Sensualist by Gary Shteyngart review – a delectable romp through life's pleasures

====================================================================================================

In 2024, the novelist Gary Shteyngart flew from New York to Milan in search of the perfect
midnight-blue suit. There he chose fabric, before flying to Hong Kong to be fitted for four shirts,
and on to Tokyo, where he was measured by master tailor Yuhei Yamamoto and master shoe­maker Yohei
Fukuda. A short king ("five foot five and a half, with that 'half' doing a lot of work"), Shteyngart
dreamed of a fit to disappear his 52-year-old moobs ("my tatas", "my chimichangas") and melt the
heart of the toughest maitre d' at the most in-demand restaurants.

And guess what? The world's finest craftsmen will give you all that – though Shteyngart, who
emigrated from Leningrad to the US in 1979, argues that a great suit serves a deeper need than
vanity. Trying it on two days after Trump's second election, "it was the final certificate of
naturalization … I couldn't make my adopted country fall out of love with fascism, but at least I
could enjoy my new shoes." Clothes become an advanced form of self-care.

In this first collection of essays, Shteyngart makes the case for pleasure in a world of joyless
productivity. Screw Mark Zuckerberg in his monk's hoodie. Screw the oligarchs and the mindful
drinkers. Here's a better life hack: choose two best friends "and form a martini trio for life".
Shteyngart adores chilled vodka, old watches and giant rodent TikTok. Again, this is self-care:
"'They regulate you down,' my shrink told me … as we watched a video that featured superimposed
capybaras sashaying down a fashion runway."

When Shteyngart loves a thing, he is all in. His capybara odyssey takes him from Tokyo to Florida
and on to South America, where he shadows a Brazilian capybara influencer and witnesses a standoff
between marauding rodents and a swanky new development in Buenos Aires. Yes, they are weird and
cute, with their "coconut-shaped" bodies and buck-teeth that never stop growing – but Shteyngart
also feels they are the animal for the zeitgeist: "a desperately friendly creature always afraid of
being attacked … Do we all feel trapped in a world that encourages us to be hyper-social yet
rewards us with nothing but endless existential anxiety?"

He got into martinis for health reasons (less sugar than beer or wine) though this is easy to forget
when Shteyngart is 10 drinks down on a New York bar crawl, trying to engage a wall mural in
conversation. A martini is "the elixir of quietude", he tells us, quoting EB White, though he
prefers "friendship juice". Friends are another revolt against the individualist new world order,
and in an introduction Shteyngart mentions he has lost three close ones in the past five years. He
doesn't dwell on this; instead he takes Gerri from Succession (Shteyngart was a consultant on the TV
show) to her favourite martini joint in the Village.

Fine suits, cuddly capys, cool celebrities: the world unfurls smoothly before Shteyngart, except in
Brooklyn, where he is ignored by waiters while millennials down their fruits de mer. As befits a
sensualist, his style is deeply pleasurable, great lolloping sentences meeting the punchline of
a short one, the jokes generous and surprising. The tone is confiding, charming, often unreservedly
silly. I have no idea why Shteyngart decides that wearing a T-shirt with the slogan DADDY'S LITTLE
MEATBALL will be the perfect icebreaker on a cruise ship but he brilliantly milks it: "the very
sight of me (small, ethnic, without a cap bearing the name of a football team) elicits no reaction
from other passengers".

Shteyngart teaches writing at Columbia and mentions that pleasure has been notably missing from his
students' work. Could it be that they can't afford it? His sensualism is very expensive, a long way
from touching grass, and the moral case for it retrofitted to a series of articles that tell us more
about American magazine budgets than the national mood. Is getting a beautiful suit an act of
rebellion or a tremendous treat? The Atlantic footed that bill, while the New Yorker picked up the
capybara tab, and each essay to some degree reflects the ambition of the editor who commissioned
it. The travelogues (for Travel + Leisure magazine) are the most narrowly observational; a
parenting piece for Men's Health is sweet but familiar.

The most outrageous, and least sensual, expense is the cruise, for which the Atlantic forks out
$19,000 for a last-minute suite. The trip is predictably hellish, with its bad food, litter-strewn
pools and Ayn Rand-spouting passengers, and even Shteyngart thinks the conceit (smart interloper
infiltrates _hoi polloi_) has had its day. David Foster Wallace made it look easy in 1996, and it
is. Docking at St Kitts, Shteyngart emails to ask if he can abandon ship. "'It would be funny …,'
my editor replies. 'Now get on the boat.'"

Being funny is the point, a pleasure in itself, but Shteyngart is at his best when his satire has
more bite. A week in a Four Seasons hotel watching Russian state TV starts in dilettantish fashion,
the author lolling on the bed with wagyu beef and pinot noir, but builds to something more sad and
meaningful. "What a powerful weapon Putin's television is," he writes. "How skilfully it combines
nostalgia, malice, paranoia … unless you're a true believer, its endless din just reminds you of
how alone you are in another man's designs."

Shteyngart's own parents have become radicalised, as have his friends, a generation who rant
"about the possibility of catching critical race theory from an open jar of mayonnaise". Caught
between the duelling propaganda of Fox News and Russian TV, they are fearful of vaccines and full of
strange advice. His own is to find your personal bulwark of "immense, ecstatic, unencumbered
ahistorical joy". Hug a capybara, pour a drink, buy a $4,000 watch. Cheaper options are available.
