

====================================================================================================

Jacob Bethell's place remains a puzzle as gifted batter is let down by stop-start approach | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

_One down. Devilish gamble on Aussie plonk (5, 7)_

Would Douglas Jardine have cheated at the crossword? No he would not. Or rather he would, but only
in a way that still technically complied with the current rules of crosswords, while also causing a
violent crossword-based diplomatic incident. Would Mike Brearley cheat at crosswords? No. He would
instead ask the crossword why it feels it needs to be so difficult.

There has been a search for a defining Brendon McCullum image on this haphazard Ashes tour: a
need-to-look-at-the-data moment, a McClaren's brolly. If anything we overprepared after the defeat
in Brisbane was good, and also dangerous because it made him look foolish, and the moment McCullum's
insouciance looks foolish the whole thing is in danger of collapsing.

On a rain-shortened first day of the fifth Ashes Test we got the crossword. England's head coach is
often seen doing crosswords during play. Here he was spotted flipping to the answers at the back of
his book and then seeming to fill in a couple of unsolved clues. The clip was recycled gleefully
across social media. It just seemed to fit with a man often accused of looking for shortcuts,
skimping on his homework and so on.

There was even a degree of frustration here in an unbroken partnership of 154 between Joe Root and
Harry Brook, enough to leave England in a belated position of strength in the final dead rubber of a
losing series.

Root looked beautifully in control on a good, even pitch. Brook played half the time like a man
desperately in need of the toilet, the rest like some kind of hyper-skilled alien princeling. But
perhaps the most tantalising unsolved riddle on day one was the sight of Jacob Bethell out there
batting at No 3 on the perfect stage, a lovely sunlit lime green oval, an elite Test attack, the
event-glamour of the New Year Test.

In the event Bethell produced a perfect miniature of his time around the Test team so far, all easy
lines, symmetry, balance, shadow brilliance, and always leaving you wanting more. He is obviously a
genuine high-end talent. But does anyone actually know what to do with this? Do we trust these
people not to break him?

Bethell came out to bat here at 35 for one after Ben Duckett had breezed his way to a fun and
flighty 27, opening the innings with all the hard, seasoned permanence of Paddington Bear having an
accident with a bar of soap and a skateboard. Bethell faced the final ball of Mitchell Starc's
fourth over. He curtain-railed his bat inside the line, sleeves rolled, all springy, coiled intent.

And as ever he looked great. He left, he nudged, he defended in tight shapes. He ticked along to
nought off nine balls. Bethell has no trigger movements. He just stands still and plays from the
crease, a tribute to the speed of his hands and the fast-twitch energy in his feet. Scott Boland
ripped one past his outside edge, a good, cinematic play and miss. Nought off 11. Then it was nought
off 13 as Starc whizzed a really horrible bouncer across his nose.

[Jacob Bethell plays a shot during his brief innings on day one at the SCG.]
Ball 15 was shorter, and beautifully crunched through point to break the spell. At which point you
couldn't help thinking, hmm, maybe they have solved this. Maybe they can game Test batting. Maybe
the clue was there in the back of the book all along.

The answer to this will take a little decoding. And it will require some care from here. Bethell did
a very interesting interview with Nasser Hussain on a Sky podcast released just before this Test. He
sounded smart, confident and single-minded in the way of all elite sports people. But he also said
some odd things.

Had he read, Nasser asked, Greg Chappell's recent article suggesting Test batters needed to defend
better? No, he hadn't. But, yeah, Bethell supposed, you could defend more. But you won't score many
runs that way.

Chappell scored 7,000 of them. Bethell has yet to get to 20 in the first innings of a Test. Hmm.
Does he think young players should start at No 3 or work their way up? No, no working up, his game
is perfectly adjusted to batting three. Which may be true. But how does he know this? How does he
know it with such certainty? Bethell has never scored a first-class hundred.

Later he said he never listened to any outside advice or suggestions ("noise"). Really? Why not? And
is it helpful that the only voices Bethell hears are those of his captain and coach, who both favour
positive reinforcement, the you-go-girl style of preparation? Doubt Kills More Dreams Than Failure
Ever Will. This is McCullum's X account strapline. Maybe that's true too. But doubt can also be a
useful tool.

Bethell was out here caught behind edging a fine ball from Boland, although even this was a good
edge, an edge that flew, that looked right. Would a No 3 who had played No 3 for a year, who was
utterly plugged into that role, have been able to leave it?

We will never know, or at least not for now, because Bethel is one of the areas where Bazball (as
was) has blinked in the past 12 months. They really should have trusted the initial impression in
New Zealand, should have had him in all year in that slot.

How would Bethell look now if he'd played all summer, then started in Perth, as opposed to being
starved of cricket after sitting on the bench at the Indian Premier League (and please, spare us the
standard learned-so-much stuff)? Like everything else in this cramped and complex sport, this is a
question wrapped up in guesswork and cryptic clues. Although as ever, and just as at is to one down
above – _Jacob's Creek; bet hell_ – the answer for England is still likely to be Jacob Bethell.
