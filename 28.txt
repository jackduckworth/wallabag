Democrats Are in the Middle of a Remarkable Comeback—Mostly Because of One Man

====================================================================================================

Shortly after their 2024 disaster, Democrats couldn’t even console themselves with thoughts of
2026. The House might have been in play, but they were facing a brutal Senate map—one that made it
hard to see them picking up many seats even if voters had soured on the party in power.

The only Senate Republican up for reelection in a state won by Kamala Harris was Maine Sen. Susan
Collins, and Collins is famous for her inability to lose. The next best opportunity was North
Carolina Sen. Thom Tillis’ seat, but Democrats hadn’t won a presidential or Senate race there
since 2008. After that? Bupkis. Once-swing states like Iowa, Florida, and Ohio had become red
states. Texas hadn’t been blue once this century, and retreated into safe GOP status in 2024.
Plus, Democrats would have to defend Democratic seats in Georgia and Michigan, two states Trump won
in 2024.

• Video from Slate 

Now, one day before the 2026 midterms, Democrats don’t just have an opportunity to net the four
seats they need to retake the Senate majority. They’re actually narrow favorites to do so,
according to election forecasters. Democrats hold delicate polling leads for Republican-held seats
in Maine, Texas, Ohio, Alaska; are neck and neck in Iowa and Kansas; and are throwing a few bucks
South Carolina and Mississippi’s way in the event of a generational night. On the defensive side,
Democrats’ biggest threat of losing a Democrat-held seat is in the tight Michigan contest. And, by
all accounts, Jon Ossoff has put the Georgia Senate race to rest, as has Gov. Roy Cooper in North
Carolina’s Senate race.

That Democrats have this good of a shot at the Senate majority isn’t something they would have
believed two years ago—or a year ago, or six months ago, or even three months ago.

It is, however, just a shot. A 2- or 3-point polling lead in any of these improbable states is
within the margin of standard polling error. There’s a world in which Democrats outperform their
own incredible polling, and the evening resembles the biggest landslide since the 2008 election. But
there’s another in which they underperform it, even modestly, as red states revert to their
partisan lean in the end. It could either be a historic night for Senate Democrats or a night with a
historic number of impressive but close losses.

Still, if you would have offered Democrats these odds in 2024, they would have scrambled for them.
So how, then, did Democrats get in this unlikely position?

For the most part, Donald Trump put them there. We’ve never seen a president do less to help his
own party heading into an election, with so little to show for it, than Donald Trump has. There are
6 or 7 million instances we could recite in which Trump has done the opposite of attempting to help
his party win, but the two defining policy moves of his second term (so far) are directly
responsible for the creation of this map.

Trump weaponized (and at times exceeded) powers ceded by Congress to both blanket the globe with
tariffs and threaten countries whose foreign leaders were mean to him with additional levies. This
has raised prices under a president who won the 2024 election on his promise to bring prices down.
The pain has been especially acute in farm country, which can’t export its products due to
retaliatory tariffs; the U.S. auto industry, whose supply chain is closely integrated with that of
Canada; and both northern and southern border states. We refer you, again, to the map of Senate
battlegrounds. Whoops.

And then, of course, there’s the war in Iran, a major conflict the president launched after
running on a pledge of no more forever wars. Aside from increasing gasoline prices for everyone, it
spiked diesel and fertilizer prizes, placing yet another burden on rural America. It has resulted in
the death and injury of Americans abroad, and caused a rift in the Republican coalition between more
traditional hawks and MAGA isolationists. It’s also a war that has not, at the present time, been
won by the United States.

These issues make the 2026 midterms such a different beast than the midterms of Trump’s first
presidency, in 2018.

In 2018, the economy was pretty good, and Trump’s approval rating on his handling of the economy
wasn’t half bad, either. Those midterms centered around some material factors—the attempted
repeal of Obamacare, for example. But so much more of it was a reaction to Trump’s very presence
in the White House, and the shock of his behavior, and his tweeting. That revulsion allowed
Democrats to pick up 40 House seats largely concentrated among suburban, college-educated voters
drifting away from the MAGA-fied GOP. But it didn’t spread into red America—especially with the
Brett Kavanaugh confirmation fight that fall firing up Republican base voters—and the GOP that
year flipped Democratic Senate seats in North Dakota, Missouri, Indiana, and Florida.

This year, Trump’s approval over his handling of the economy is in the low 30s, while his overall
approval rating is only 5 or so points better. It’s much more fertile ground for Democratic gains
across blue, purple, and red America than would be a campaign against Trump’s poor manners. “The
president is making it harder to pay your bills” is also a more effective line of attack
than—valid as they may be—highfalutin arguments about Trump’s threats to democracy and the
Constitution. But Trump’s behavior has not been entirely excised from Democrats’ message.
Instead of emphasizing his foulmouthed tweets, Democrats are marrying a populist corruption argument
with their broader criticism of the economy: that while _you_ are suffering, Trump and his allies
are enriching themselves. Corruption, according to some recent polls, has joined the economy as
voters’ most pressing issue.

Democrats were able to meet this opportunity in red states with quality recruits. The North Carolina
Senate race has effectively been over since Democratic Gov. Roy Cooper entered it. The only Democrat
who’s won an Ohio Senate race this century, Sherrod Brown, opted to run again. The only Democrat
to win a federal election in Alaska since 2008, Mary Peltola, was also convinced to run for Senate
instead of governor. (We’ll put a slight asterisk on the quality of the _overall_ effort to elect
Peltola in light of recent news developments.) Democrats landed friendly and compelling preacher
types in Kansas and Texas, and a moderate Paralympian in Iowa. Party leaders failed to get their
preferred candidates in Michigan and Maine—a purple and a blue state, respectively—but the
anti-GOP atmosphere may well carry Democratic nominees Abdul El-Sayed and Troy Jackson to victory.
(Of course, things got more complicated Sunday for El-Sayed when his primary appointment retracted
her support and endorsed his Republican opponent.)

So Democrats got the environment, gift-basketed to them by Donald Trump. They got the message. They
got the candidates. How gettable, really, is the Senate majority?

We need to reemphasize how tall an order this is, and the amount of ground Democrats need to make up
relative to 2024. That year, Trump won Iowa by 13 points, Ohio by 11, Kansas by 16, Alaska by 13,
and Texas by 14. All of those numbers are _just_ on the cusp of what Democrats’ advantages in the
national environment, candidate quality, and messaging will allow them to reach. If polls are
underestimating GOP turnout in these states even a little bit—or not able to capture a last-minute
GOP awakening—Democrats could be in for an agonizing night of close misses. Trump has brought
Democrats back from how far they’d fallen in 2024. Now it’s just a question of whether they can
breach the walls that two years ago seemed impossibly high.
