

====================================================================================================

Premier League money has lost all meaning amid chaotic spectacle of excess | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

There was a research study a few years back into a key period in the life of the TV show Location,
Location, Location. You know the one. Half an hour of mid-morning drift. Hosted by two people whose
common quality is they both look as if the minced essence of Wellington boots, the Volvo XC40 and
the phrase "picky bits" has been reconstituted into human shapes, then sent out to judge people for
buying houses.

The big reveal was always the bit where they came back a year later and saw how much the value had
shifted. Because all conversations about houses are, in the end, an excuse to talk about money. Oh
wow, it's gone up by £20,000. You are good. Oh no, you're now bankrupt and your flat looks like a
hostage cell with fairy lights. Unlucky. You are bad.

What the study found was that prices basically always increased. Ripping out the stairs, living in a
tent in the garden, arguing about what to do with the two-and-a-halfth bedroom – all of this was
irrelevant. These were the boom times of the early 2000s, UK housing recast as a global money trap,
an endlessly whirling laundromat, a bricks and mortar central bank bond.

These people could have bought their run-down three-bed, left it empty and done absolutely nothing
for two years. The price would have still gone up. That drama about exactly which shade of Anaconda
Breath Morgue And Cakebread Mega-Gloss to use in the underfloor wine cave? Just doesn't matter. Buy
the soil. Shut the door. Let money do its thing.

And yes, this is a way of talking about the 2026 summer transfer window, and the way in which
everyone has basically now been Chelsea-ed. Think back four years to the start of the age of
Clearlake, the first post-Roman spree. At that time it seemed impossible there could be a profitable
outcome. Chelsea were out there buying the vintage copper-plate statement bidet. Chelsea were
digging out a third sub-basement. Who will redeem this expense? Who will provide a similarly
deranged response to such overinvestment?

Well, it turns out the market will do this for you. If you'd predicted then that Chelsea would miss
out on the Champions League and go through five permanent managers, but still make a profit from
selling Enzo Fernández, would anyone have believed it?

[Todd Boehly of Chelsea during a training session at Drake Stadium UCLA Campus on July 14, 2022]
Or that Mykhailo Mudryk could run around briefly like a deranged hamster, get banned for failing a
drugs test and still become a potential growth asset? This is pre-subprime crash energy. Yes, you
can install a solar-powered mega-wetroom that fires sourdough breadsticks directly into your eyes.
Whatever. Ownership is all. Money will take care of you.

There are still levels to this. The one Chelsea thing to shift in the window is that the curtain has
now been drawn on those initial eight months, the period where Todd Boehly became the world's worst
director of football, a director of football who basically didn't know what football was.

That period has now been safely capped off. Only two players remain. Rough out the numbers and
Chelsea spent something like £200m in fees, wages, leaks and cuts, with only Malo Gusto and Wesley
Fofana to show for it. No real damage. Money comes, money goes. We roll on.

This is the lesson of the latest summer transfer window. Right now it doesn't really matter what you
do in the Premier League. Just being in it is the thing. With one caveat, which is that nobody
actually knows what's happening here. We can marvel at a record window. We can see the biggest
spenders are Manchester City on £457.5m, the lowest Crystal Palace on about £50m, the highest net
Liverpool on £216.8m. We can pore over Aston Villa buying the bones of a new squad for £56.7m net
gain.

[Bradley Barcola checks out Anfield]
But we can't know what it means, at least not financially. This is part of the design of money on
this scale. As the then Federal Reserve chair Alan Greenspan put it in a famous speech on the
mysteries of big money: "How do we know when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset
values, which then become subject to unexpected and prolonged contractions?"

Nobody knew what this meant then. Nobody knows what it means now. "Irrational exuberance" is a good
phrase, one Greenspan later said came to him in the bath. It was designed to convey the essential
point: opacity, vagueness, invisible flows. If not for those actually riding the tide, because
someone is always making money. Someone out there actively wants the Premier League's financial
chaos, and is benefiting from it gloriously.

Otherwise, reading the runes is just a matter of guesswork. Money moves between networked entities.
Everyone inside wins. Everyone outside pays for it. But still, this feels new, if only in its
extremity.

This was captured by the weirdness of Sky Sports' transfer deadline programme on Tuesday. At one
point a man appeared strumming a guitar, smiling as if he had just filleted a litter of kittens in
his basement, and sang a Bradley Barcola Liverpool song to the tune of Don't Dream It's Over, like
this is all just normal and fine.

Later there was a golden period at about 5.30pm where every deal just seemed wrong and impossible, a
fever dream after an over-ripe seafood risotto. Aston Villa have paid £47m for an 18-year-old
winger. Gabriel Jesus is going to Barcelona. Jack Grealish is having a medical at Everton, an event
that seems to prove the non-linear nature of time, a medical that is always happening, never not
happening, a constant time-space event.

[Gabriel Jesus at his Barcelona unveiling]
Meanwhile two private jets are on the tarmac in Lyon as three clubs try to sign Malick Fofana. "We
STILL don't know what Malick Fofana is thinking," the man with the microphone concluded, mournfully,
and this seemed profoundly true and on point. We don't. Does Malick Fofana even know this? If so,
how can he? Nobody else has a clue what's going on.

It is worth trying to list the best deals. Harry Wilson on a free can only be good. Djed Spence to
Inter is great. _Forza_, Djed. Barcola to Liverpool for £123m is lots of money, but he's really
young and knows how to score once he's settled.

Newcastle seem to have had a good window, racking up a list of signings that scream inside knowledge
and good data, and chime with Matthias Jaissle's mercurial vibe. Manchester City's signings have to
be good. There's so many of them, and they're all excellent players. That midfield is surely just
too big to fail.

Arsenal will also be fine, despite not signing a 1950s-style hotshot goal ace with dynamite in his
boots and the brain of a hungry magpie. Yes, they've lost two, maybe three attackers. But these are
Jesus and probably Gabriel Martinelli, meat in the room, plus Leandro Trossard, replaced by
basically the same guy. Arsenal won the league with Kai Havertz, Martin Ødegaard, Bukayo Saka and
Eberechi Eze short of their ceiling. If you can't score enough to win with that lot then it's really
not the players.

Manchester United's window looks quite good, but also scattergun. Carlos Baleba may be great, or may
wander around like a lost child in Lidl before being loaned to Monaco. Tottenham have mined the
club's DNA, gone back to their spiritual roots, and signed players who will be really good for four
games, then disappear and get tattoos.

Meanwhile Everton have swapped Dwight McNeil for Brennan Johnson. What does it mean? Can you even
visualise this as an event? Beto and Iliman Ndiaye have gone, taking away a third of last season's
league goals. Welcome, Everton fans, to the fully immersive Thierno Barry multiverse.

Otherwise the main takeaway is the meaninglessness of big football money. Most notably the size of
the most basic fees. Little wonder there is a sense of alienation from familiar notions of team
building, blocks in place, footballers as recognisable avatars of graft and cultural chemistry.
These are stocks, talent units, a flow of resources.

[Caleb Yirenkyi challenges Arsenal's Myles Lewis-Skelly on his Coventry debut]
Remember the Peter Odemwingie deadline day fiasco, where Odemwingie sat fuming in his car while the
world was sucked into this human drama? That whole thing was about the unbridgeable leap from £2m
to £3m. Coventry have just signed Caleb Yirenkyi from Nordsjælland for £23m. Does "Caleb
Yirenkyi" really exist? Are you sure?

Who pays in the end for this circulation of funds? You, for starters. There is no such thing as
football money. It's all TV rights, state propaganda funds and hard private investment from the kind
of people who really don't give without taking back in the end.

On one level books are being balanced, accounting rules satisfied, clubs swapping assets at fees
driven in part by regulatory requirements. More obscurely Boehly once talked about a rush of cash
being diverted football's way as a sub-avalanche from quantitative easing in the US, because money
just has to go somewhere. No, me neither.

All that seems certain is money is being pushed around the board. And that it feels wildly abstract.
The window was thrilling, its possibilities endless, a shot of blood for the new season. Beyond
this, big football and big finance are playing their own game, in a way that goes beyond the human
scale and into a world of pure money. The message below the headlines is clear. This thing belongs
to them. Your job, human consumer unit, is simply to look at it and have feelings.
