Netflix Adapted a Great American Classic. What Have They Done to It?

====================================================================================================

John Steinbeck’s 1952 novel _East of Eden_ is bursting with character and incident, a decades-long
story spanning from the America of the Civil War to the America of World War I, wrapping in topics
from the impact of anti-Chinese immigration laws to improvements in refrigeration technology to the
mnemonic devices people employed to remember how to start a Model T. It’s a deeply enjoyable book
you should revisit out of your own free will, if your only memory of previous readings was (like
mine) tied up with learning about Biblical allusion in high school English. It’s also hefty,
clocking in at around 600 pages, depending on the edition. The new Netflix adaptation, created by
Zoe Kazan, whose grandfather Elia directed the 1955 movie starring James Dean, has only seven
hourlong episodes, so it must make choices in bringing certain elements to the screen. Elia Kazan
trimmed _East of Eden_ into a two-hour movie by focusing only on the novel’s final quarter; Zoe
Kazan has slimmed down Steinbeckian overabundance by almost completely excising one of the two
families that make up the book’s dramatis personae. Ninety percent of the novel’s material about
the Hamilton family, Steinbeck’s experiment in fictionalized memoir (the Hamiltons were based on
his real-life grandparents), is gone. What Zoe Kazan found compelling instead was everything having
to do with Cathy Ames (played in the new show by Florence Pugh), Steinbeck’s tough-as-nails female
villain who, born missing a conscience but gifted with intelligence and beauty, kills and
manipulates her way through life.

The series hits Netflix on Thursday, but I’m already weary of the brewing debate over this new
adaptation’s biggest zag: Pugh and Kazan want to make you feel sorry for Cathy. On a panel at the
Atlantic Festival earlier this month, Kazan said that she was “freeing up Steinbeck’s better
instincts” by making Cathy into more of a “person,” describing Cathy’s “journey” and
efforts to make it “relatable to an audience and not something to gawk at.” Pugh, for her part,
added, “It’s my job to make people sympathize and maybe fall in love with her a little bit.”
Unsurprisingly, there are people in the comments of the YouTube video of this panel describing this
approach as “woke,” which I guess it is. (Kazan did describe Steinbeck’s intention in writing
_East of Eden_ as containing both “humanism” and “inevitable midcentury white male racism and
misogyny,” phraseology that likely triggered that response.)

As a book reader, a viewer, and a critic, I don’t much care if the new _East of Eden_ is
“woke.” The better question is: Does it work? Long story short, I’m sorry to say, I don’t
think it does.

Pugh’s smooth, catlike face is very Cathy Ames, Christopher Abbott does what he can with the role
of her simp husband Adam Trask, and Mike Faist is fun, cast against his usual good-boy type as
Adam’s meaner, stronger brother Charles. The actors are fine, but Kazan seems to have looked at
Steinbeck’s story and determined it’s terminally out of fashion to consider matters of good and
evil. The novel offered a wide array of interpretations of these questions, in the form of its
characters: Cathy is inherently bad, maybe what we would now call a sociopath, and always acts out
of self-interest. Adam is good, but weak. Charles and Cal (the son born to Cathy as one of a pair of
twins) are cynical, knowing, and forceful—but they also, each to his own degree, feel love, and
act on it. Aron, Cal’s twin, is so good that, by the end of the book, he has become judgmental,
with a “purity” “a little on the self-indulgent side,” as Steinbeck wrote. Only Samuel
Hamilton, the Hamiltons’ patriarch, and the family’s longtime servant Lee (more on him in a bit)
_are_ good and _act_ out of goodness.

The goings-on between these characters loses something significant when you start to pity Cathy, and
even begin to cheer her on as a kind of misunderstood girlboss. Early on, Cathy kills her parents;
in the novel, she does it after her father lectures her, threatening her, speaking of “the
authority God had given him over his child and of how this natural authority had been armed by the
state.” The show’s script modernizes this speech a bit, having Mr. Ames tell his daughter that
she is his “property,” a term that feels designed to evoke the viewer’s outraged gasp.
Steinbeck means this scene to demonstrate the total powerlessness of Cathy’s parents, who she soon
kills in a house fire while faking her own death, while Kazan holds the father in front of us as yet
another in a parade of examples of men who do Cathy wrong. In both book and show, Cathy loves the
story of _Alice in Wonderland_, because of Alice’s ability to drink from a tiny bottle and shrink
until she vanishes from human perception. Does Cathy love this idea because she was—as the show
heavily implies in an invented scene where an older boy holds two pieces of wrapped candy toward
young Cathy as she sits in a field—sexually assaulted when she was 5, or because vanishing from
people and everything they expect is yet another way for a bad person to escape accountability?
Perhaps because we’ve been inundated in the past few decades with stories about villains with
traumatic pasts, the second idea simply feels more original, more unusual, and more interesting to
me these days.

The problem is not just that trying to ground Cathy in a backstory of male rape, assault, and
control, as Kazan and Pugh are doing, feels like some kind of unwelcome modernization of the story.
The problem is that Cathy’s pure evil was the thing the rest of the characters orbited around, and
softening her changes everything for the worse. The role of Adam Trask is a tough one—he’s the
“better” brother in childhood, then an impassioned and completely benighted husband whose first
definite life choice, to move to California despite Cathy’s objections, gets cast as patriarchal
in Kazan’s interpretation. After Cathy, postpartum, shoots Adam and leaves the family, changing
her name to become the brothel madam Kate (you go, girl!), Adam spends a _decade_ sitting on a chair
on a porch, mourning the wife he barely knew, who everyone else can see faked her way through their
marriage. Christopher Abbott does his best, but Adam Trask, “good” though he may be, is acted
upon by life far more than he acts, and that’s a tough thing for a TV character. If Cathy is evil,
Adam is a victim; if Cathy has some good in her, Adam made a totally different kind of mistake.

Charles vanishes from the story when Adam and Cathy move to California and he stays in Connecticut
on the Trask farm. The show would clearly like this trio of handsome actors to have more screentime
together, and so invents a sequence in which Faist’s Charles appears as a ghost to Kate in later
episodes, speaking to her and appearing in her mirror before she dies by suicide, suffering from
arthritis, fearing a past crime will be revealed, and feeling like she’s losing control of men’s
attention as she ages. This occasionally tender memory of Charles, like Pugh’s operatic depiction
of Kate’s show-invented breakdown when she sees her “good” son Aron (Joe Anders) appear at her
brothel, gives Kate entirely too much credit—and makes Charles, who in the novel tolerates Cathy
for only as long as she’s in his immediate vicinity, even more complicit in Kate’s evil.

Although it’s sexier for publicity purposes to represent this tale as a love triangle between the
two brothers and Cathy, the actual third anchor of the story—and the person at the heart of the
parts of the show I most enjoyed watching—is Lee, the Chinese American housekeeper, tutor, and
surrogate parent, played with a grave kindness by the actor Hoon Lee. Lee, despite not even making
it into some _East of Eden_ adaptations, like the 1955 movie, is one of the most interesting
characters in the novel, a highly educated man who, because he happens to be employed by the family
when Cathy abandons her newborns and Adam recedes into grief, ends up a de facto parent to Cal and
Aron, living with the family for decades. The moments in the show when Lee chides Cal (Joseph Zada)
or Aron for putting their feet up on the wall or flicking light switches (“Electricity costs
money!”) are warm and sweet, but Kazan’s adaptation can’t help but make the subtext text,
having a fellow Chinese American tell Lee, when he describes his situation and wonders if he will
ever have a home and a wife of his own, that it seems like he’s already “somebody else’s
wife.” At one point, in the novel, Lee leaves the family for a few days, intending to start a
bookstore in San Francisco; although the show invents a reason involving racist exclusion for him to
surrender this dream, in the novel he just gives up because he’s lonely. Lee’s kindness sets him
up, along with a big dose of “wrong place, wrong time,” to live a life he never meant to live.
That’s a soft trap, and that’s fascinating—it’s a subtle thing, unlike anything else that
happens in the Netflix series, to its own detriment.

How would I adapt _East of Eden_, if this were my assignment? Make Lee into the point-of-view
character? Turn it into science fiction, have Cathy reveal she’s really an evil alien in a human
suit? Go for broke and propose a maximalist approach: 27 hours of television, covering everything
from the heartbreaking death of my favorite Hamilton, Dessie, to the secret corruption of Abra
Bacon’s father? Perhaps the truly messy spirit of the novel can’t be corralled on screen. Maybe
that’s why this just-so story about Cathy, which seems so proud of itself, bothers me more than it
should.
