True Crime Conspiracists Have Homed In On Patrick Clancy. I Can’t Believe What I’m About to Say.

====================================================================================================

There seem to be only two kinds of people in the world lately: those who can’t bring themselves to
look at the Lindsay Clancy case, and those who refuse to miss even one gruesome detail.
Participation in either camp makes sense, since the case is both too hideous to really enjoy as true
crime grist and so thick with detail and credulity on all sides that it’s impossible not to want
to read just a little bit more.

It’s a rarity for a trial like this, but there are some basic facts that the prosecution and
defense have agreed upon. In January 2023, Clancy strangled her three children, a 5-year-old, a
3-year-old, and an 8-month-old, using exercise bands in the basement of their home in Duxbury,
Massachusetts, an affluent suburb of Boston. She then tried to kill herself by cutting her wrists
and throat, then jumping from the second floor of the house. She killed her three children and
paralyzed herself from the waist down, leaving behind a scene for her husband, Patrick Clancy, to
find when he came home that night.

Typically, this is the kind of case that would fit neatly into the Bad Mommy canon of true crime,
from Diane Downs, in the 1980s, who shot her kids and claimed that it had been a carjacking; to
Andrea Yates, in 2001, who drowned her kids because she was hallucinating; to Taylor Parker,
featured in the recent Netflix documentary _Maternal Instinct_, who in 2020 faked a pregnancy, then
killed her pregnant friend and her in utero baby. But in this case, the public’s response to the
Clancy trial has been markedly more nuanced.

Clancy has pleaded not guilty by reason of insanity. Her defense team is claiming that she killed
the kids and tried to kill herself because she was experiencing severe postpartum psychosis after a
long bout with postpartum depression and anxiety. Her auditory hallucinations, which she says took
the form of a male voice telling her to commit these crimes, are the alleged culprit. The trial,
then, is about not just whether Clancy is sent to prison without parole or to a mental health
facility for the remainder of her tragic life. It’s about the validity of a postpartum woman’s
experience, the kind of help available to her, and the members of her community who were required to
do more.

The Clancy case is true-crime catnip—rest assured you’ll see a spate of documentaries, podcasts,
books, and torn-from-the-headlines _Law & Order_ episodes about her and her family by the time fall
comes around. Reddit and the comment threads of local Boston papers are rife with calls for Clancy
to be put in a maximum-security prison for life. But this is a case as much about motherhood as
about mental health and criminality, and so some of the most layered responses are coming from women
online who feel as if they’ve heard a lot of Clancy’s story before.

On TikTok, you’ll now find countless amateur sleuths, along with some delusional conspiracy
theorists, offering not only their versions of how the murders might have happened, but sometimes
arguments for why Clancy might not have committed the crimes at all. Coverage is coming from the
usual fixtures online, podcasts and video series that tend to chronicle the most bombastic true
crimes available in the public record. But increasingly, women from all industries and life
experiences are going mini-viral for posting their takes on the case and, saliently, why they think
the husband is actually at fault. Moms who have rarely posted before are becoming forensics
influencers, watching around-the-clock trial footage and news reports. Outside the courtroom on
Day 17 were hundreds of women wearing pink, joined together to “stand in peace for Lindsay.”
Many think Patrick Clancy is hiding something around his involvement in his kids’ deaths, while
others believe that Lindsay Clancy committed the crimes with her own hands.

What they have in common, however, is a united fury against a healthcare system that barely listens
to women at all, never mind new mothers, and Western, heteronormative family structures that leave
women to handle the bulk of the child-rearing. This side of true-crime TikTok consists of unbridled
rage. “This lady did not do this shit on her motherfucking own. I’m willing to die on that
hill,” says TikTokker @alchemistsosoho in a video from early August that, as of this article’s
publication, has nearly 130,000 likes. “Any mom that has gone through postpartum—Lord Jesus,
I’m getting emotional—all type of thoughts done ran through your mind, if you’re real about
that shit. On top of that, when you’re dealing with a man who really don’t even fucking want
you, ready to fucking replace you, doing whatever the fuck he can to play on your mindset—shit
happens.”

The details of the tragedy sound all too familiar for women who have experienced postpartum
depression or anxiety, have had children with an indifferent (at best) man, and have a village that
doesn’t really know how to help. The details that the women of TikTok are focusing on in
particular also speak to that apparent negligence. Testimony from her mother showed how much Clancy
was struggling with her mental health in the months running up to the murders. She told her mother,
her mother-in-law, her husband, and her sister about it, as they’ve all testified. In one exchange
submitted into evidence—a conversation that the defense blew up into a poster-board-size
screenshot, and that has been picked apart by Clancy-sympathetic sleuths for days now—she texted
her mother in October 2022 asking her to drive over from Connecticut. “I’m really sick.
Something is wrong. I have horrible insomnia all night and I just don’t know how I’m going to
get through the day,” Clancy wrote. “I started taking the medicine my doctor prescribed for
anxiety and I think it’s made things worse. It’s just really scary and I don’t want to be
alone.”

Her mother said that a month or so before the killings, Clancy told her and Patrick that she was
having thoughts about hurting the kids. When Patrick asked if she felt as if she could be alone with
them, she allegedly said no. That November, he took a two-day trip to New York, leaving her alone
with the kids. The day after he returned, he took a four-day trip with just the eldest of their
children, again leaving Clancy home with their boys.

Clancy was a labor and delivery nurse, as was her mother-in-law, and so there were plenty of people
around her who understood the basics of postpartum anxiety and could have assisted in getting her
help. She was seeing a psychiatrist but seemed to keep getting misdiagnosed. Meanwhile, she was
prescribed 13 psychiatric medications in the months leading up to the murders. Some of the drugs on
her list, medications prescribed by Jennifer Tufts—who had finished her residency in psychiatry in
2022, months before the killings—appear to be contraindicated, if she was indeed taking them at
the same time. (Notably, despite taking all these meds, she still wasn’t sleeping. Extreme sleep
deprivation can cause psychosis.) Clancy had never even met Tufts until the trial; all their
sessions occurred over Zoom, even while she was being prescribed benzodiazepines and antipsychotic
drugs. Her husband also didn’t attend any appointments with Clancy to glean more information about
what was going on with his wife. (Clancy and her ex-husband are suing Tufts, among others who
handled Clancy’s postpartum care, in civil court for medical malpractice.)

Tufts has plenty of defenders on Reddit, including in r/DuxburyDeathsFreeTalk, on which people seem
by and large anti-Clancy. “You Are NOT Lindsay Clancy,” reads the subject line of the
subreddit’s top post. “This woman is not our poster child. She is not someone who should be held
up as a martyr for those of us who have gone through this. I cannot say this enough: WE DID NOT KILL
OUR CHILD OR CHILDREN.” But from tradwives to IVF influencers, social media remains a hub for
women and mothers to elaborate on what it feels like to be ignored by doctors, prescribed medication
instead of paid attention to, and dismissed by a husband on a business trip.

And so, even if Patrick Clancy didn’t commit these crimes, is he not still partially responsible
for the conditions that allowed the killings to happen? The theories about him range from the
patently absurd (Patrick’s current wife did it) to the possible (he ignored her when Lindsay
Clancy said she was sick). But it’s not even about Patrick anymore; it’s about what he has come
to represent for women watching this trial.

Since the murders, Patrick has moved to New York and remarried, something that’s driving online
comparisons to family annihilators like Chris Watts and Scott Peterson. (There are rumblings, still
unconfirmed, that he vacationed with his now wife just under a month after his children’s deaths.)
Other details, like how low Clancy’s basal temperature had fallen by the time paramedics got to
her, or that she somehow told Patrick what she had done even after jumping out of a window, cutting
her own throat, and crushing her thyroid, are impossible to ignore for those who think her husband
was more involved than he’s letting on. There are completely unsubstantiated theories that he has
family members in the police department that investigated him, and that they’ve been covering for
him for years. More unhinged theories include that he was actually the voice telling her to kill the
kids, and that he strangled them and framed her, pushing her out the window, assuming she would die
upon impact.

Popular true-crime cases always receive a certain level of derangement on both sides, and although
some of these theories feel far-fetched, they’re voicing a universal truth for women across the
country. Clancy’s alleged crime is undoubtedly cruel and terrifying, but the motivation is perhaps
solved through better healthcare access, more support for women with children, and, perhaps toughest
of all, believing women when they say something is wrong with their health. Women on TikTok are
flocking to her side—and have sent her family nearly $1 million dollars on GoFundMe—because her
story sounds similar to their own. The women obsessing over this case now are the same women fed up
with having to explain their own moods and needs during and after their pregnancies.

A case like Clancy’s would always have garnered headlines, but it speaks to how rarely women are
heard in medicine, and in their marriages. Patrick has become a placeholder for every lousy father
who treats parenting more like babysitting, and who expects the wife to hold it together through
insomnia, exhaustion, and parental overwhelm. Clancy was struggling, and despite having a medical
education and a husband, she didn’t know what to do. He, too, seems not to have realized how bad
her mental state could get, even as she was literally crying out for help. You can easily make the
argument that Patrick was a disengaged husband and a negligent caregiver to the woman who had three
of his children. That isn’t criminal, but it is creating a very particular wave of true-crime
obsession. It’s not about only what Clancy might have done or why she may have done it. It’s
about how Patrick may have (inadvertently or otherwise) facilitated an environment for his wife to
commit one of the ugliest crimes in recent history.

Clancy is receiving this level of focus and support in large part because she’s a good-looking,
white, middle-class mother who had all the tools available to seek mental healthcare. Were she poor,
Black or Brown, or less telegenic, she might not have such an audience rally around her. That, too,
lends to the constant attention on the case: If this woman, with her many privileges, couldn’t get
adequate medical care to save her and her kids, and if the state would rather incarcerate her than
get her the care she needs, what hope is there for the rest of us? The crime in this story concerns
the lack of postpartum education—even among the educated—and the fact that no one really knows
what to do about an unwell mom. Clancy is just one piece of it, a constant media reminder of what
could happen to you as a new, exhausted, overwhelmed mother, even if you try to do everything right.

During Day 16 of the trial, Brooklyn-based therapist and TikTokker Emily Thorndike addressed the
courtroom without the jury present, offering her experience at one of the hospitals where Clancy was
treated. The defense wanted the jury to hear her testimony—Thorndike had, in late July, posted on
TikTok about McLean Hospital, arguing that Clancy had failed to get the care she needed while she
was admitted.

The judge called her credible but wouldn’t allow her testimony. She had some interesting thoughts,
but she wasn’t a part of Clancy’s treatment. “We need to correct the misinformation that this
prosecutor is saying,” Thorndike said in her TikTok. But her words would never make it to the
jury. After all, she’s just a woman on the internet, convinced of a sliver of Clancy’s
innocence, much like millions of other women following along online, rapt, invested, and furious.
