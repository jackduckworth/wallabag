Democrats have discovered the key to midterm victory

====================================================================================================

Former Speaker of the House Nancy Pelosi always said that the only way for Democrats to win
elections was to focus on the proverbial “kitchen table issues.” As the leader of the caucus for
20 years, she had quite a track record, presiding over quite a few big wins and many small ones, so
she knew what she was talking about. According to her way of thinking, people are motivated to vote
over concerns about their own material well-being, and in recent years, this has meant healthcare,
job security and the cost of living — the basics.

Republicans, by contrast, tend to rely on a broader appeal to security in general. “Law and
order” has been their bumper sticker, covering a wide range of free-floating anxieties in American
society having to do with cultural changes, race and the loss of status of straight white males.
Their answer to economic stress comes down to blaming the bogeyman of the day, whether it’s
immigrants or the deficit, and the answer is always tax cuts and more tax cuts. 

Democrats have usually been trusted more on the kitchen-table issues, depending on whether they
happen to be in office when some kind of economic crisis hits. Jimmy Carter paid a high price,
losing his 1980 re-election bid after having inherited crippling stagflation from his GOP
predecessors, and was hit with a big recession as a result of the Federal Reserve policies
— which he supported — to break it. His successor, Ronald Reagan, reaped the benefit, a common
consequence of painful Democratic policies necessarily deployed to clean up a Republican
administration’s economic mess.

The GOP enjoyed 12 years in the White House after Carter’s loss, including a big boom which
inevitably turned into a recession and helped ensure Bill Clinton’s victory over George H.W. Bush
in the 1992 election. Clinton presided over an economic boom that saved his presidency, which was
otherwise dogged by scandals largely perpetrated by the GOP’s final evolution from the party of
mainline Protestants and main street business owners to one of ruthless street-fighters.

Economics, of course, aren’t the only issue that motivates voters. While more abstract notions of
foreign policy and national security are rarely at the top of the agenda, war certainly can be.
George W. Bush learned this in 2006, when the American people finally realized the Iraq war was a
disaster. But something else was looming on the horizon. Bush had also made the fatal mistake of
trying to privatize Social Security, breaking whatever trust people had in Republicans to safeguard
their hard-won benefits. The Democrats won the midterm election in a rout.

Two years later, Barack Obama was hit with a double whammy. The war in Iraq was still the top issue,
but at the height of his campaign against Arizona Sen. John McCain, the Republican nominee, the
bottom fell out of the housing market and the world suffered the worst financial crisis since the
Great Depression. At the same time, the healthcare crisis had reached a crescendo, with millions of
people going bankrupt, and many suffering and dying due to the high price of insurance. After his
win, those kitchen-table issues were front and center in shaping Obama’s presidency. In fact, two
years into his first term, as the Great Recession continued to drag on, the American people let out
the equivalent of a political primal scream and gave Democrats what Obama famously admitted was a
“shellacking” in the 2010 midterms. Perversely, this was largely caused by the passage of the
Affordable Care Act, the party’s healthcare plan, underscoring that no good deed goes unpunished.

Then came Donald Trump. When he announced his candidacy for president in 2015, the economy was on
the upswing, and just enough people were gulled by his phony reputation as a great businessman and
promises to make everyone rich that he eked out an Electoral College win. Once in office, Trump
basically just rode the recovery that was already underway and took credit for it. When the Covid-19
pandemic hit and his incompetence fully manifested itself, voters turned against him. If there was
ever a kitchen-table issue, the pandemic was it: We were all stuck sitting around the table for
months, wondering if the world was ever going to be the same. Trump failed to rise to the occasion.

But he was elected again after Joe Biden presided over the pandemic’s economic recovery, which was
painful, ushering in inflation for the first time in decades to a chunk of a population that had
never experienced it. The issue did him — and his vice president, Kamala Harris, who replaced him
on the ticket — in, returning Trump to the White House on the promise that he would restore the
economy to the alleged golden age of his first term. Unfortunately for America and the world, enough
people bought into Trump’s chicanery once again, and here we are today in a crisis of chaos,
mistrust, incompetence and despair over the radical policies wrought by a second Trump
administration. 

Kitchen-table issues are still at the top of most voters’ lists. The cost of living remains high
and keeps climbing. Housing is out of control, and gas prices are through the roof due to Trump’s
war of choice with Iran. His much-ballyhooed tariffs have helped keep inflation high, and there is
now little doubt what is driving the polls showing that Republicans are about to take a drubbing in
the November midterms. 

But the concept of kitchen-table issues has its limits. Not everything in life is actually about
money, and people’s perceptions of the economy are often colored by other issues that don’t
appear at first glance to be directly related.

money, and people’s perceptions of the economy are often colored by other issues that don’t
appear at first glance to be directly related. Bush’s losses in 2006, for instance, were affected
by his mishandling of Hurricane Katrina and the Iraq War, both of which contributed to a sense that
the administration suffered from hubris and incompetence. There is little doubt that the backlash
against Obama during his first term was also driven by the recoil on the right to his identity as
the first Black president. Trump likewise benefited from the fact that misogyny and racism were in
play in his victories over the first two women to win the nominations of a major party.

The problem is that Democrats have too often relied solely on the kitchen-table issue agenda and
failed to address underlying anxieties that contribute to their distress. And with Donald Trump,
they are legion. 

The unrelenting pandemonium of his presidency, from his brutal immigration crackdown to the flagrant
corruption and degeneration of the president himself, as manifested in his obsession with gold and
the building of personal monuments in a time of economic stress, is making the public depressed and
angry. The fact that Trump broke one of his fundamental promises to not get the country into a war,
as he whines constantly about being denied the Nobel Peace Prize, is maddening. His ongoing
disregard of the rule of law and the assault on the democratic process are obvious. The disconnect
between what average Americans are feeling and the way their leaders are behaving is a mile wide.

People have been yearning for a holistic explanation for this chaos and confusion — and Democrats
have finally found a way to express it. As POLITICO recently noted, Sen. Jon Ossoff of Georgia,
along with Senate candidates Dr. Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and James Talarico of Texas, as
different as they are individually, are “leaning hard into anti-corruption messaging in the final
weeks of the campaign as new research suggests that linking corruption to Americans’ economic
struggles can boost Democrats into the home stretch.”

The corruption argument is compelling. Beyond the hated war in Iran (and its resulting gas prices),
Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner and old friend Steve Witkoff’s deals in the Middle East have
come under scrutiny and outrage, as have the government contracts being handed out to companies in
which Trump’s sons are invested. Pardons are being sold to anyone with enough cash, some of them
to people convicted of healthcare fraud, even as the Republicans are cutting Medicaid and healthcare
subsidies. Contrary to his blarney, Trump’s Louis XIV-style monuments to himself are being largely
paid for by the taxpayers. On top of all that, there are the reports that Trump has made $2 billion
since returning to the White House, a figure that is excessive, even for him. 

The shameless grifting is too obvious for voters to overlook, and the Democrats have finally
realized that the critique of Trump and MAGA Republicans must go beyond the kitchen table because it
is all connected. The chaos, incompetence, deceit and self-dealing are at the heart of America’s
distress, and it’s benefiting Trump and his cronies at the expense of average taxpayers. 

Distilling all this into a media-ready indictment, a soundbite, is difficult, but “Washington is
rigged to benefit the wealthy at the expense of everyday Americans” is about as good as it gets.
Let’s hope Democrats can carry this message of reform past Election Day and actually do something
about it.
