This Is Samuel Alito’s Ugliest Dissent in Some Time

====================================================================================================

You don’t need to kill anyone to be put to death in Texas. The state doesn’t need your
fingerprints on the weapon, your DNA on the body, or your shadow in the doorway. It just needs you
nearby and legally adjacent to the violence. And if the blood under the victim’s fingernails turns
out to be someone else’s? If the DNA proves you weren’t the one who did it? Texas shrugs. It
wants the execution to proceed.

That’s the legal terrain Ruben Gutierrez has spent more than two decades navigating and that
finally resulted in the Supreme Court’s intervention on Thursday. Convicted in 1999 for the murder
of Escolastica Harrison, an 85-year-old woman killed during a robbery in her home, Gutierrez has
always admitted to helping plan the crime. He knew that Harrison kept large sums of money hidden in
her mobile home, and he and two others intended to steal it. That admission is why not even
Gutierrez claims full innocence. But partial guilt is not the same as death eligibility. Gutierrez
insists he wasn’t in the room when Harrison was killed. He didn’t wield the screwdriver used to
stab her. He didn’t intend to harm her. He didn’t anticipate that she would die. That
distinction, under Texas law, matters; not just morally but legally.

Texas law allows a person to be convicted of capital murder if they were a party to the underlying
felony and someone was killed in the course of it. But to sentence someone to death, the jury must
go further. It must find that the defendant caused the death, intended it, or at least anticipated
it. That’s the line Gutierrez has spent years trying to draw—between being part of a robbery
gone wrong and being the reason a woman is dead. And that’s why he has sought DNA testing of
physical evidence recovered from the scene—fingernail scrapings, bloodstains, and hair—that
could show that someone else committed the killing. If the evidence reveals that he was not
physically present at the murder, it could unravel the jury’s finding that he met the legal
threshold for death.

Texas refused. Its postconviction DNA-testing statute permits access only if the evidence would
likely have changed the outcome of the trial. And since Gutierrez was convicted under Texas’ law
of parties—a legal mechanism that holds responsible for murder participants in a crime that
resulted in that murder—the state insisted that proving that someone else committed the murder
wouldn’t necessarily matter. He could still be held accountable under a theory of shared
responsibility. In Texas’ eyes, DNA demonstrating that Gutierrez wasn’t the killer might be
scientifically conclusive but legally meaningless.

When Gutierrez sued under Section 1983, claiming that Texas’ DNA-testing procedures violated his
right to procedural due process, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit dismissed the case.
It held that Gutierrez lacked standing: that even if the DNA proved that someone else had done the
killing, it wouldn’t redress the injury, because the law could still permit his execution. The
result was a ruling that elevated legal form over factual inquiry, treating the death penalty as a
sealed outcome rather than a sentence contingent on truth.

On Thursday, the Supreme Court reopened that door. In a 6–3 decision, it held that Gutierrez
_does_ have standing to pursue his procedural due process claim in federal court. Chief Justice John
Roberts, writing for the majority, rejected the state’s argument that the potential impact of the
DNA evidence was too speculative. The issue wasn’t whether Gutierrez would ultimately prevail; it
was whether the DNA evidence he seeks to test could, if favorable, plausibly support a renewed
challenge to his death sentence. And it could. If that evidence shows that someone else inflicted
the wounds—if it casts doubt on whether Gutierrez caused, intended, or anticipated Harrison’s
death—it vitiates the findings that made him eligible for the death penalty. And that, Roberts
wrote, is enough to establish standing.

It is a subdued assertion of what should be self-evident: that the law cannot claim legitimacy if it
shields itself from the facts it purports to judge. That finality is not a license for ignorance.
That procedural rules are not meant to entomb the truth, especially when the state seeks to end a
life.

We live in an era when the capacity to be shocked by what members of this court are willing to
defend has all but collapsed. And yet, even in that desensitized landscape, Justice Samuel Alito’s
dissent in _Gutierrez_ manages to stun. He, joined by Justices Clarence Thomas and Neil Gorsuch,
argued that Gutierrez’s claim should have been tossed from the start.

In their view, the state can strap a man to a gurney and stop his heart even if the law forbids it
so long as the process doesn’t flinch and the record stays closed. It is a vision of justice so
warped that the legality of death depends not on what happened but on whether anyone is still
allowed to care.

Because Texas allows accomplice liability in capital cases, Alito reasoned, it didn’t matter
whether Gutierrez was the actual killer, so DNA testing that might identify someone else wouldn’t
change the outcome. The majority, he warned, was inviting an endless wave of litigation over
evidence that could never truly exonerate a person under Texas law.

But this is more than procedural stinginess. It is a philosophy of deliberate obliviousness.
Alito’s dissent reduces the death penalty to a matter of bureaucratic form in which the sentence
rests not on what happened but on how skillfully the prosecution framed the murder. Rather than
treating DNA as a tool for truth, it sees it as a threat to tidy verdicts. In this view, the only
error worth acknowledging is one that fits neatly within existing doctrine; everything else, even
proof that the state does not have the authority to extinguish a life, is noise.

And that’s what makes it so chilling. The dissent is hostile not just to Gutierrez. It’s hostile
to the very idea that new facts should have power. It reflects a jurisprudence in which legal
theories matter more than human lives, in which a man can be executed as long as the state dots the
right i’s, even if he didn’t do the thing it’s killing him for. The cruelty, as always, is not
incidental. It’s a feature of a system that, way too often, protects its own inertia above all
else.

And it exposes something deeper about how the law handles its most final punishment: its willingness
to seal off inquiry precisely when the stakes are highest. _Gutierrez_ is not merely a dispute about
legal thresholds. It’s a window into how death-penalty doctrine has learned to forget. How it has
perfected the art of procedural insulation. How it builds walls around verdicts—not because they
are trustworthy, but because they are old.

DNA, in this system, is threatening because it might demand that the machinery slow down. It might
say: _This man should not die for what he did not do._

In this case, the court did not order testing. It did not resolve the constitutional claim. But it
cleared the fog of legal fatalism and said, at least, that the possibility of truth still has
standing. That if science might call the state’s story into question, the law must not turn away.

Gutierrez’s case now returns to the lower courts. The evidence remains untested. The verdict
remains intact. But something essential has been reasserted in spite of the intransigence of the 5th
Circuit and three extreme members of the court: that facts still matter. That a death sentence is
not a license for indifference. That there remains, even now, a narrow place for light.
