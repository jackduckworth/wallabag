The Real Lesson to Take From the Viral Lesbian-Bar-Meltdown Story

====================================================================================================

At first, the rubbernecking at the Western Massachusetts lesbian bar seemed all in good fun.

Everyone wanted to read the viral Boston Globe feature about the noxious backlash that had engulfed
Last Ditch. On Aug. 31, 2026, the establishment, which typically has a policy that patrons must wear
masks, announced that Saturday nights would be “masks _optional_.” “The new policy was not
received with love,” Annalisa Quinn wrote, in an article that quickly rose to become the paper’s
top story of the year.

It was a carefully reported piece of local journalism, but there’s no doubt that its popularity
was powered by the slew of easily mockable characters and tropes within: a person using _it/its_
pronouns; a bar co-founder who left to attend clown school; leftists who accuse people of racism and
ableism willy-nilly; a bartender who won’t play Lady Gaga because the singer is a “Zionist.”

Multiple queer friends of mine sent the link around for a laugh and a bit of good-natured
commiseration at how self-defeating our LGBTQ+ comrades can be in pursuit of an unreachable ideal of
inclusivity. By demanding fully-masked perfection—and that the bar provide free drinks for anyone
who claims they can’t pay—queers clamoring for a safe space may force one of the few in
existence to close. Amused and dismayed by the depiction of gays fatally overdosing on their own
self-righteousness, the general consensus in my circles was something along the lines of “this is
why we can’t have nice things.”

But soon, inevitably, the story broke containment. No longer was it mostly fellow queers and
left-leaning Globe readers cringing at our own values contorted to the point of absurdity. On X,
Sen. Ted Cruz reposted screenshots of the piece, writing, “Leftism is a mental disease.”
Right-wing provocateur Andy Ngo and conservative commentator Michael Knowles chimed in. Robby
Starbuck dug up old photos of the bar’s founders to publicly insult their looks: “Leftism
destroys someone inside and out.”

Instead of viewing the Last Ditch feature as a glimpse into a fringe community struggling to
reconcile its own norms, a rising internet consensus seems to see it as a cautionary tale that
should discredit the left _as a whole_. I’m writing this mere days after the story published, and
already it has become a weapon for anti-gay and anti-trans ideologues invested in painting all
LGBTQ+ people as identity-obsessed, antisocial freaks.

It’s a shame, because there are a few worthy takeaways to be gleaned from the case of Last Ditch.
One is that people are often harshest to those who care about them. The Heritage Foundation won’t
give us the time of day—so we rag on the Human Rights Campaign. The random dive bar down the
street hasn’t been requiring masks since 2020—so we expect mandatory masking every single night
from the queer bar. The most powerful man in America is boldly, unabashedly racist, so we focus our
anti-racist protests on people who are trying the hardest _not_ to be racist.

Some of these critiques are valid and important—you can care, and still mess up!—while others
are raised as a way to claim some small sliver of power over anyone willing to cede it. The
shrieking demand for absolute perfection becomes a weapon. For Last Ditch, this meant hundreds of
online messages accusing the bar operator of killing people (via allowing infection to spread), and
a multi-hour livestreamed meeting at which they were called racist for sharing how hard it’s been
to keep the place afloat. Attendees also said the owner had jeopardized their safety by inviting a
licensed therapist to moderate the conversation.

This impulse isn’t unique to activists, or to queers: Kids are better behaved for babysitters than
they are for their parents, in part because they feel safe in the knowledge that their parents will
forgive and love them no matter what. But the Last Ditch story speaks to the thick skin required to
run any event or institution in a community with radical expectations of accommodation. It also
points to the value of having humbler ambitions—ones suited to a bar, not a utopian
experiment—instead of making big promises to people with out-there demands and then absorbing
their rage when you can’t fulfill them. When that fails, instead of capitulating to the loudest
and wackiest among us, it is better to just say no.

But there’s a deeper context to the Last Ditch controversy that’s getting lost in the
mean-spirited commentary. The bar’s struggle to survive is part of a broader pattern in the
Western Massachusetts queer scene. In 2024, the Majestic Saloon in Northampton, the only LGBTQ+ bar
in “Lesbianville, USA,” closed. Within days, queer-centered Greenfield venue 10 Forward
shuttered. Last Ditch opened in the property vacated by 10 Forward, with a name that literally
described its purpose: It was intended as a last-ditch effort to create a viable space that cared
for people in all the ways that previous venues had failed to.

Unfavorable economic headwinds surely contributed to the closure of Last Ditch’s predecessors.
Running a small-town gay bar in the post-pandemic, high-inflation, sober-curious 2020s is no easy
feat. But according to community members, both spaces also shut down after weathering a wave of
criticism that resembles what Last Ditch is facing now: In an Instagram post, a local resident
launched accusations of ableism and racism and called for a boycott. The Majestic Saloon even held a
community mediation session akin to the one Last Ditch hosted.

Rene Pellissier, who co-hosts a monthly comedy and poetry open-mic showcase at Last Ditch, admires
the ethos of the venue, which she describes as an idealistic project “run by several well-meaning
queer people in their early- to mid-20s.” The Last Ditch co-founders stepped up to fill the void
left by the bars that closed. In turn, the one remaining operator of Last Ditch has been vilified by
a vocal subgroup in town that is unwilling to accept the compromises that maintaining a sustainable
community gathering space entails.

“This isn’t about the masks—this is about perfectionism. We are expecting too much from our
local community,” Pellissier told me. “If we turn our backs any time we disagree, we will lose
everything.”

At a time when LGBTQ+ people, disabled people, and people of color are suffering systemic neglect
and targeted attacks from the state, some are demanding that the few institutions catering to their
needs agree to fulfill _all_ their needs. Others, wanting to be good allies and feeling helpless in
the face of the hostile political landscape, believe their only choice is to “amplify,” in the
preferred jargon, the voices of the most aggrieved. But every bar-slash-arts-venue must operate
within the strictures of the society in which it opens. No bar-slash-arts-venue is capable of
mending the structural shortcomings of the wider world.

This is a difficult reality to face, and it’s a conversation more queer communities could stand to
have—even in places with less extreme ideals than Greenfield. But it’s impossible to have it
with the compassion and clearheadedness it requires in an online cesspool of bad-faith commentary.
It’s actually kind of ironic that in a discourse about the management practices of a Massachusetts
lesbian bar being denounced by a handful of entitled people, a senator from Texas thinks _he_ is
entitled to weigh in and scold.

Even before the Globe published its piece, the discourse around Last Ditch had already fallen into
this internet pit. Some of the most impassioned online pushback to the relaxed mask mandate didn’t
actually come from bar patrons or community members, but from far-off keyboard warriors. The person
whose Instagram comment read “I hope your venue gets firebombed for robbing my fellow
immunocompromised people of their Saturday plans,” as quoted by the Globe, clarified that “I
don’t even live in your town.” To the extent that they are a real, sincere person, they are also
a deeply unwell conspiracy theorist, not an emblematic member of the Greenfield scene. This story
has never been just about a queer bar setting itself aflame to placate a militant few. It’s about
how digital distance distorts everyone’s perspective, endangering the physical spaces where
lasting connections—the things that make a collection of people a true, resilient community—are
built. As a result, the people who are desperately clamoring for a safer space to gather risk
creating conditions that make it impossible to sustain one.

Look past the laughable excesses, and there is clearly much to cherish about queer life in
Greenfield. A spin through Last Ditch’s Instagram page reveals a vibrant schedule of themed
parties, drag shows, DJ nights, movie screenings, karaoke, music showcases, line dancing, and
co-working opportunities. In Washington, D.C., the 700,000-person city where I live, we can barely
keep two lesbian-centric spaces open, and one is in the basement of a gay bar. For now, at least,
this tiny town of 17,700 has one of its own, run by a 26-year-old who is struggling to keep the
business afloat while balancing the competing interests of its patrons.

No matter how misguided or incomprehensible some of their decisions look from outside this insular
community, they have clearly been made with the admirable intention of making people who have been
snubbed by much of the outside world feel welcomed, safe, and rich in opportunities for creativity
and delight. They deserve better than what they’re getting: an internet-enabled pile-on that has
made them a villain to unreasonable purists on the left and culture-war fodder to bigots on the
right.
