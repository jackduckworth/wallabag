The Most Highly Anticipated Horror Movie of the Year Is Here. How Scary Is It?

====================================================================================================

_For die-hards, no horror movie can be too scary. But for you, a wimp, the wrong one can leave you
miserable. Never fear, scaredies, because Slate’s_ _Scaredy Scale_ _is here to help. We’ve put
together a highly scientific and_ MOSTLY SPOILER-FREE _system for rating new horror movies,
comparing them with classics along a 10-point scale. And because not everyone is scared by the same
things—some viewers can’t stand jumpscares, while others are haunted by more psychological
terrors or can’t stomach arterial spurts—it breaks down each movie’s scares across three
criteria: suspense, spookiness, and gore._

_Backrooms_, A24’s trending horror film built on a classic piece of internet phenomena, has
officially become the buzzy studio’s highest-grossing movie, domestically, of all time. By
Wednesday, within a mere six days of release, the film, which was one of the more highly anticipated
offerings of the year, had netted both critical acclaim and $100 million in the box office,
officially surpassing the Timothée Chalamet–led _Marty Supreme_, which earned $96 million in the
U.S. Shockingly, _Backrooms_ came from the mind of a teenager: one Kane Parsons, now 20 years old,
whose viral YouTube shorts he made about the creepy internet meme inspired A24 to tap the
then-17-year-old to make a feature-length film.

_Backrooms_ takes place in the 1990s and incorporates the found-footage genre into the story of
Clark (Chiwetel Ejiofor), an alcoholic furniture store owner who is struggling with his recent
separation from his wife. When he comes across an entrance to an unknown realm of endless office
rooms—home to mysterious dangers lurking within—he chooses to explore these backrooms,
unknowingly roping his therapist, Dr. Mary Kline (Renate Reinsve), into his precarious adventure.
Both _Backrooms_ and the other much-discussed film right now, _Obsession_, are evidence of the
success Hollywood has found in relying on YouTube–grown talent to craft today’s most popular
horror films. In fact, _Backrooms_ has punched so far above its weight that Parsons is already
looking to make a sequel.

But none of that answers the biggest question surrounding the picture: Just how scary is it? If
Parsons’ early YouTube shorts are anything to go by, the answer would be _very_. But does the
full-length _Backrooms_ live up to the anxiety-inducing hype?

It’s inevitable that _Backrooms_ approaches sound design much like _A Quiet Place_, considering
that most of the liminal space the characters are maneuvering through is eerily silent. Of course,
this means that the hidden realm is a prime setting for a filmmaker to really maximize the suspense,
as the characters creep around corners hoping nothing ominous is waiting for them on the other side.
The setup begets plentiful jumpscares, but _Backrooms_ keeps the audience on its toes by not always
delivering jolts when you might expect them. However, rest assured, a good number definitely _do_
come. The run time is full of odd appearances that get a rise out of the audience, like birds that
squawk out of nowhere and themed rooms in the labyrinth that just get creepier and
creepier—there’s a particularly well-done scene involving a Christmas-themed room—until a
release of tension resets you to ease, before the unease starts trickling back in again.

As a film, _Backrooms_ is not intent on scaring the living daylights out of its viewer or even
grossing watchers out to the point of queasiness. Instead, it is more focused on raising goosebumps
and making arm hairs stand at attention. Therefore, Parsons’ world is not a particularly gory one.
There are the occasional moments when the realm’s monster attacks people, but the damage is done
off-screen and leaves nothing but smears of blood on the set. Additionally, there are a number of
humans who look disfigured in an unnatural, grotesque way, but it’s not seriously upsetting as
much as it is disquieting. At one point, when the lore behind some of the Backrooms’ creatures is
revealed, it seems like the moment will result in a moment of disgust. But, alas, that feeling is
undercut by a fairly mundane concept.

This was the hardest element of _Backrooms_ to assess. The film succeeds at spookiness and
disconcertion, really hitting its scary stride when the movie is in the thick of exploring the
vastness of the Backrooms, trapping both the audience and its characters. Parsons is great at
combining mazeophobia with the fear of the unknown.

However, the actual mechanics established in the film of how to access the Backrooms keep the plot
from terrorizing you after the credits roll, when lingering nightmares might normally set in. In
Parsons’ world, you can enter the Backrooms by simply walking through a specific part of a wall in
the furniture store basement. This is where some of the suspension of disbelief fails: The fact that
a number of characters willingly reenter the creepy alternate reality, and are so uninclined to exit
immediately even though they’re able to, becomes a little distracting. When the worst of what’s
inside the Backrooms gets them, it’s hard to feel much sympathy, and it’s even harder to imagine
their demise as something you’d ever fall victim to. Consider all of us who have even a modicum of
common sense marked safe from knowingly entering a twisted Upside Down–esque maze of terror.

The Backrooms part of _Backrooms_ is great, well-rendered, and incredibly unsettling. Though the
story supporting the film could use a little work, the production is overall an achievement. It is
particularly good at doing the one thing all horror movies are supposed to do: eliciting feelings
we’d rather not experience in our real lives. All of the mechanics and tactics that Parsons uses,
his mastery of which are evidenced by the acute terror his earlier shorts evoke, are employed at the
right moments and in ways that bring a fresh look to the horror genre.

With that being said, my mother always warned me to never walk through walls that defy the rules of
metaphysics and lead to creepy-ass unending mazes of yellow rooms. All of the drama of _Backrooms_
would be solved if they just turned around. And that, unsurprisingly, cheapens the overall effect
just a tad.
