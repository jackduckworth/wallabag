The First Great Thriller of 2026 Is Here. Will It Leave You Scarred?

====================================================================================================

_For die-hards, no horror movie can be too scary. But for you, a wimp, the wrong one can leave you
miserable. Never fear, scaredies, because Slate’s_ _Scaredy Scale_ _is here to help. We’ve put
together a highly scientific and_ _MOSTLY SPOILER-FREE_ _system for rating new horror movies,
comparing them with classics along a 10-point scale. And because not everyone is scared by the same
things—some viewers can’t stand jumpscares, while others are haunted by more psychological
terrors or can’t stomach arterial spurts—it breaks down each movie’s scares across three
criteria: suspense, spookiness, and gore._

Come one, come all, devout disciples and atheists alike, to _Is God Is_. The film—the directorial
debut of Aleshea Harris, who penned the play of the same name from which the film is adapted—is a
tour de force that perfectly blends Afro-Surrealism, humor, and gritty drama. The storyfollows two
twins, Racine (Kara Young) and Anaia (Mallori Johnson), who were badly burned, alongside their
mother (Vivica A. Fox), in a house fire that was set by their abusive father (Sterling K. Brown)
when they were young. In adulthood, the twins journey through the Black South to find their
estranged father as they decide whether they will, at the behest of their mother (who serves as this
story’s “God”), exact their revenge by killing him. At its heart is a torrid tale about the
cyclical nature of abuse. In the movie’s opening scene, which tells the twins’ backstory, a
narrator states that the twins are still burning to this day; “they have no say in it … they
just burn and burn.” So just how gruesome does this incendiary tale get? Will the weakest among us
be able to sit through the horrors to get through to the deeper meaning?

Thankfully for the high-strung among us, _Is God Is_ won’t overwhelm you with jumpscares. The film
contains only one scene that is akin to a traditional horror sequence—you know, the kind where you
can feel that a jolt is coming because the music cuts out and the character is wandering through
different rooms of their house in an attempt to identify where an ominous noise is coming from. _Is
God Is_ is focused more on its socioeconomic commentary on the Black South, and on subverting the
character tropes we often see as belonging there, than on spiking your cortisol. The frights tend to
come instead from the real-life issues it’s highlighting, though those are chilling.

_Is God Is_ may not be terrifying in the traditional sense, but it is _gruesome_. Given the nature
of the tragedy at its center, that should perhaps be no surprise. But it also dips a toe into body
horror by considering a world wherein the characters’ old wounds still actively burn enough that
they rub ice on them nightly, years after the initial incident. And other grisly scenes are more
unexpected, as when someone’s tongue gets ripped out, or when one character beats another
character to death with a rock in a sock. Though the worst is depicted off-screen—you don’t
actually _see_ the tongue excision, for example—the film is very liberal with the spatters of
blood. And while we might be spared some of the sights, we still must endure the sounds: The
agonized screams as people are being burned, and the Foley work as bones and sinew fail to stay in
their original form, could definitely linger with you. If you’re easily disturbed by these sorts
of images (or to narratives of abuse), I would approach _Is God Is_ with caution.

It’s hard to predict whether _Is God Is_ might continue to unsettle someone after the credits
roll. The film carries themes that are as old as time—notably, how violence and trauma only beget
more violence and trauma—but even if it’s not reinventing the wheel, this wheel ain’t broke.
Brown’s performance as the abusive father is so good (as Brown often is), and the theme of being
unable to escape the real-life demons that follow you is so palpable, that I found myself often
thinking about the movie long after I left the theater. Because I’m someone who will always be
more shocked by the more grounded evils of a thriller than by supernatural dangers, _Is God Is_ has
become an entry into that personal canon of movies that still scare me when they’re long over. I
remember the movie’s beauty and humor as much as the darker moments, but audiences that are more
sensitive to the cruelty of man: be warned.

Moviegoers who come to _Is God Is_ expecting to have the living daylights scared out of them will be
sorely disappointed. However, cinephiles who enter the theater anticipating a complex, balanced
meditation on the best and worst parts of the human condition will definitely find a lot to
appreciate and leave with a lot to think about. _Is God Is_ offers a deft rumination on the effects
of domestic violence, on the devastating cocktail of psychopathic tendencies and patriarchal power
that can fuel it, and on how the world treats you when your scars are something you can’t hide.
And still, the story is peppered with the beauty of womanhood, of sisterly bonds that last longer
than the body does, of the positioning of motherhood as something so divine and magical it may just
be the only accurate definition of “God.” Though it has no shortage of miseries, it also says a
lot about our resilience. Still, make no mistake by thinking that its ending wipes away those
atrocities—they might be hard to shake.
