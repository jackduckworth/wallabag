It’s the World’s Most Popular Sports League. Nothing Else Comes Close. Why Has It Been So Hard for Me to Enjoy It?

====================================================================================================

This story is adapted from the Slatest newsletter. You can sign up here to get it in your inbox
every day.

Happy Friday, everybody. This is Luke Winkie. I’m filling in for Ian today, so let’s take a
break from the usual ignominies we cover on the Slatest to talk about something completely
different. By the time you read this, I will be at a bar cheering on Crystal Palace FC—an English
Premier League team founded in 1905—which I have been a fan of for exactly two weeks now. This is
my latest attempt to turn myself into a European football fan after far too many false starts to
count. To my horror and delight, I fear that it’s actually working this time.

I don’t have a good reason for why it’s taken me this long to make the leap. I’ve never been
one of those churlish, anti-soccer American chuds. My dad is from England, and naturally there are a
number of photos of me wearing a hand-me-down West Ham United jersey to elementary school. I have a
distinct childhood memory of being shocked awake at 3 in the morning during the 2002 World Cup—the
one that was held in Japan and South Korea. I thought the house was on fire; instead, England had
just scored on Brazil. (The team would go on to lose 2–1, a tradition that was just reconsecrated
in July.) But the particularities of the sport outside its elegant, digestible, quadrennial national
context were always too byzantine to wrap my head around. What the hell is a “transfer fee”? How
is there any drama in, say, a league where Bayern Munich has 34 of the 63 championships? Why are
there so many different tournaments? I still don’t know the answer to these questions. It’s
going to take me _years_ to become fluent in all the nuances of the sport. But so far, I’m having
a lot of fun.

Patrick Reis, Slate’s politics editor and EPL sicko, tells me that Crystal Palace has recently
sold several of its best players, which is a thing he said I ought to get used to, as it tends to
happen a lot. That sounds about right to me. I chose my beloved Eagles (that’s their nickname; I
had to Google it) because I enjoyed the juxtapositional sumptuousness of the name—surely there is
nothing resembling a “crystal palace” in deep South London—and because I was tickled by the
fact that the organization is owned partly by Woody Johnson, chairman of the New York Jets. Johnson
has apparently instituted a number of USA-style flourishes into the program. (For instance: Crystal
Palace is the only team in the Premiership with cheerleaders.) And, most importantly, as a washed-up
hipster living in central Brooklyn, I am surrounded by fans of Arsenal and Tottenham Hotspur—two
teams I also know nothing about—and I simply refuse to conform to that coalition. Being an Arsenal
fan and being into the band Geese is the same type of thing.

Here is where I must mention that I grew up in San Diego, the Arctic of the American sports
landscape. I have spent most of my life rooting for anonymous Padres and Chargers squads that nobody
in the national media cares about. As such, when I saw that Crystal Palace had the lowest number of
subscribers on its subreddit of any side in the league, I felt a natural kinship.

Today we are playing Manchester City, the nouveau riche heavyweight in the EPL. (It is estimated
that City is paying something like 200 million pounds for its roster. Crystal Palace is somewhere in
the 70s.) Similarly, in the offseason it was frequently rumored that Manchester City coveted Crystal
Palace star midfielder Adam Wharton, whose verve and creativity pops off the screen even to my
untrained eyes. This is the ugly part of European football: the irradiated hypercapitalist hierarchy
perpetually preying on the weak in a way that makes college football look meritocratic in
comparison. Historically, this has been my greatest barrier to full-throated soccer fandom.
Americans are sold a linear understanding of professional sports. There is this idea that through
market-controlling systems like the draft and the salary cap, any team, no matter how minor or
shabby, may stumble into a transcendent talent and anoint a dynasty. Tim Duncan played 19 years in
San Antonio! Those principles simply do not apply in the EPL. My wide-eyed aspirations need to be
lowered significantly. Yes, you still may savor the glory of a great player wearing your colors, but
there’s a good chance they’ll be packing up shop by the time they’re 25.

This means that in order to truly appreciate European football, one must find a different way to be
a fan. And, frankly, maybe it’s healthier to develop a version of fandom that isn’t maniacally
obsessed with the illusive hope that with the right stroke of luck, the San Diego Padres will one
day win the World Series. That such a destiny is written in the stars, or that it ought to be
guaranteed through the sheer gravity of my angst. Years ago, I was talking to my cousin—also
English—about how one could find the Premiership compelling when only a small handful of
upper-crust blue bloods ever have a shot at winning. He told me, with absolutely no artifice, that
what he finds more interesting about the championship is whether Hull would face relegation or if
Leeds could finish in the top half of the table. Relatedly, my cousin is a fan of the Wolverhampton
Wanderers, a team that finished in last place in 2025 (three wins, 11 draws, 24 losses!!!) and has
spent long stretches of its existence in the wilderness. I got the sense that he was less capable of
getting disappointed by those failures than I would be.

This is the attitude I intend to take with me to the bar today. Come On, You Palace! Let’s at
least beat the spread.
