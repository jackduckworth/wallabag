Republicans Have One Huge Advantage Ahead of the Election, and They’re Banking On It to Avoid a Midterm Wipeout

====================================================================================================

With three weeks to go until the midterms, Democratic campaign groups and super PACs are speaking
with one voice: They have the right environment. They have the right message. They have the right
candidates.

They just need more money. ASAP.

“Given the Republican cash advantage, we now find ourselves in a situation where we could be
leaving winnable races on the table,” Julie Merz, executive director of House Democrats’
campaign arm, wrote in an Oct. 9 memo, warning that a dozen or so opportunities—those only
reachable because of the anti-GOP environment—might be missed without an infusion of cash. The
memo encouraged donors to cough up another $17 million over the next two weeks. House Democrats’
main super PAC, House Majority PAC, put its request at $38.5 million. On the Senate side, the main
Democratic super PAC, Senate Majority PAC, warned that “Republican-aligned outside groups have
built a financial advantage neither party has had before, large enough to bury our many advantages
under a paid media blitz before November.”

And Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer has put the cash disadvantage front and center in his
messaging: “With just three weeks until Election Day, the battle for control of the Senate comes
down to one defining question,” Chuck Schumer wrote in a letter to his colleagues on Monday.
“Will the unprecedented tsunami of Republican money defeat the massive Democratic advantage in
candidates, policies, and political climate?”

“Unprecedented tsunami” isn’t hyperbole. While it’s hard to get an exact number on the cash
advantage, the Associated Press estimates that the “$1 billion that Republicans have amassed
compares with roughly $579 million held by a comparable collection of major Democratic groups.” A
Washington Post analysis of the top 50 donors this cycle shows Republicans having raised $1.07
billion from the group relative to Democrats’ $454 million.

These numbers don’t even account for third-quarter sums that have yet to be disclosed. Elon Musk,
as the New York Times first reported, gave a stunning $276 million to GOP groups and super PACs in
the third quarter, raising his total giving for the cycle to $366 million. The $158 million alone he
gave to Senate Republicans’ top super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, covers the group’s
staggering investment in Texas to rescue Ken Paxton.

“Democrats are in the strongest position yet to win a Senate majority with a map we have expanded
deep into Republican territory,” Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee executive director Devan
Barber said in a statement. “Republicans are making a last-ditch effort to rescue their flailing
candidates and hang onto their vulnerable majority by flooding battlegrounds with increased
spending. Democrats have been vastly outspent across the battleground and need additional resources
to maintain the advantage we built with stronger candidates and a better message.”

Where did this cash advantage come from, anyway?

It’s not that Democratic groups are struggling to raise money (with the notable exception of the
Democratic National Committee). As is typically the case, individual Democratic _campaigns_
themselves are regularly outraising their GOP counterparts, whose candidacies are more reliant on
big checks from outside groups. Consider the Texas Senate race, where James Talarico’s campaign
had raised $69 million through the end of the second quarter while Paxton had only raised $9
million. While that’s a particularly lopsided example, Senate Democratic campaigns hold
significant advantages in most other major battlegrounds. And Democrats’ main House and Senate
super PACs each recently announced record third-quarter fundraising totals.

The bulk of the disparity can be traced to a GOP fundraising vehicle for which Democrats have no
equivalent: MAGA Inc., the president’s super PAC. By the end of August, MAGA Inc. had $416 million
on hand. It begun disbursing that money in bulk to House and Senate races after Labor Day.

The nature of how Donald Trump, as well as other GOP super PACs, came into this money has become a
campaign talking point for Democrats. By speaking so openly about the cash disadvantage, Democratic
leaders aren’t just trying to raise more money. (Though they are _definitely_ trying to raise more
money—think of these despairing memos to “interested parties” as the highfalutin, big-donor
equivalent of daily Democratic spam emails threatening the apocalypse if you don’t donate $1 RIGHT
NOW.) They’re working the fundraising disparity into their larger messaging on Trump’s
corruption, with MAGA Inc. and others serving as a cash receptacle for those seeking something from
the transactional president. As Schumer put it in his letter to colleagues, “The checks are coming
in from every entity looking to buy a pardon, curry favor, and move the needle in favor of their
personal interests.”

“Trump’s modeled a way to get this unlimited amount of cash from people who are seeking business
before the government,” Lauren French, a spokesperson for Democrats’ Senate Majority PAC, told
me.

So how does this financial edge for Republicans help them—and how does it not?

In short, it keeps Republicans—who have very little going for them—in the ballgame and allows
them to avoid some tough decisions. While Republicans have started to pull ads in some states and
districts they deem unsalvageable, that list would be a lotlonger were it not for the hundreds of
millions of bonus dollars that have materialized for them. Only recently did Republican outside
groups cut funding from the Georgia and North Carolina Senate races; they may not have put much
money into those states to begin with in any other cycle. They have the resources to backstop
suddenly vulnerable Senate candidates in places like South Carolina and Nebraska, and can spend $150
million to save Jon Husted in Ohio while Elon Musk effectively foots the roughly similar bill to
rescue Paxton in Texas. And in the House—where MAGA Inc. is sprinkling a few million dollars here,
a few million there to dozens of races—the money can prevent the bottom from falling out.

But money isn’t everything—especially super PAC money. And Republicans haven’t managed their
largesse particularly well.

It’s not just that campaigns can purchase ads at lower rates than super PACs. It also matters
_when_ those ads are purchased. Much to congressional Republicans’ chagrin, Trump’s team dawdled
for months before committing their MAGA Inc. funds. That means Republicans have been buying ads in
already saturated markets at expensive rates. Advertising rates in Texas are now so expensive, for
example, that pro-Paxton super PACs have found it cheaper to run ads nationallyduring football games
than to buy time in the Texas media markets. In short, Democrats, by having better-funded campaigns
and reserving advertisements earlier in the year, are getting more bang for their buck.

It’s also important to note that for ads to work, it’s important for them to be _good_.

Even with the higher rates that Republicans are paying, they are airing more ads. But a Politico
survey from last week presented a peculiar finding: 70 percent of respondents “said they’d
either seen more spots in support of Democrats or an equal amount between the parties” over the
previous week, while “only 24% said they’d seen more ads for Republicans.” In other words,
Democratic ads appear to have been more resonant. It’s not just that Republicans have less to run
on in this environment. It also raises questions about how well these ads are being slapped
together. With hundreds of millions of dollars being pumped into the ecosystem at once, and
ad-makers earning astounding sums of cash for their quick work, you’re going to get a lot of slop.

“I was talking to a Republican consultant who ran one of the big super PACs and he, like, hates
all of their media consultants,” Anna Greenberg, a Democratic pollster, told me. “Because
they’re grifters, they make so much money. There’s no incentive for these Republican media
people to do a good job. Because they make, whatever it is, tens of millions of dollars whether they
win or lose. And so they buy so badly.”

However haphazardly they manage it, the GOP’s financial advantage is still an advantage. It
won’t change the overall direction of the midterms, magically giving Republicans a good night
against such wretched fundamentals. Where it matters is on the margins, and so much rests on the
margins this cycle.

In order to take control of the Senate, Democrats don’t just need to flip the regular old swing
states. They need to flip some redstates. Among the options: Ohio, which Trump won by 11 points in
2024; Alaska, which Trump won by 13; Texas, which Trump won by 14; Iowa, which Trump won by 13; and
Kansas, which Trump won by 16. (Then there is Maine, which Harris won by 7, but where Susan Collins
is sui generis.) Democrats, in other words, need a strong Election Day to take control of the House,
but a historicone to take control of the Senate. If all Republicans’ billions can muster is even
one measly percentage point in their direction, that could be enough to turn Democrats’
performance from one of unlikely Senate conquest to one of very, very impressive losses across the
map.
