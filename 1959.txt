The Fall of the West by Owen Jones review – a bracing history of the changing world order

====================================================================================================

Like many Britons born in the 1980s, the journalist and Guardian columnist Owen Jones came of age
with Tony Blair's platitudes ringing in his ears. Politics at the turn of the millennium was a story
of temporary dispensations mistaken for eternal truths: the definitive triumph of free market
ideology; US military supremacy; a rules-based global order. As Jones recounts in his new book,
these certainties would come crashing down over the next quarter of a century amid a succession of
economic failures, foreign policy disasters and political crises.

Indeed, "the west" itself – as a shorthand for the US plus the wealthier western European nations
– feels almost quaint today, given the increasing unilateralism of the administration in
Washington. But in the 2000s it was part of the lexicon, as if the hemisphere were a political actor
in its own right. Revisiting the US-led wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, we are reminded not just of
their horrific death tolls (totalling half a million, by conservative estimates) but also their
total strategic failure; the 2021 evacuation of Kabul airport, Jones writes, was "among the starkest
images of American decline yet broadcast to the world".

In addition to destabilising the region, these interventions weakened the foundations of the
international legal system. As Jones points out, a precedent was set when Nato bombed Yugoslavia
without UN security council authorisation in 1999; the 2003 invasion of Iraq, also without
authorisation, further undermined the UN. So when Vladimir Putin subsequently invaded Ukraine on his
own dubious pretext, western objections could be dismissed as hypocrisy. Perhaps the most
ignominious example of western indifference to the international rule of law was Keir Starmer's limp
assertion that Israel had the "right" to cut off water and electricity to 2 million civilians in
Gaza – a war crime – in retaliation for Hamas's attacks on 7 October 2023.

Alongside these geopolitical catastrophes, Jones tells a story of domestic decline, of western
economies built on chronic insecurity and precarious employment. In the 2000s, Thatcherite policies
pursued by New Labour fleeced the public: "Essential services [were] turned into assets designed to
generate returns. Passengers paid more for overcrowded trains. Rivers and seas were fouled while
water companies loaded themselves with debt and paid out dividends." Then came the 2008 financial
crisis and the bankers' bailout, paid for by austerity. In Britain and elsewhere, Jones notes
a "correlation between the inability of centrist governments to tackle economic decline in any
meaningful way, and the rise of the far right".

The book is interspersed with snippets of interviews with an assortment of eyewitnesses,
journalists, scholars and activists. They include the director of Iraq national museum in Baghdad,
a former Lehman Brothers employee, a former head of the Afghan Red Crescent, the Greek economist
Yanis Varoufakis, and the conservative commentator Andrew Sullivan, a repentant cheerleader for the
Iraq war, who admits: "I was wrong on the major issues, on the morality of it and the practicality
of it and the reason for it."

Much of this terrain – Bush and Blair's wars, the financial crash, the populist surge – has been
extensively covered. The average well-informed reader will learn nothing new, but it is bracing to
see what was once the news cycle take its place in the sweep of history, and Jones's focus on
free-market fundamentalism provides a compelling through-line. He suggests the second Trump
administration, in all its cynical venality, is "the logical endpoint" of that philosophy. And
he reminds us that the aggressive privatisation of the Russian economy in the 1990s brought
terrible economic hardship which ultimately fuelled Putin's revanchist nationalism.

For decades, electorates have been mis-sold a fanatical ideology: the prerogatives of the private
sector must override the collective interest, and anything less is tantamount to Stalinism. This
remains orthodoxy, but the rise to prominence of Zohran Mamdani, the avowedly socialist mayor of New
York, offers a glimmer of hope to those who, like Jones, would prefer a society built on "common
provision, democratic power, mutual obligation and the recognition that our fates are bound
together".
