Viewers Are Obsessed With Netflix’s Controversial New Hit. After Watching It, I Get It.

====================================================================================================

There is no better narrative setting for exploring the cross section of guilt and desire than a
Catholic school full of repressed teenage girls at the dawn of the digital age. Judging by the
cheeky tagline—“They pray hard and sin harder”—of Netflix’s latest hit series, this is
something _The Body_ understands. The eight-episode drama, which follows a group of schoolgirls
after they jokingly perform a satanic ritual that results in a mass hysteria event, has turned into
something of a mass hysteria event itself. _The Body_ swiftly became a Netflix No. 1 show, achieved
internet virality, and, judging by how hard the streamer is promoting the series and its relatively
unknown stars, has become a Netflix darling approaching the breakout success of last year’s
_Heated Rivalry_. At the same time, a reported 100,000 people of Christian faith have signed a
petition demanding that Netflix ban the show for spreading “blasphemous” ideas about their faith
and encouraging shameful behaviors. (A counterpetition in support of the series has already garnered
over 20,000 signatures.) With its core of psychological horror, sapphic longing, and tonal and
aesthetic throwbacks to Y2K-era girlhood classics—think _Mean Girls_, _Carrie_, and _Jennifer’s
Body_ meet _Jawbreaker_, _Gossip Girl_, and even a dash of the 1998 horror film _Ring_—it’s no
wonder _The Body_ has blown up in every way imaginable. And after watching it myself, I can safely
say that I get it: _The Body_ is one of the best shows of the year.

The seriesdrops us off in 2004, on Halloween, at St. Mark’s, a Catholic high school nestled in a
small religious town where the school’s all-girl dance team, the Lions, is holding auditions to
find a replacement for an injured classmate. The head captain and quintessential popular girl, Anya
(Kristina Bogic), oversees the auditions, which have a clear front-runner: Grace (Sofia Wylie), the
niece of the St. Mark’s headmaster. Following tradition, the Lions have to haze a new member,
which is how, at a Halloween party that evening, the girls end up jokingly performing a satanic
ritual offering up Grace, whom they’ve pressured to take ecstasy, as a virgin sacrifice. But after
Grace falls into a coma later that night, the girls’ paranoia about their complicity in her
ailment—was it the drugs, or was she actually possessed by Satan?—puts them on edge. When one
teammate falsely claims to have seen a vision of Grace, it sparks a chain of other students
experiencing sightings of Grace; soon, rumors about the comatose girl’s so-called spiritual powers
begin to take hold over the entire town. In reality, much of the hysteria is orchestrated by Lisbeth
(Geena Meszaros), a loner who covets both Grace’s spot on the team and a closer relationship with
Anya, who is busy struggling to accept her mutual attraction to her best friend, Elise (Sara
Boustany).

Here, the phrase _the body_ serves as a reference to the body of Christ—which already has multiple
meanings: the church, Communion, and a reminder of the violation of Jesus’ body—as well as to
the body of a teenage girl and all it suffers, from eating disorders to drug use to frustrating
sexual experiences. Though heavy-handed, the metaphor doesn’t feel rudimentary. While the Lions,
in their guilt, believe that their visions of Grace are some sort of godsent comeuppance, reading
between the lines suggests that these anomalies are more easily chalked up to a psychosomatic
response to copious drug use and teenage paranoia. The few coping mechanisms—drugs, sex, religious
absolution—these girls have only cause them to wig out further.

The themes that creator Quinn Shephard tackles—the tensions of growing up as a girl, of having a
queer sexual awakening, and of Catholicism’s weaponizing of guilt to hold power over its
disciples—are familiar but irresistible in this complex portrait of girlhood. Guilt already
plagues the teenage girl experience even without the weight of religion or sin. It’s a time in
girls’ lives when everything is changing, from bodies to identities to relationships with parents
and faith. _The Body_ is a poignant story about the unlikely places where the teenage mind will run
in order to explain truths it doesn’t want to confront.

What makes _The Body_ so good, though, is that it explores all of this without sacrificing its
biting humor or sense of fun. My favorite media about the trials and tribulations of being a teenage
girl shoulder those burdens with style and whimsy, both of which _The Body_ has in abundance. There
are the charming, era-appropriate references: flip phones, the AOL Instant Messenger notification
sounds, Jeff Buckley needle drops and a refrain of Grace’s Kelly Clarkson “Breakaway”
ringtone, and even the show’s bejeweled title card, which looks like something you’d see on the
backside of an early-aughts Juicy Couture sweatsuit. Lisbeth’s fearsome conniving is undercut by
the hilarity of her having no eyebrows. The dance scenes are rare but fun and frenetic, and the
personalities of even the show’s background players (like _The Traitors_’ Gabby Windey, who
plays the Lions’ pregnant coach) carry the humor. If there are any glaring anachronisms, they lie
with the boys, like Anya’s boyfriend, Steven (Orlando Norman), who is much more up to 2026’s
social standards of a significant other than one would expect of a 2004 Catholic schoolboy jock.
It’s fine, though—the boys aren’t the stars here, thank God.

It’s been a minute since we’ve had a hit high school drama, and even longer since we’ve been
treated to a good follow-up to the likes of _Jennifer’s Body_, a cult-favorite film that is as
smart and funny as it is unapologetically brutal about the devastation and rage that occurs when
predatory men meet young girls. In this moment, when the present-day culture wars—tradwives!
incels! rollbacks on reproductive rights!—have wreaked havoc on young women’s (and men’s)
bodies and minds, _The Body_ couldn’t have come at a better time.
