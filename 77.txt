This Is a Very Embarrassing Way to Find Out How the Economy Works

Things went downhill very quickly this week for Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, one of the eminent
figureheads of the global artificial intelligence boom. Not long after his extramarital relationship
with a Chinese spy—and the Trump administration’s active cover-up—was publicly revealed, the
New York Times reported that Huang had had _another_ affair with one of his direct reports. Mere
hours after the news broke, Nvidia’s board of directors convened to figure out what to do about
their philanderer in chief; Huang then stepped down Thursday morning, with the board appointing CFO
Colette Kress as his interim replacement. Shortly after _that_ switcheroo was made official, Reuters
reported that he had engaged in yet another tryst with a second Nvidia employee.

“Jensen’s contributions to NVIDIA are profound,” the board wrote in its press release.
“NVIDIA is one of the most consequential technology companies in the world.”

Somehow, those are both remarkable understatements. Jen-Hsun “Jensen” Huang is a Taiwanese
immigrant who trained as a microchip engineer during the 1980s personal-computer revolution, then
launched Nvidia in 1993 to produce more of the intricate semiconductors that gave Silicon Valley its
name; he’s thus inextricable from the highly valued company that’s come to be the most essential
hardware vendor of an A.I. age that demands more and better chips by the day. The leather-jacketed
Huang has been CEO of Nvidia since the beginning, and he savvily pivoted the corporation’s focus
once generative A.I. sped off. His business deals are key for keeping the stock market roaring. His
products are so treasured that Huang is now something of a geopolitical kingpin, earning an upfront
view of the trade negotiations purportedly intended to maintain the United States’ technological
lead over China—_and_ to help his home country, Taiwan, expand the foothold of its all-important
Semiconductor Manufacturing Company.

It’s nigh impossible to succinctly convey how vital this man has been to the new global arms race,
how singularly intertwined his cult of personality is with one of the most powerful corporations of
the modern age. (As Huang biographer Stephen Witt put it in a recent _Hard Fork_ interview:
“Nvidia _is_ Jensen, to a degree that I’ve almost never seen in any other corporation.”) His
misdeeds and resignation aren’t merely alarms as to the fragility of the A.I. bubble. (Look at my
plummeting tech stock market, dog.) They’re set to influence the future trajectories of the
largest global economies and trade routes. The memes pointing out how an entire sector has relied on
one man’s libido are thus closer to the truth—and far more troubling—than they may otherwise
appear.

Unless you’re an especially nerdy gamer, you may not have known much about Nvidia prior to 2023,
when it rode a wave of ChatGPT-era hype to become the ninth publicly traded trillion-dollar
corporation in American history (and, subsequently, a bit of a meme stock). Nvidia was first and
best known for developing video cards that helped the burgeoning gaming industry incorporate
increasingly lifelike, sophisticated graphics that once rudimentary consoles and PCs couldn’t
otherwise handle. That positioned Nvidia perfectly for A.I., as major steps forward in visual
autogenerations and rapid-pace text outputs were enabled by the very chips that could keep intensive
video games running without overheating. But the A.I. boom proved to be a more formidable beast. The
demand for Nvidia’s graphic processing units in particular escalated so quickly that it became an
all-around market leader for the broader semiconductor industry, its shortages and production
rollbacks and order pileups and supply restrictions becoming indicative of market health as a whole.

There was also the China factor. Almost everything about the American A.I. agenda, under Presidents
Joe Biden and Donald Trump, has been positioned in opposition to our great-power rival—maintaining
competitive advantages in computing power, research, and real-world deployment. Yet Huang, in spite
of his Taiwanese origins and business ties with TSMC, never sounded as China-hawkish as his fellow
A.I. executives. He’s publicly hedged on the matter of his home state’s sovereignty, he
personally traveled to China amid the peak of Trump’s tariff-based trade-warring, and he
repeatedly objected to the trade restrictions that both China and the U.S. imposed on the former’s
access to Nvidia’s most premium chips.

Certainly, Nvidia has long had exposure to the Chinese market, with the People’s Republic
accounting for nearly a quarter of the company’s data center revenue as of 2023. There was sheer
business interest here, despite Nvidia’s ballooning income from several other countries. But what
the spy scandal astoundingly demonstrated is that the Trump administration is not as China-hawkish
in practice as in rhetoric. As my colleague Ian Prasad Philbrick noted, the U.S. government already
agreed last year to roll back some of the Biden administration’s export controls as Huang lobbied
the new president (and donated handsomely to the ballroom). More alarmingly, Trump’s circle _knew_
that Jensen’s paramour Alice Huang (no relation) had connections to Chinese intelligence, yet
_allowed_ Nvidia’s CEO to sell chips to her Singaporean company, Megaspeed International. Notably,
Nvidia reported early last year that about one-fifth of its all-around revenue hailed from
Singapore, then erased those numbers from follow-up statements after the Commerce Department began a
probe into Megaspeed—which it wound down after Nvidia asked the feds to lay off.

“Once Congress started asking whether a suspected Chinese intelligence asset had access to the
CEO, Huang could no longer walk into a meeting with regulators or contracting officers without that
question walking in with him,” wrote tech lawyer Eugene Rome in a statement to Slate. “Every
license application and contract renewal would have become about him and his relationship with the
spy—and any other compromising personal relationships—instead of the company.”

What we have here isn’t limited to the matter of an American CEO who let the glory get to his head
and began comporting himself as a Casanova. (Many such cases.) The geopolitical deceptions here
frankly amount to nothing less than a major rethink of how _the global A.I. industry has been
operating_. The sector-centric saber-rattling with China, sold to the American public as the basis
for aggressive data center buildouts and increasingly unwelcome A.I. applications, is revealed to
have been kayfabe at best (at least, under the easily flattered Trump). The people of Taiwan, who
once had such pride in their countryman’s global success, feel betrayed that even _he_ sold them
and their key industry out to the Chinese forces that persistently threaten their autonomy. The
nature of our tech trades with an entire region, Southeast Asia, will have to be probed. And, most
saliently for you and me: The man who birthed the company that’s made the A.I. rush possible is no
longer there, and there’s no clear successor with the same levels of internal knowledge, ability
to inspire employees, willingness to flatter Trump, and get Wall Street hyped the heck up.

What l’affaire Huang ultimately portends, then, is nothing less than an overhaul of the wholesale
A.I. business as we know it, further muddying the future of this economy-propping,
job-market-disrupting, politics-exploding realm. No one quite knows where we go from here. The only
thing we know: Jensen Huang, the man who once defined this tech revolution as we know it, will no
longer be leading that charge.
