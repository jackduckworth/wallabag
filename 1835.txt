Anita Brookner by Hermione Lee review – an enigmatic literary great given her due

====================================================================================================

'Dr Weiss, at forty, knew that her life had been ruined by literature." The first fictional
sentence Anita Brookner ever published, aged 53, in A Start in Life, certainly set a tone. If people
have heard of Brookner today, Hermione Lee says, they imagine a lonely, cardiganed woman yearning
for an absent lover. "This," says Lee (an emeritus English professor at the University of Oxford),
"won't do."

The cardiganed image confuses the author with her protagonists. Of 24 novels written across 30
years, only the first four – A Start in Life, Providence, Look at Me and Hotel du Lac – are
about a single woman pining for love. There is certainly a "Brooknerian" style, characterised by
elegance, restraint and melancholia, but her fiction engaged with our emotional lives broadly,
examining family, exile, home, trauma, ageing and much more.

Born in 1928 and raised in south London by a fearsome Polish-Jewish grandmother and absurdly
ineffectual parents (a tobacco salesman and a failed singer), Brookner was a highly academic only
child. After a degree at King's College London, she briefly tasted freedom as a student in Paris,
but was soon dragged back to care for her extraordinarily needy mother. Yoked to home for the next
decade, she managed, nevertheless, to become a brilliant art critic and a charismatic lecturer at
the Courtauld Institute, where she stayed until retirement.

In the introduction, Lee describes hearing the author's voice saying, repeatedly, "Go away", and
Brookner's 20s and 30s were the "most hidden, the least witnessed" of all. Brookner did her utmost
to erase her life, and Lee is forced, therefore, to mine any detail she can find. As well as
Brookner's academic work and extensive reading habits, we get her poor handwriting, fear of trains,
love of perfumes and at one particularly desperate point, salary increases over two decades. A
couple of love affairs in her 30s fizzled out, but if there was ever a record of her feelings,
Brookner expunged it. The first 200 pages, then, can be hard going. She was, says Lee, through
gritted teeth, "an object of mystery and puzzlement".

But Lee persists – always rake thin, Brookner kept slimming biscuits in her handbag; she loved
babies; she doled out tranquillisers to nervous PhD candidates – and just as this enterprise
starts to feel doomed, something miraculous happens. The tireless details begin to cohere and
Brookner steps out of the shadows, intriguing, contradictory – the super brain who shaved 10 years
off her age in her own Who's Who entry; the self-proclaimed "loneliest woman in London" who had
loads of friends.

The older she gets, the more interesting this book becomes. In 1979, her beloved Courtauld mentor,
friend and boss, Anthony Blunt, was exposed as a Soviet spy. Shaken, Brookner remained loyal until
she discovered he'd used her to extract information from a hospitalised colleague.

Reeling and depressed, facing another lonely summer, she found herself writing fiction. Her process
was extraordinary. Each novel took only three months from start to finish. She'd roam London forming
sentences in her head, then simply write them down, at speed. Hilary Mantel, a fervent admirer, said
Brookner arrived as a novelist perfectly formed, "as if she had hatched from a marbled egg". She
would repeat this process, annually, for more than two decades.

In 1984, her fourth novel, Hotel du Lac_,_ about a woman who jilts her fiancee and retreats to a
Swiss hotel,won the Booker prize. Pretty much everyone expected JG Ballard's Empire of the Sun or
Julian Barnes's Flaubert's Parrot to win. The misogyny was mind-bending. At the ceremony, Ballard
hissed, "You stole my Booker." Private Eye snarked about "Hotel du Lac of Interest" and a grim
pattern of critical disdain was set. For the rest of Brookner's writing life, critics would carp
about "doily" novels and compare her to Mills & Boon. Profiles were invariably illustrated with sad
spinsters holding cups of tea. It tormented her. But she kept on writing.

She was, personally, far from isolated. She had devoted friends, including the Virago publisher
Carmen Callil and fellow writers such as Rosamond Lehmann or Julian Barnes, who offers loving
memories here. He was so intimidated by her eloquence when they lunched that he'd feel himself
inserting semicolons into his own speech.

Was she a literary genius? She once, brilliantly, described novel-writing as "trying very hard to
remember something which has not yet taken place". Lee offers genuinely transporting literary
criticism, unearthing the elegance and complexity of the work, but admits the compulsive production
of a book a year diluted the impact and sometimes the quality. Still, she singles out three –
Family and Friends, Latecomers and The Next Big Thing_–_ as truly "outstanding" novels.
Thankfully, it is no longer cool to Brookner-bash. This astute and tireless biography succeeds,
against the odds, in unearthing the author – who would be appalled by our scrutiny. Above all, it
shows that the work deserves our full attention.
