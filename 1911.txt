A Major New Oscar Contender Has Arrived. It Is Once Again About … Putting on Hamlet.

For the second year in a row, the Toronto International Film Festival’s audience prize might go to
a movie about putting on _Hamlet_.

Last year, of course, the People’s Choice Award—a reliable, if not infallible, bellwether of a
Best Picture nomination—was _Hamnet_, which doubled down on Shakespeare’s greatest fictional
tragedy by fusing it with the real-life death of his 11-year-old son. This year, the movie that had
TIFF-goers sniffling in their seats is _Elsinore_, starring Andrew Scott as actor Ian Charleson, who
took the lead in a National Theatre production of _Hamlet_ mere weeks before he died of AIDS-related
complications.

Building a tragedy around _Hamlet_ is arguably the dramatic equivalent of starting on third base,
but _Elsinore_ doesn’t simply pop in a few lines of Shakespeare when it runs short on poetry.
While _Hamnet_ thrives on the parallels between the play and the grief that, at least in theory,
inspired it, _Elsinore_ drives at an unstated irony: _Hamlet_ is about a man who wants to die, and
the movie is about a man who is determined to live.

Although Charleson played one of the leads in the Oscar-winning _Chariots of Fire_ and a supporting
role in _Gandhi_, which won Best Picture the following year, he never quite became a movie star, and
by 1989, when _Elsinore_ picks up the story, he’s openly envious of the meteoric rise of Daniel
Day-Lewis, whose forthcoming Hamlet is already the talk of the town. But when Day-Lewis abruptly
walks offstage in the middle of the performance—as legend has it, because he saw the ghost of his
own father onstage—there’s a sudden opening, and director Richard Eyre (Johnny Flynn) wants Ian
to fill the part. Ian, however, has just discovered his first Kaposi’s sarcoma, and his doctor
(Olivia Colman) suggests it would be wiser to put all his energies into fighting the disease rather
than taking on a four-hour play that climaxes with a swordfight. He can play the role of a lifetime,
or he can prolong his own life. And, as he tells her, “I’d rather die of _Hamlet_ than AIDS.”

The movie, directed by Simon Stone and written by Stephen Beresford, touches on _Hamlet_’s
greatest hits: There’s Yorick, the nunnery, and eventually some “To be or not to be.” But it
also circles back again and again to a line that doesn’t usually get as much attention:
“Remember me.” Originally spoken by the ghost of Hamlet’s father and repeated by his son, it
becomes _Elsinore_’s refrain, not just for Charleson but for the era he represents, when even the
most enlightened clinics still burned the bedding after a patient died of AIDS and coming out of the
closet could be a career-killer. Charleson, whose sexual orientation was not made public until after
his death, is heavily criticized by the pioneering gay director Derek Jarman (David Dawson) for
putting his Hollywood aspirations above the fate of his community. (The fact that Charleson
apparently left his part in Jarman’s _Jubilee_ off his résumé adds salt to the wound.) At the
film’s Toronto premiere, Beresford admitted that after the two men’s friendship ended over
Charleson’s decision to stay closeted, they never spoke again. But he invented an arc for them
that plays out across several scenes and becomes the spine of the movie, two gay men living with
AIDS who choose different paths and find a way to reconcile, although that arc has far more to do
with the politics of 2026 than the way Jarman actually felt.

Based on the lengthy ovation that greeted Scott when he returned to the stage after _Elsinore_’s
screening, he’s a lock for a Best Actor nomination, but he doesn’t make Charleson into the kind
of tortured, regretful gay man the Oscars tend to favor, especially when the character is played by
a straight actor. The dying actor never blames himself for getting HIV or rages against the heavens
at the cruelty of his fate, but neither does he accept the trade-off of prolonging his life by
diminishing it, whether that means accepting the side effects of AZT, which he derides as a failed
cancer drug, or withdrawing from the rigors of doing eight shows a week. Instead, he chooses to make
what may be his final mark on the world, prepared for what comes next, because, as Hamlet himself
puts it, the readiness is all.

As a young stage actor, Scott drew comparisons to Charleson and played a lauded Hamlet of his own in
2017. But in _Elsinore_, he reimagines the role through Charleson’s eyes, and the movie dwells on
the moments when Hamlet works through his fear of death and comes out the other side. Stone gives us
few prolonged moments from the play, using the rehearsal process to jump through the text and ending
the film with an extended montage that freely intermixes Charleson’s life and his art. Some may
regard the rush of images and Stefan Gregory’s swelling score as heavy-handed, but the sequence
opens the floodgates as intended and brings us to the moment when Hamlet realizes that the
uncertainty he feared above all has been pierced; he sees his own death with clarity and, at least
in this telling, to a peace beyond it. The rest is silence.
