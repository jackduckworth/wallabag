There’s One Series I’ve Recommended Over and Over. It’s Proved a Hit With Everyone.

====================================================================================================

Fantasy and mystery can be tricky to combine. Part of what makes the _Ana and Din_ series so much
fun is watching how deftly Bennett integrates those genres, playing off the freedom of an invented
world against the strict rules of the fair-play detective story. The result is wildly imaginative,
sometimes pointed, often hair-raising, and very entertaining, a series that has been a hit with
everyone I’ve recommended it to.

At the heart of the series—the third installment, _A Trade of Blood_, has just been
published—are his two sleuths. Dinios Kol, who narrates the novels, has been chosen to assist Ana
Dolabra, whose investigative acumen is rivaled only by her peculiarities. A young man from the
provinces who sends his salary back home to a family struggling under the debts run up by his
wayward father, Din knows relatively little about the greater world, which makes him a fine avatar
for readers who are also gradually discovering how the empire works. While the Holmes-and-Watson
parallel may spring most readily to mind, the true model for Ana and Din’s working relationship is
the Nero Wolfe mystery series, by Rex Stout. Wolfe, an agoraphobic gourmand, sends his assistant,
Archie Goodwin, out onto the streets of New York City to gather information, then uses Archie’s
reports to solve crimes from his capacious armchair. Here, Ana, whose mind finds sensory input so
overwhelming that she wears a blindfold most of the time, prefers to cogitate in her room, reading
old books and devouring bizarre and exotic meats while she waits for Din to return with the goods.

But Ana and Din are Nero and Archie turned up to 11. Like many of the people in the Khanum Empire,
they’ve been biologically altered. Din agreed to undergo a painful process that has left him
capable of exactly remembering events and information; he’s basically a human recording device.
The middle-aged Ana’s modifications are one of the series’ enduring enigmas, since she suggests
in the second book, _A Drop of Corruption_, that she has also been altered so as to render her
unable to speak of them. Profane and tactless, Ana has a habit of grinning (“like a sharkfish,”
Din thinks at one point) when pronouncing zestfully on the most ghoulish and sinister developments
in a case, and most of the people they encounter during their investigations consider her mad and a
bit terrifying. Din, who always addresses Ana as “ma’am,” like the dutiful sergeant in a
British detective show, serves not only as her eyes and ears but as a sort of mediator with the
various sources and fellow officers Ana tends to offend and alienate.

The banter between these two is the most delicious element in the series. _A Drop of
Corruption_—a classic locked-room mystery, along the lines of _Knives Out: Wake Up Dead
Man_—includes a scene in which Din reports to Ana on his investigation of a safe-deposit box,
where the murderer has left a pair of clues, one of which is a severed head:

> “He left … a fucking note?” she asked, incredulous. 
> 
> “It, ah, appears so, ma’am. Along with a head.” 
> 
> Ana’s grumbling stretched on and on. She seemed to have entirely forgotten I was there. 
> 
> “Ah, ma’am?” I asked. “Are yo—” 
> 
> “I mean,” she thundered finally. “He left a note! I just … I just can’t fucking
> believe that he had the audacity to leave a note! It bothers me terribly!” 
> 
> “I, too, was bothered, ma’am,” I said. “Though mostly by the sight of the hea—” 
> 
> “But frankly, this whole thing reeks of audacity!” She ripped her blindfold off, shot to her
> feet, and began pacing the room like a convict just told of an extension to their sentence …
> “A fucking note! I feel we needn’t bother looking at faces to find this man, Din! Just keep an
> eye out for the fellow with testicles large enough to cause back deformities, and we shall have
> our culprit!”

As circumspect as Din may be with his superiors, the reader knows of his complicated inner life. In
the first novel of the series, _The Tainted Cup_, he conceals the dyslexia that has hampered his
advancement in the ranks of the empire’s officers, using his enhanced memory to compensate for his
difficulty with reading. The alterations that made him an engraver (as those with this class of
skills are called) come at a cost, rendering him sterile and subject to diminishing sanity in later
life, as the burden of so much exact memory takes its toll. Din regards this as a sacrifice he’s
made for his family, although it’s not until _A Trade of Blood_ that the reader meets a member
of that family and learns that they see things differently.

Through Din’s eyes, the series explores Khanum, a marvelously ambiguous state based loosely on the
Ottoman Empire. The empire, a series of cantons ringed by concentric walls facing the coast, is
shaped by a single imperative threat. Every year, during the “wet season,” enormous beasts
known as leviathans rise from the depths of the sea and attempt to lumber onto the land, leaving
vast swaths of destruction in their wake. Everyone in Khanum lives in exquisite awareness of this
danger, and only the empire has been capable of summoning the resources and workers to fend off the
leviathans. Eventually, it emerges that the beasts themselves are the source of Khanum’s
biological technology. The empire is a society with some obvious flaws, as Ana and Din often
discover in the course of their investigations, but the series maintains a sense that it is worth
improving and saving.

The duo’s investigations also allow readers to explore the wonders of Khanum’s freakish world
through the customary rituals of detective fiction. This is a place where an injured
engineer—someone whose job it is to maintain the walls and weapons that keep out the
leviathans—is confined not to a hospital bed but to an algae bath, and where, if you lose a hand
in the course of duty, medics can deploy a swarm of altered snails to rebuild it. The murder victim
who kicks off the series in _The Tainted Cup_ dies as a result of trees that grow explosively out
of his body. There’s more than a dash of body horror in the _Ana and Din_ series, but that’s
tempered by Bennett’s endless inventiveness in coming up with ways that the altered life forms in
Din’s world might serve to replace the useful machines and devices in ours.

Because, of course, the two worlds are not really that different underneath. Khanum, too, has its
wealthy elite class that gets away with too much and its determined strivers like Din. Just as our
mechanical technology has consequences, so does the organic technology of Khanum. This becomes a
persistent theme in the novels and often serves, if indirectly, as a motive for the crimes that Ana
and Din investigate. It’s easy to see the parallels between their world and ours, and if this
engrossing series has a flaw, it’s Bennett’s penchant for including tendentious and superfluous
author’s notes at the ends of the books explaining the messages he intended to impart about
autocracy or the environmental impact of the beef industry. (Since only the second and third novels
feature these notes, perhaps the fact that _The Tainted Cup_ won both the 2025 Hugo Award for Best
Novel and the 2025 World Fantasy Award went to his head.) But that’s a minor and eminently
skippable shortcoming in a series that promises to reveal more tantalizing secrets about
Khanum—with its emperor who has lived for centuries but hasn’t been seen for several of them,
and its myth-shrouded past—and especially about Ana and Din themselves, all while delivering the
reliable pleasures of a great detective yarn, very strange and delightfully familiar all at the same
time.
