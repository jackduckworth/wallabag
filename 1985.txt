

====================================================================================================

Tom Cruise’s New Movie Is a Disaster of Almost Apocalyptic Proportions

====================================================================================================

A video call from an employee on an oil rig Digger owns lays out the unfolding crisis: His
company’s reckless drilling has caused a massive methane explosion, sending a chunk of Arctic ice
the size of a major city floating across the North Sea toward Europe. Even Digger, a leader with
little investment in the details of his business—he has 42 unread emails in his inbox warning him
of the disaster—has to admit that this development is serious enough to call for a meeting of the
nation’s top leadership. Unfortunately, that leadership is made up of such nincompoops as the
blustering President Compson (John Goodman), his simpering vice president (Michael Stuhlbarg), and
the belligerent secretary of defense (Robert John Burke) who wants to nuke the iceberg into
submission.

It’s at this point that, like the stupid, cruel men in that boardroom, this writer faces a tough
choice: Should I reveal anything about the early twist that sets the whole movie on a different path
(though without making it any more watchable)? Because there has already been a wave of reviews
published that mainly keep the twist under wraps, and because _Digger_ is one of those films
that’s hard to discuss meaningfully without disclosing at least its basic premise, I plan to
briefly describe that first big tear in the movie’s narrative fabric. So proceed knowing that
SPOILERS ARE AHEAD.

About half an hour in, this whole story we’re watching turns out to be part of the final dress
rehearsal for a massively ambitious play, with the result that every member of _Digger_’s cast is
now playing a dual role: as their character in the stage production and as the actor playing that
character. “Is this funny? Is it _meant_ to be funny?” asks Cruise as Curtis, the anxious and
decidedly non-Digger-like performer cast in that role. The play’s harried director (Sophie Wilde)
doesn’t have a satisfying answer, and it’s not clear that we’re meant to either, but
Iñárritu, who wrote the script along with Alexander Dinelaris, Nicolás Giacobone, and Sabina
Berman, evidently wants the viewer to reflect on those questions as we make our way through his
movie’s intricately constructed, sumptuously designed, yet ponderously oppressive labyrinth. With
the introduction of the play-within-a-movie device, the blunt political obviousness of those early
scenes—and of the many bluntly obvious scenes to follow—is thrown into a different light. But
what is this shift in illumination trying to show us? And what should we make of the disappointing
fact that this new extradiegetic universe is even less dramatically engaging than the already dull
diegetic one we were previously trying to follow?

The choice to turn _Digger_ into a metafiction springs from an instinct that could have been
promising: By revealing the story’s structure as a multimedia nesting doll, Iñárritu brings the
spectator into his movie’s moral world. Like Curtis when he breaks character to browbeat the
director about the play’s meaning, we are suddenly free to question our own role as the paying
observers of a representation of a crumbling, chaotic reality eerily similar the one that waits
outside the theater. In that sense, _Digger_, a highly citational film that at times seems to
reference _Citizen Kane_, _Fail Safe_, and _Don’t Look Up_in a single overwritten scene, also
recalls recent metafictional explorations like Wes Anderson’s _Asteroid City_ or Charlie
Kaufman’s _Synecdoche, New York__._ Both those movies built frame stories around proposed
fictional entertainments whose sheer scale would have made their full representation, either onstage
or onscreen, impossible. The play-within-a-movie segments of _Digger_ are as spatially and visually
cinematic as an image created by three-time-Oscar-winning director of photography Emmanuel Lubezki,
a frequent Iñárritu collaborator, can be. His nimble camera, alternating between unflattering
low-angle close-ups and virtuosically blocked scene-length single takes, never stays bound within a
proscenium frame—though images of theatrical-style curtains do abound, alongside a
groan-inducingly symbolic poster for John Cassavetes’ backstage drama _Opening Night_.

Lest you be fooled by all these name-drops of other, better movies (yes, even _Don’t Look Up_ is
better), let me reiterate that _Digger_ is a grandiose folly of a film, a failure on its own perhaps
impossible-to-satisfy terms. It fails evenat its most basic task, which was to supply Tom Cruise
(who also produced and has been central in the movie’s cryptic marketing campaign) with a role
that would allow him to break out of the action-hero cage in which he’s willingly placed himself
for the past decade. Cruise can be startlingly fearless and funny when he plays a grotesque
antihero: the crude studio executive Les Grossman in _Tropic Thunder_, the arrogant motivational
speaker Frank T.J. Mackey in _Magnolia_. Hearing he had been cast as a broad comic villain in an
Iñárritu political satire, I had hopes that his performance would be memorable even in a
less-than-brilliant movie. Alas, neither Cruise nor anyone else in the stacked cast gets a chance to
do much more than demonstrate that their speakers go up to 11—or rather, to my ear, at least,
start there.

As Cruise has shown over a long career of scaling high-rises and leaping between battling prop
planes in midair, he is an all-in performer who will risk everything to prove to his audience that
nothing was left on the table. But the suffocating bombast of _Digger_’s script gives both of
Cruise’s characters little to do other than expound directly and at length on the movie’s
already unsubtle themes. The script presents a similar problem to such usually unsinkable stars as
Goodman, Stuhlbarg, Jesse Plemons, the recently ubiquitous Sandra Hüller (who plays the one-note
role of Digger’s embittered eco-entrepreneur half-sister), and the always welcome Riz Ahmed. Ahmed
does all he can with the slightly more demanding part of the movie’s sole relatively sane and
humane character, environmental scientist Dr. Ganesh, who’s brought in to tell Digger and his
pasty cabal the unwelcome truth: that their callous selfishness has condemned the whole planet to a
future that, if people like them (and us) had only acted sooner, need never have been so bleak.

The closest _Digger_ gets to normal human interaction is in the one-on-one scenes between Digger and
Ganesh, who, however thinly drawn, at least seem like characters with opposing goals attempting to
make themselves understood to one another—a basic component of drama that much of this stylized
rhetorical showcase is sorely lacking. An extended one-take scene in which Digger addresses the
European Parliament, shot in pallid tones that emphasize that legislative body’s near-uniform
whiteness, has been cited by many critics as the movie’s high point. Though I found this bit just
as ideologically ham-handed as the rest, it is certainly a master class in endurance, both for the
tireless Cruise and for the by then thoroughly tired audience.

Iñárritu has long been a master of miserabilism, first in his brutally downbeat dramas (_Amores
Perros_, _Babel,_ _21 Grams_, _Biutiful_) and more recently in his formally recursive would-be
comedies (_Birdman_, _Bardo_, and now _Digger_). His decision to turn his gaze outward from the
narrowly self-referential show-business milieu explored in _Birdman_ and _Bardo_ to a broader
sociopolitical landscape in _Digger_ constitutes, in theory, an admirable big swing of the kind
multiple-Oscar-winning international directors with access to big budgets should be taking more
often. But for all its well-considered craft—the dazzling production design by Richard L. Johnson
and Dennis Gassner, the inventive if gratingly omnipresent score by Cosmo Sheldrake—_Digger_ is so
invested in its Brechtian framing conceit that it seems to forget the intended purpose of Bertolt
Brecht’s signature “alienation effect.” That revolutionary theatrical technique was meant to
keep audiences at a distance from the narrative drama unfolding onstage, forcing them to confront
their position as consumers of bourgeois entertainment. Instead, Iñárritu immerses us in not one
but two fictional worlds that are both so cacophonous and off-putting all we long for is to get home
and throw a nice bourgeois entertainment on the nearest streaming channel. There’s a difference
between being high concept and being high on your own supply.
