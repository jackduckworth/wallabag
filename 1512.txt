The Best Match of the World Cup So Far Was a Total Shocker in Every Possible Way

====================================================================================================

INGLEWOOD, CALIFORNIA—As I arrived at Monday’s Group G World Cup match between Iran and New
Zealand, the first thing I saw was a group of about 50 demonstrators standing on a corner outside a
choked traffic checkpoint holding up signs decrying January’s mass slaughter of protesters in Iran
by the Islamic republic’s government: “40,000 people murdered in two days,” the sign declared.
An accurate count is not known, but at least 7,000 citizens were butchered by their government in an
incredibly brief span just five months ago, to little account.

As hundreds of happy Iran soccer supporters—almost certainly opponents of the regime
themselves—walked by, the loudest protester shouted, “You’re going to cheer terrorists! Good
for you!”

The cry honestly sounded tinged with jealousy. The Iranian soccer team is one of the best in world
soccer and has historically been beloved both in Iran and among the large Iranian diaspora that
hates the regime for which the team plays. This previously unifying force, though, had become a
lightning rod prior to this week’s game, with boycotts and protests promised following the horror
of January.

The question ahead of this match, for all Iran fans, was: Is it OK to cheer this team?

Inside Los Angeles Stadium on Monday—in a town that is home to 230,000 Persians, the largest
Iranian diaspora in the world—the answer was very much yes, as the team played to a 2–2 draw in
one of the most unexpectedly thrilling games of the World Cup, cheered on by a crowd of 70,000, most
of them Iran fans. The soccer was very, very fun.

What those fans witnessed was fantastic and wholly unexpected. The chatter before the game was that
New Zealand was the worst team in the tournament—ranked dead last among the field at 85th in the
FIFA World Ranking, behind such minnows as Cabo Verde and Curaçao—and that the contest against
20th-ranked Iran should not be close or interesting. Instead, New Zealand rose to the moment and
nearly achieved the upset and a first-ever World Cup victory, while Iran struggled in defense for
the entirety of the game before pulling off two comebacks to earn the point.

Team Iran, to be fair, had contended with more than any other side in World Cup history. Its
domestic league was suspended in March due to the American–Israeli war against Iran, as most
players have missed months of match play. For a long time, it was unclear whether the team would
attend this World Cup, with the Iranian federation threatening to boycott and U.S. authorities
threatening not to allow the team into the country. Even when the visas were granted, they were
limited to two days at a time and excluded many team staffers. After the team’s base camp was
moved from Tucson, Arizona, to Tijuana, Mexico, the visa situation required the team to travel
across the border the day before match day and back to Mexico immediately after the game. On top of
it, the Iranian federation left one of its best players, Sardar Azmoun, off the roster due to
perceived “disloyalty” to the regime. In a prematch press conference, manager Amir Ghalenoei
disingenuously shrugged off the exclusion, comparing it to Neymar missing the early phase of the
tournament due to injury.

On top of it all, when game day finally arrived, many of the team’s fans booed Iran’s national
anthem. And although many said they would boycott, the fans who did show up in the tens of thousands
staged their own form of microprotest, with attendees holding up the “Lion and Sun” flag of the
previous Iranian regime that had been officially banned by FIFA as overly “political.” Despite
seeing a few desperate attempts by match stewards to get fans to put their flags away, they mostly
remained in the stadium and were waved visibly during the match’s biggest moments.

Oh, and until Sunday’s peace deal, this would have been the first-ever World Cup match in which
the host country was at war with one of the competing teams.

So the Iran squad, despite its heavily favored status, was not without its challenges.

Still, at the whistle, the Iranians came out as if none of that mattered and they wanted to win the
entire tournament in the first five minutes. They perhaps emerged too hot. Despite wave after wave
of initial early attack by Iran, 26-year-old New Zealand winger Elijah Just scored one of the best
link-play goals of the tournament. In just the seventh minute, after a fantastic back-and-forth with
star Burnley striker Chris Wood, he opened the scoring to the utter shock of the crowd.

At that early point, it seemed as though all of Iran’s tribulations might get the better of the
team, with New Zealand dominating over the next 15 minutes and nearly scoring a second goal a number
of times, including one absolute sitter for Wood right in front of a goal that the Iranians just
barely managed to clear.

Then, in the 23rd minute, the crowd exploded as Iran captain Mehdi Taremi, a striker for Olympiacos
in the Greek first division, nearly scored on a phenomenal outside strike but just hit the right
post.

The crowd returned in full force after that effort and after the momentum once again shifted back to
the Iranians, who nearly earned a penalty and then came close to scoring on a wide-open goal after
an error by All Whites goalkeeper Max Crocombe left the net unguarded.

It was a windy and relatively chilly evening in the stadium, below 70 degrees for much of the
match, and the two-and-a-half-minute hydration break felt even more unnecessary than usual, coming
almost immediately after a long period of injury stoppage. As a cameraman with one of the official
World Cup broadcasters put it to me, these breaks were clearly about “more money for FIFA: more
beer, more soda, more business.” _Less soccer_, was my immediate thought, as these breaks have
consistently broken up the momentum of the game and of whichever team had been getting the better of
the match.

This was not the case for Iran. When play resumed, the team got its breakthrough equalizer, as Ramin
Rezaeian poked the ball in right past Crocombe off a rebound from striker Shahriyar Moghanlou.

These two early goals set the pace for the entire match, which went back and forth with both sides
having a number of very good chances throughout. Right before halftime, Iran seemed to take the lead
off a free kick into the box headed beautifully into the net by Iran defender Ali Nemati. The crowd
exploded more than at any prior point in the match, but Nemati was clearly offside, and the goal did
not count.

New Zealand took the lead back early in the second half, with Just scoring a beautiful second after
another fantastic one-two with Wood. Mohammad Mohebi scored another equalizer just nine minutes
after that New Zealand goal, with a beautiful header into the side of the net off a gorgeous long
ball into the box by the previous goal scorer, Rezaeian. The rest of the match was on a total
knife’s edge, with both sides having chances to steal it in injury time, before ultimately ending
in a 2–2 draw.

Again, it was one of the most thrilling matches of the tournament so far, if not the best. And this
was not a given coming into the game, particularly considering the outside drama that seemed to
envelope Iran’s squad. Prior to the match, Tarebi complained of having “felt the tension from
the first moment you arrived at this World Cup.”

“This kind of tension, it undermines that joy, and it undermines the message of FIFA and our
people, that is about football that brings about peace,” he added. “I think this World Cup could
have provided a better atmosphere than it had.”

After the match—a joyous celebration of Iran’s team by Iran’s enormous diaspora—Tarebi
seemed to view things a bit differently, expressing gratitude toward the fans, even as he displayed
bitter disappointment at the hosts for forcing the team to travel back to Mexico that very night.
Coach Ghalenoei, in his own postmatch press conference, described Iran as “the most oppressed team
in the whole World Cup.” Still, it was impossible not to be grateful to Southern California’s
Iran fans, who showed up in full force, tens of thousands strong.

“I want to mention our supporters in Los Angeles—it was an incredible atmosphere during the
game, all 90 minutes, and it was like home for us,” Tarebi said. “I hope they are doing the next
two games the same thing.”

Given the controversy around this team and the criminality of this regime, which won’t be going
away anytime soon, Iran supporters will continue to wrestle with whether to provide that support
over the next two games.

Before the game, I spoke with two members of the Persian diaspora on opposing sides of the question.
Fred Parvaneh, the social media director of the English-language Iranian site Kayhan Life, was
planning to attend the game with his sons and cheer for Iran. “I keep sports and politics very
separate,” he told me.

Parvaneh had posted something to this effect on his personal Instagram page:

> I have a bone-deep, burning hatred for the Islamic Republic and the wreckage it has inflicted on
> my family, my friends’ families, and generations of Iranians robbed of their lives and futures.
> That hatred is real and it is not going anywhere. But these athletes are not the regime, and
> anyone who cannot tell the difference has a serious problem.

This sentiment had received enormous backlash, and he had had to turn off comments on the post. In
our conversation, Parvaneh blamed the exiled son of the shah, Reza Pahlavi, for the backlash against
the team this year. He noted that Pahlavi had previously been an Iran national team supporter but
argued that he turned against the team this year for political gain. “All the people that are
monarchists that support the old regime, they follow whatever he says, so now they’re all in an
uproar,” he told me, derisively calling Pahlavi “an ex–soccer fan.”

After speaking with Parvaneh, I talked to another ex-fan, who asked to be identified only by her
former name, Sara, because she still has family and property in Iran and worried about retribution
from the government for speaking out.

Sara immigrated to the United States when she was 20, after leaving the Islamic faith and after
learning that a girl two blocks from her university had been killed in the street by authorities
after an argument over her hijab.

She understands Parvaneh’s criticism of Pahlavi’s expediency but said that after the massacre in
January, there was no way she could support this team. This is especially difficult for her, she
says, because she comes from a big soccer family—her father, who remained in Iran after she left
home, followed the team nearly everywhere it went for years. Sara says the athletes are in an
understandable bind.

“They cannot speak freely. They cannot express freely because their family will be under a lot of
pressure,” she said. “But at the same time, we can’t just choose convenience over principle
when it matters. Sometimes we have to say no.”

Sara sees the January crackdown as that _no_ moment and feels that Iran should have been kicked out
of this World Cup, the way Russia was disqualified from international competition following its
invasion of Ukraine.

Still, her brother and nephew will be traveling all the way from Chicago for the next match at Los
Angeles Stadium on Sunday, against favored Belgium. Sara says she thinks that they plan to wear
Belgium uniforms and cheer for that team, despite her family’s longtime support of the Iran team.

If Sunday’s game is anything like Monday’s, it’s going to be very hard for them not to switch
sides right back.
