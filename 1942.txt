We Finally Found Out What It Would Take for Republicans to Abandon Donald Trump

====================================================================================================

_This story is adapted from the Slatest newsletter. You can_ _sign up here_ _to get it in your inbox
every day._

For more than a decade, Democrats in Congress have wondered what, if anything, would convince their
Republican colleagues to drop Donald Trump. Despite private misgivings, GOP elected officials held
their noses even as the president insulted veterans, bragged about assaulting women, trafficked in
racism, abused his office, and tried to overturn a free and fair election. So far, their
loyalty—measured purely by expediency—has been worth it. Despite his many flaws, Trump twice won
his party unified control of Washington.

But if winning elections is Republicans’ excuse for forgiving Trump, losing them appears to be the
line that cannot be crossed. Case in point: the 2026 midterms. As the GOP limps toward November with
an unpopular, term-limited president at the helm, candidates in tough races—including several who
previously supported Trump without reservation—are starting to highlight their differences with
him. It’s both a sign of desperation and a strategic move that could break a Democratic wave.

WHICH REPUBLICANS ARE SHYING AWAY FROM TRUMP?

• Video from Slate 

As you might expect, several are in closely divided states where moderation and independence can pay
dividends. One is former Rep. Mike Rogers, who’s running for Senate in Michigan and blames
Trump’s Iran war for squeezing voters’ pocketbooks. “The war with Iran needs to end, and end
quickly,” he said yesterday. In Iowa, Rep. Ashley Hinson has called for an immediate end to the
war and avoided campaigning with Vice President J.D. Vance. And last week, two other vulnerable
Iowans, Reps. Zach Nunn and Mariannette Miller-Meeks, joined the handful of House Republicans who
have voted to halt the fighting.

SO ARE REPUBLICANS MOSTLY JUMPING SHIP OVER IRAN?

Not exclusively. Rep. Tom Tiffany, who’s running for Wisconsin governor, recently distanced
himself from Trump’s effort to rename Lake Ontario, criticized him for lowering tariffs on beef
imports, and flatly affirmed that “Joe Biden won” the 2020 election. Ohio Sen. Jon Husted, who
faces a stiff challenge from Democratic former Sen. Sherrod Brown, told the Wall Street Journal that
he doesn’t share Trump’s view that Americans who oppose A.I. data centers “want to end up
being backwards and poor.” Overall, Republican candidates now talk about the president and his
MAGA movement less often than they did earlier this year, a Washington Post survey found.

OK, BUT REPUBLICANS HAVE GROUSED ABOUT TRUMP BEFORE WITHOUT DOING MUCH ELSE. WHY SHOULD I TAKE THESE
BREAKS MORE SERIOUSLY?

In some cases, the candidates are literally broadcasting them. Rogers made his comments about Iran
in a campaign video. Others are distancing themselves on issues that used to be solidly in the
president’s wheelhouse. Last week, Florida Rep. María Elvira Salazar said in an ad that Trump’s
approach to immigration enforcement has “gone too far.” Even Republicans who should be
campaigning on safe ground are looking nervous. On Tuesday, the Miami Herald reported that Rep.
Byron Donalds, who’s running for governor in solidly red Florida, had scoured prominent Trump
references from his campaign website. Other defectors can’t even point to political expedience.
Sen. John Curtis of Utah isn’t up for reelection until 2030. But on Tuesday he said the Senate
should investigate whether Donald Trump Jr. is trading on his father’s name following revelations
that a Russian oligarch helped pay for his wedding.

BUT ISN’T DISTANCING FROM AN UNPOPULAR PRESIDENT PRETTY NORMAL?

Similar things have happened to Democratic presidents who found themselves underwater come midterm
season. In 2010, which famously turned into a shellacking for Barack Obama, many Democratic
candidates shied away from his agenda. Biden got a similar treatment in 2022. But what makes these
defections notable is that Republicans are doing it to Trump, who is infamously thin-skinned and
treats political tactics like personal slights.

HOW’S TRUMP RESPONDING?

Mostly with denial. “President Trump is the unequivocal leader, best messenger, and unmatched
motivator for the Republican Party,” a White House spokesperson told Politico this week, despite
polls showing even GOP voters souring on his handling of Iran, inflation, and other issues. The
president, whose approval ratings have hit all-time lows in some surveys, has also tried deflection.
“They’re not angry at me, but they are angry at Republicans,” he said of voters last month.
Some Republicans who have offered Trump constructive criticism claim he’s listening. On Sunday,
Salazar recalled a pleasant conversation she had with the president after warning that Hispanic
voters “feel betrayed” by his immigration agenda. “He was a gentleman, very courteous, very
receptive,” Salazar said. We’ll see if that lasts.

OBVIOUS QUESTION: WILL THIS ACTUALLY WORK?

Distancing from an unpopular president helped some Democratic midterm candidates win in red and
purple states during the Obama and Biden years. Republicans, meanwhile, whiffed several winnable
races in 2018 and 2022 because their candidates hewed too closely to Trump, proof that not breaking
from a floundering commander in chief can cost seats (even if Trump later helped Republicans retake
power).

On the other hand, the fervor of Trump’s base makes crossing him risky, and voters rarely reward
Johnny-come-latelys. Earlier this year, Rogers was extolling the president’s Iran war as “a
mission for safety and justice” and downplaying Michiganders’ anger over gas prices. Hinson has
repeatedly voted against House resolutions that would’ve ended the war, including last week.
Tiffany tried to help Trump reverse Biden’s 2020 victory. Throwing the president under the bus is
one thing. But for Republicans belatedly trying to backpedal, the bigger problem may be that they
were for Trump before they were against him.

STORIES FROM OVER THE YEARS. FOR YOUR EVENING ENTERTAINMENT, HERE ARE A FEW OF THEM. 

FOR THE PERFECTIONISTS: Back in 2011, Farhad Manjoo argued that “you should never, ever use two
spaces after a period.” His typographical cri de coeur has entered the annals of classic Slate
pitches.

FOR THE SCI-FI FANS: Did you know that Slate used to publish speculative fiction? Dive into this
short story about humans’ first contact with an alien who’s been corrupted by some very modern
vices.

FOR THE ROMANTICS: Emily Yoffe’s essay “My Husband’s Other Wife” is a poignant ode to her
husband’s first wife, who died of breast cancer tragically young. Political Gabfest host Emily
Bazelon praised the essay’s unflinching candor in exploring her relationship, noting that
“it’s the honesty that makes me cry.”

FOR THE DETECTIVES: There was a period of time when you could not escape “Baby Shark.” The
kids’ song became a worldwide hit in 2019—but that wasn’t the first time this earworm went
viral. Willa Paskin investigated the multidecade rise of “Baby Shark” in a great episode of
Decoder Ring.
