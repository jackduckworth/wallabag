I’m a Military Historian. The Odyssey Movie Is About As Historically Accurate As The Lord of the Rings—but It Nails Something Crucial.

====================================================================================================

The summer is just warming up, and it is already safe to call Christopher Nolan’s _Odyssey_ the
film event of the year. The first feature film shot entirely in 70 mm IMAX, it sold out IMAX
screenings in major cities across the country a full year in advance. While the three-hour R-rated
movie may fall to bigger box-office receipts raked in by competing Marvel movies coming later in the
year, the epic literary subject and the rapturous reviews make it the early awards front-runner.

Indeed, the idea that Nolan was specifically gunning for Oscars (despite the fact that he already
won Best Picture and Best Director with his previous film) is one of the many accusations leveled in
the intense culture war that has been raging around this film for more than a year, and that has
drawn in belligerents high and low, from right-leaning classicists like Alex Petkas to autodidacts
like Roman Helmet Guy to prominent journalists like Matthew Yglesias. Elon Musk, one of the most
powerful men on earth, called celebrated historian Tom Holland (not the actor who plays Telemachus
in the film) a “cuck” for having the temerity to like the movie. The arguments embroiling the
film have centered on Nolan’s casting choices (particularly Lupita Nyong’o as Helen of Troy and
the inclusion of trans actor Elliot Page as anyone at all), assuming an intent to make a “woke”
version of the story as awards bait; his admitting to being influenced by Emily Wilson’s
revolutionary feminist translation of the story; and rampant criticism of departing from the
historical reality of the period, particularly in his depictions of arms and armor.

Like all culture-war imbroglios, these arguments generate a lot more heat than light. Accusations of
a woke agenda collapse under the weight of history when you consider that the diversity of Nolan’s
casting choices much more closely reflects both the ethnic and gender diversity of the period.
(Herodotus’ detailed description of the Persian army shows a polyglot force as varied as the crowd
in Times Square on New Year’s Eve, and he tells of Artemisia I of Caria, a woman admiral who
commanded at the Battle of Salamis.)

On the matter of historical accuracy, the detractors are right, and they miss the point entirely.
When it comes to accurately depicting how the Greeks of the period armed themselves and fought,
Nolan isn’t even trying. His rendition is about as rooted in the historical record as the weapons
and armor in _The Lord of the Rings_. The multiple performers we call “Homer” were building on
an oral tradition that produced the version of the story we call the _Odyssey_ probably around the
700s B.C., and they were using the kit and tactics of that era to imagine events that happened long
ago (much as the famous _Crusader Bible_ of the 13th century illustrates the time of Christ using
medieval weapons and armor). Professor John Ma of Columbia University rightly points out that the
_Odyssey_ can be said to represent three different historical contexts spanning more than seven
centuries: It takes place during the late Bronze Age (circa 1400–1200 B.C.), incorporates elements
of the early Iron Age (circa 1000 B.C.) Greeks who passed it down orally, and was reworked in the
image of the circa–700 B.C. Greeks who first organized the many threads of oral tradition into a
single, monumental form.

So when detractors complain of ahistorical armor, which armor would they prefer? The boar’s-tusk
helmets and massive bronze cuirass found at Dendra? The “dipylon” shields of the Bronze Age or
the round aspides of the early Iron? The classical “Corinthian”-pattern bronze helmets so easily
recognized as “ancient Greek armor” in the popular imagination? Should we see loosely organized
war bands surrounding heroic leaders or the more disciplined and organized blocks of spearmen we
call the phalanx?

Indeed, in Nolan’s mob-rule battle scenes, he could be accused of trying to represent the
preclassical war bands of Bronze Age Greece, but that would be to accuse him of historical intention
at all. It’s clear Nolan didn’t care. He’s making a movie about a myth, not a YouTube video
about military history. In selecting the _Odyssey_, he is clearly focused on rendering one of
humanity’s great stories relevant for the widest audience possible, not with gratifying the niche
concerns of purist military historians like me, a vanishingly small fraction of viewers. In the
screaming over historical inaccuracy, these critics reveal the truth—the loneliness that nerdy
obsessives like us feel when we are forced to confront the fact that most folks don’t care about
minutiae like we do.

The one complaint I will indulge is that Nolan made the same mistake in his representation of the
ancient Greek warriors as Zack Snyder made in his hit 2006 film _300_. Nolan and Snyder share a
vision of warfare in the ancient world as dirty, dark, and grim. Colors are muted, sepia-toned, all
gray iron and filthy hardened leather. This Hollywood misrepresentation of ancient warfare is the
one hill I will die on. These warriors were shining, as vividly hued as the bright paint on the
ancient statuary we wrongly believe to have been bare white. Far from the dim, grimy stock of both
Nolan’s and Snyder’s palettes, an ancient battlefield would have sparkled and flashed like a
fireworks display. This failure to do justice to the actual beauty and drama of the _real_ arms,
armor, banners, horns, ships, and other kit of the ancient theater of war is, as I wrote when
comparing the helmets of _300_ to the genuine article, like making a tin can stand in for a Picasso.

But the most important topic of historical accuracy Nolan nails with such poignancy and contemporary
pertinence that I was left choked up. I have written in Slate’s pages about moral injury, how we
carry the things we do in war for the rest of our lives. The _Odyssey_ has always been a story about
PTSD, about how homecoming effectively becomes impossible after the experience of battle. We return
from combat so changed by it that we haven’t returned at all, reunited with loved ones we no
longer recognize, attempting to reconnect to relationships that no longer make sense. I will never
forget coming back from Baghdad and heading right to dinner at a fancy restaurant in Old Town
Alexandria, Virginia, staring incomprehensibly at the waiter as he tried to explain the
butter-pairing options that would go best with the wine. I clearly remember the confusion and anger
that I could barely keep off my face at the many friends who had gathered there to welcome me back,
and how I felt perplexed and furious that anyone could ever think that bullshit like this mattered
at all.

Toward the close of the film, Nolan gives Matt Damon as Odysseus an outstanding monologue as he
explains to Penelope why it took him so long to return, perfectly encapsulating the agony of this
disconnect—the realization that to go to war is to die, even if you survive, because the change is
so complete that not just the returning veteran but the world they return to are irrevocably
altered. It captures the rather generous translation of the famous poem by Heraclitus: _No man may
step in the same river twice. For it is not the same river, and he is not the same man._

The beauty and tragedy of the _Odyssey_, the reason this story resonates so powerfully across miles
and millennia, is how perfectly it grasps the agonizing truth of impermanence, that the things we do
to be worthy of those we love inevitably separate us from them.

In this, Nolan perfectly portrays the universal and enduring historical truth of war. Whatever he
may have gotten wrong in depicting physical kit and color, on this—the most crucial fact—he
absolutely hits his mark.
