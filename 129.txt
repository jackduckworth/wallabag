The Scariest Part of the Jensen Huang Scandal Is What Will Happen Next

====================================================================================================

American capitalism is a scarier and more malignant force today than it was last week. A high
standard to meet, but it’s the result of President Donald Trump’s intervention to reinstate
Jensen Huang as CEO of Nvidia. Huang’s return is a scandal stacked on top of a different scandal.
The first was the one about at least two reported affairs, one with an Nvidia subordinate (not good)
and another with a suspected spy for the country trying really hard to secure Nvidia’s chips in
violation of U.S. law (very bad). This new second scandal is about what just happened to how our
corporations operate.

Fortunately, it doesn’t take a deep dive into U.S. intelligence to see what’s wrong here: Trump
spent Monday bragging about his personal role in forcing Huang back into the C-suite, and Nvidia’s
board said it too in a statement announcing his reinstatement. Getting angry about this scandal also
does not require you to check your party registration or submit a card at the door declaring your
views on the future of artificial intelligence. You can be bullish on A.I. or you can be bearish.
Either way, this is a tire fire for American capitalism.

The company’s directors did the obvious thing when they pushed out Huang, only to watch him return
because the United States apparently now has a command economy where the great leader tells its
largest companies what to do. They have to resign, right? It’s either that or sticking around to
“lead” a company where they now have less than zero oversight of management.

If you think A.I. is crucial tech, then the stakes are even higher. There’s a fashionable debate
right now about “guardrails” and what the United States, its leading A.I. labs, and their
Chinese counterparts should do about the pace of A.I. development. Trump is not a big fan of
guardrails. He sees A.I. as a nuclear-style arms race between the United States and China. More
pressingly, he sees a stock market line graph, one of the only things he reads religiously, going up
and to the right. Neither view leaves much room for holding back a CEO who promises to keep it
climbing.

Trump is _escalating_ his laissez-faire approach to A.I. regulation. There are already few serious
A.I. regulations. What Trump has done here is, in essence, build the inverse of a fence, or lower
the bar so that it is somehow underground. There’s a difference between declining to police an
industry and outright intervening when a company tries to police itself. Nvidia tried to hold Huang
accountable, and Trump helped undo that decision. Not only will A.I. executives be able to take
advantage of a regulatory open field while making decisions that might affect the future of
humanity, but they will now have a presidential shield for the most indefensible acts, as long as
they are important enough to the industry that their absence would make the S&P 500’s line go
down. Trump cares more about the stock market looking good than about, say, gas prices. He also held
at least tens of thousands of Nvidia shares as of the end of last year. It’s good to see the big
man growing his wealth.

It was already clear that if you were Trump’s political ally or benefactor, for example, you’d
be much likelier to get a pardon, if the Justice Department would even bother to prosecute you. But
what’s happening with Huang, a carnival barker who has aligned himself closely with Trump, is even
more audacious. Huang is not just being spared from consequences at the corporate level for having
multiple affairs with some of the least convenient people in the world for a CEO of a chip company
to welcome into his bed. He is being _rewarded_ for these things, by now being bathed in more
corporate power than he ever enjoyed before. Huang was already a singular CEO, but at least he, like
most CEOs, had bosses on the board. Not now!

It’s not just Nvidia’s board that is now incapable of providing oversight of anything. Huang has
never exactly been a champion for the paced or restrained development of A.I. He has been the
opposite, a man whose stock price benefits from rapid, no-questions-asked acceleration. But at least
the competitive pressures at play had caused Huang to _entertain_ the idea of some mutual
cooperation. He was a signatory, along with Trump and other A.I. bigwigs, to the White House’s
frontier A.I. accord in September. That accord at least called for the companies developing frontier
models to have independent board committees to oversee what they were doing. During the brief period
of Huang’s exile, Nvidia’s doomed board created a committee that would work on national security
and export control issues.

A company committee does not exactly have the law enforcement teeth of the FBI. But now Trump has
obliterated even the faintest possibility of the government _or_ the private sector policing A.I.
for the remainder of his term. If people like Meta’s Alexandr Wang are right and the next year or
two will be decisive in A.I.’s growth, well, then that’s the playing field.

If they’re wrong, the most powerful people in A.I. (who already had, to put it mildly, competing
incentives) are now being guided in the most destructive direction possible. Imagine Sam Altman
decides to push ahead with the release of a new OpenAI model even though his researchers believe it
is impervious to efforts to guard against its use for the creation of child pornography. Why would
Altman not release that model anyway? He now has good reason to believe that Trump’s primary
problem with him would be if he didn’t do whatever it took to make the stock market go up.

We have already been shifting for a few years toward corporate governance by cult of personality.
Elon Musk’s accumulated dominance over SpaceX had already underlined the point that sober-minded,
old-school corporate governance was no longer a requirement at the biggest U.S. companies. When
Nvidia’s board accepted Huang’s resignation, it was evidently a last stand for the idea that
boards of directors should focus on things like “not sleeping with multiple subordinates and a
Chinese spy.” Just like they taught it at Harvard Business School! But now that’s out the window
in favor of corporate governance by cronyism and by vibes.  Whatever A.I. looks like a year from
now, it’s not a good genie to have let out of its bottle.
