

====================================================================================================

He Was an Object of Mockery. Now He’s Looking Like the Next Brad Pitt. I Think I Know What Changed.

====================================================================================================

_Primetime_, the new film from A24 and director Lance Oppenheim about the making of _To Catch a
Predator_, is a fascinating mess, one in which the whole might be less than the sum of its parts,
but the parts are so compelling that you don’t really mind. The fault lies with the film’s
screenplay, which mashes together three stories with different protagonists, tones, and thematic
concerns. One of these stories is about Andie Bender (Merritt Wever), _Predator_’s producer, and
how, as a woman, she has to fight tooth and claw for the least amount of recognition or respect.
Another is about Dan Plum (Skyler Gisondo), a young actor who has no idea what he’s getting into
when he signs on as a “decoy” luring _Predator_’s pederasts to their doom. Plum winds up a
victim of exploitation himself. He wants to bring joy to the world—his first love is musical
theater—but instead lives in the muck, playing a 13-year-old who wants to have sex with
middle-aged men. The third is about Chris Hansen, the show’s anchor, played by Robert Pattinson.
It’s here that the film’s thematic confusion becomes clearest. Sometimes Dan’s story needs a
satanic father figure, and there Hansen is, playing catch with Dan and telling him that he’s a
white knight rescuing children. Sometimes Andie’s story needs an egomaniacal misogynist, and there
Hansen is again, this time as a narcissist who cannot express gratitude even as she saves him again
and again. Finally, there is Hansen’s own story, which is about a man who has already lost his
soul trying to get it back by doing good in both his marriage and the world, a tormented Batman in a
pressed tan suit, while also pleasing his corporate overlords.

These Hansens make sense as a coherent character due only to a surprisingly agile and smart turn by
Robert Pattinson. At first, it feels as if Pattinson is doing more of an impersonation of Hansen
than anything else, but what swiftly becomes clear is that Hansen is, in some bizarre way, doing an
impression ofhimself. One of the film’s best gags is when he gets a call from his wife and answers
it with a practiced, authoritative “This is Chris Hansen,” as if kicking off the nightly news.
Hansen is so lost in his performance he doesn’t know how to get back to an authentic self.
Pattinson, outspoken in his skepticism of authenticity in real life, lets his freak flag fly, but he
also grounds these gambits in a real sense of Hansen’s rage and pain.

Let me confess something here: I have long had to endure the rolled eyes and _come on_s of my
friends due to my dislike of the work of Robert Douglas Thomas Pattinson. When I would note that his
one acting trick appears to be bugging his eyes out, or that his American accent leaves a great deal
to be desired, I would be greeted with protests that I was a snob dismissing him due to the
_Twilight_ films, or that I just wasn’t giving the poor guy a chance because he was too handsome.
Shouldn’t one consider his good taste? His risk-taking? His mischievousness in interviews? His
willingness, like his ex-co-star and ex-girlfriend Kristen Stewart, to use his remarkable good
fortune and even better looks to get weird movies made by interesting auteurs?

The season of repentance for my people (that’d be Jews) recently ended, but let me extend the time
of apologies for another couple of weeks and add another mea culpa: My friends were, for the most
part, right. Robert Pattinson _is_ a far more interesting artist than I gave him credit for, and far
more than a pretty face with an unfortunate tendency to suck in his cheeks. He really _does_ use his
good looks and stardom to get interesting movies funded. In the past two decades he’s worked with
established auteurs (Bong Joon Ho, Claire Denis, Christopher Nolan, David Cronenberg, James Gray,
Lynne Ramsay) and intriguing up-and-comers like Kristoffer Borgli and the Safdies pre–_Uncut
Gems_. He’s made blockbusters (_The Batman_, _Tenet_) and smaller films (_The Rover_, _The
Lighthouse_). He’s a leading man who often is content with solid supporting turns, including
delivering the one memorable line reading in this year’s biggest smash. He is something of a
box-office Midas as well: This year, Pattinson will be in _four_ hits, between _The Drama_, _The
Odyssey_, now _Primetime_, and the still-to-come _Dune: Part Three_. His production company is
currently producing not only _Primetime_, but _The Holes_, an off-Broadway play that’s getting
rave reviews. The people I’ve spoken to who have worked with Pattinson speak highly of his work
ethic, creativity, sincere love for film, and kindness. One compared him to 1990s-era Johnny Depp,
“but with a real heart beating underneath.”

The comparison is apt, and gets at part of why I resisted his charms for so long. We tend to expect
a certain amount of openness and vulnerability from our actors, at least in front of the camera.
Being able to reveal a character’s interiority through gesture and subtle facial expression is a
hallmark of conventionally good screen acting. We want to feel, particularly when a character is
driven to the edge, that the work is _personal_. That we are seeing an artist put a genuine piece of
themselves on the line, and that through this they are helping us to confront the most secret part
of ourselves.

Robert Pattinson is not that kind of actor. He belongs to a parallel tradition, one made up mostly
of men, that instead employs opacity to great effect. Think of Robert Redford at his coolest in
_Three Days of the Condor_, never doing more than occasionally squinting his eyes, or Brad Pitt, who
often seems to have lowered a portcullis to guard his soul. Depp, in films like _Public Enemies_ and
_Donnie Brasco_, similarly has an ineffable, impenetrable cool. The pioneer of this particular mode
was the French actor Alain Delon, who had a self so hardened you could bounce a quarter off it.
Delon could not always convincingly deliver lines, but his physical presence and charisma were
undeniable.

One of the unexpected things about the opaque actors is that many of them also have a predilection
for eccentric, over-the-top wackiness. Weirdness—especially when executed from an ironic distance
from the character—can be its own form of disguise, a stylistic risk that substitutes for the
personal risk we usually desire. It signals that we are all in on the joke, even as the joke is on
the character. In this shared laughter, the actor somehow escapes, like a New Year’s Eve partygoer
making an Irish goodbye just before midnight. It is this strain in Pattinson’s work that first led
me to underestimate him. Like Pitt in _12 Monkeys_ or Depp in any number of films, Pattinson has an
unfortunate tendency toward mugging that he has not entirely shaken. In the _Twilight_ films, I
can’t help but sense that he knows he is acting in garbage, but unlike Michael Sheen, who had the
technical chops and mastery of genre to go full Hammer horror in the films, all Pattinson could do
was bulge his eyes and blurt each syllable out as if he were being pegged while delivering it. (At
times he seems barely able to contain his own laughter.) More recently, in _Mickey 17_, his
performance was all antics. He condescended to Mickey, and thus invited our condescension, robbing
the film of complexity and much-needed soulfulness in the process.

Pattinson’s performative oddity was also richly on display in his press appearances, in which
he’s famous for lying, whether as “shtick,” because he was a pathological liar, or because he
was “just bored.” (Here, too, his story keeps changing.) The best thing written about Pattinson
remains Taffy Brodesser-Akner’s profile of him for GQ, in which he comes up with ever-odder
hijinks for them to do together. Every time Brodesser-Akner agrees to one, he changes his mind,
trying to find an even stranger activity to attempt. Thus a suggestion that they do ayahuasca
becomes swimming with sharks and escalates to, uh, exchanging fecal matter with each other to enrich
their gut biomes. Brodesser-Akner comes to realize that the ideas all have one thing in common:
“They all render me incapacitated, unable to ask him any questions, and him unable to answer
any.” Later in the profile he says, “I want to be misunderstood.”

This is all a (mis?)understandable reaction to the intensity of going through the thresher of
Hollywood celebrity at the age of 22. It’s also likely that actors like Pitt, Depp, and Pattinson
are drawn to oddity because of their impossible handsomeness. It took Brad Pitt a decade or so of
making quirkier smaller films before people started to refer to him as “a character actor in a
leading man’s body,” which might be the highest praise one can give.

When, for this piece, I looked back again at some of these early offbeat turns in films like _The
Lost City of Z_ or _The Rover_, I began to see something else as well. I saw a young man learning to
act, in real time, on camera, and in public. Pattinson really is awful in the _Twilight_ films, and
not much better in _Cosmopolis_, the first film he made with a major auteur, in between the final
chapters of his vampire franchise. But if in the former he is totally lost trying to make Edward
Cullen seem like an actual character, in the latter he is trying something new and stretching
himself in unexpected ways. Ditto the various supporting roles he took in small films with limited
commercial prospects. The pleasure of watching his work during this period is not to be found in
fully successful achievements, but rather in watching someone put the pieces together. If he was not
willing to expose some deep and forbidden part of himself, at least he was willing to hazard
embarrassment on a very public stage in order to further his craft.

Somewhere along the way, he also became an adventurous cinephile. _Good Time_, the movie he made
with Benny and Josh Safdie, arose because he sent them a fan note based on a still he had seen from
their early film _Heaven Knows What_. A producer on _High Life_, Claire Denis’ film in which he
plays a death-row inmate in space, told me that “he actively sought out Claire as someone he
wanted to work with. … He really struck us as someone who knew about cinema, cared about it,
wanted to be a part of it.” At first, they weren’t sure about meeting with him, an impulse that
“seems silly now, but at that period he really wasn’t an actor you felt you could build a film
around,” because everyone still thought of him as merely a teen heartthrob. His process on the
film was not the dramaturgical script analysis or work on affective memory taught in American acting
schools, nor was it the highly technical classical technique of the Brits. Instead, “he would
basically bring Claire sculptures and photos and other pieces that evoked a feeling. Things you
can’t necessarily put into words.” He understood that the film was meant to feel like a dream
and had a process to match.

For me, the film where I really saw that Pattinson had figured it all out, like a tennis player
retooling his serve and then suddenly also improving his forehand, was _The Drama_, Kristoffer
Borgli’s poker-faced satire of hypocritical self-righteousness among yuppie millennials. In the
film, Pattinson plays Charlie Thompson, a man who learns that his fiancée Emma (played by Zendaya,
another master of the opaque) once planned (but did not go through with) a school shooting as a
teenager. The ensuing questions about whether we can ever know another person slowly drive Charlie
mad and threaten to destroy his life. _The Drama_ succeeds because it sits on a tonal knife’s
edge, with neither the camera nor the script guiding you as to where your sympathies should lie.
Pattinson skillfully vacillates between irony and sincerity, always stepping back inside the
character when it is most dangerous and provocative to do so. In a way, this two-step mirrors the
audience’s experience of the film. How upset should we be at a monstrous evil someone seriously
contemplated but never committed? As this question isn’t answerable, so too is Pattinson’s
performance hard to pin down. A more vulnerable and sincere actor would tip the role into a mildly
heroic register and wreck the film.

If it took me this long to recognize how good Pattinson can be despite a fascinating and
ever-growing filmography, it may be because the films he’s chosen to make with his cineast’s
taste and independent spirit are often quite bad. _Mickey 17_ is Bong Joon Ho’s worst film by far.
_Good Time_ is an unwatchable wallow in the gutter, made by filmmakers who do not know how to create
stakes without characters making nonsensical decisions. _The Lost City of Z_, James Gray’s take on
David Grann’s nonfiction book of the same name, is beautiful but a snooze. Cronenberg’s
_Cosmopolis_ wears out its welcome fast, and his _Maps to the Stars_ is a mess. Robert Eggers’
work leaves me cold, so perhaps I am the wrong person to judge _The Lighthouse_, but the
description, from the friend I saw it with, as “the most expensive NYU student film I’ve ever
seen,” has stuck with me over the ensuing years. _Tenet_ is incomprehensible and dull. Lynne
Ramsay’s _Die My Love_ is played as a dark comedy but burdened by its director’s self-important
and overburdened visuals.

Pattinson is often good in these movies. _Tenet_, his first collaboration with Christopher Nolan,
may be premised on the idea that watching footage go backward is interesting, but Pattinson brings
charm and oodles of bonhomie to a bland sidekick role. (At least his Neil, unlike John David
Washington’s Protagonist, gets a name.) Like all of the Safdies’ work, _Good Time_ is about an
amoral monomaniac whose frantic compulsions wreck everything around them, but Pattinson laces his
performance as a small-time hoodlum with grace notes of tenderness for his intellectually disabled
brother (played by Benny Safdie). In _Die My Love_,Pattinson gives a completely committed, wildly
embodied performance, matching Jennifer Lawrence at her most delightfully ferocious.

Many an actor has tried to escape the burdens of early fame and industry expectations. Few have been
as successful at this as Pattinson, who has slipped, Houdini-like, out of constraints that have held
back any number of his contemporaries. He’s even somehow managed to play the lead in a comic-book
franchise without it totally dominating his career or sandblasting off all his edges. If in his
early career, Pattinson could at times try a little too hard to show off those edges, watching him
bring his playful instincts and oddball choices into a more naturalistic register is a great
pleasure. Pattinson may not always seem invested in confronting us with the deepest truths about
ourselves, but he’s more than proved his ability to embody the unease of being alive in our
current moment. This feeling of discomfort—of never quite knowing how to fit in, even when you
have matinee-idol good looks—is what unites his greatest performances. It’s what makes him so
funny, and so chilling, in _Primetime_, and why he seems on the way to finally earning his first
Oscar nomination. It’s hard to imagine another actor finding those same layers as Chris Hansen,
but it’s equally hard to imagine that Pattinson would be able to find them absent the very unusual
career he’s made for himself. Perhaps his work is, despite his protestations to the contrary, more
personal than we’ve been led to believe.
