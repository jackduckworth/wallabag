

====================================================================================================

30 Rock Once Seemed on the Verge of Getting “Canceled” for Good. There’s a Reason It Will Never Die.

====================================================================================================

_30 ROCK_ ENDURES BECAUSE OF THE DENSITY AND SMARTNESS OF ITS WRITING, AND A DEEP-SET COMMITMENT TO
ABSURDITY THAT STANDS OUTSIDE OFTIME.

What changed? For one thing, Fey and Carlock’s decision to pull the offensive _30 Rock_ episodes
seems to have worked; in the post-physical-media era, what’s not on streaming might as well not
exist. The aggressive right-wing turn of post-Elon X has blunted the force of Twitter’s callout
culture, and the power of what’s now being referred to as “Woke 1.0” has waned. Or maybe we
just love Tina Fey too much to let her go.

_30 Rock_ hasn’t seen a streaming-era revival on the order of _The Office_ or _Friends_, perhaps
because it doesn’t offer the lulling pleasures of the comfort watch. But, 20 years after its
premiere, it lives on in other ways, in turns of phrase that have become part of the everyday
lexicon (“I want to go to there”), and in the brainy irreverence of shows like HBO’s _The
Other Two_ that share its lethal allergy to pat sentimentality. (Fey and Carlock share a deep-seated
Gen X revulsion to even the faintest whiff of sincerity.) Like _The Simpsons_, _30 Rock_ endures
because of the density and smartness of its writing, and a deep-set commitment to absurdity that
stands outside of time. The show is often pegged as a satire (a word invoked in the Times’ Top 100
list), and it’s true that Fey and Co. frequently turn their sights on the culture of network TV,
parodying their parent company’s actual acquisition by Comcast by having the in-show NBC acquired
by an unglamorous conglomerate named Kabletown. But the series’ prescience also works against it
in some ways; it’s less funny to conjure a fictitious competition show called _MILF Island_ when
real life has since followed suit. What shine through now are the moments when _30 Rock_ snaps its
tether to reality entirely, entering a realm of sheer ridiculousness where only the boldest and
dumbest TV shows dare to tread. The flair for free-floating gags is one reason the show makes such
immortal meme-bait; you don’t need to remember why Steve Buscemi’s posing as a high school
student to toss off a quick _How do you do, fellow kids?_

Fey and Carlock and _30 Rock_’s writers took plenty of opportunities to bite the hand that fed
them during their seven-season run, but the show’s jabs at the cluelessness of network suits,
embodied by Alec Baldwin’s Jack Donaghy, tended to be at least guardedly affectionate, or at least
tinged with the understanding that, as a primetime sitcom with middling ratings, they were in no
position to seize the moral high ground. In the second season, Fey’s Liz Lemon, an idealistic
comedy showrunner who occasionally bends her principles to keep her show on the air, meets a
legendary TV writer, Rosemary (Carrie Fisher), who she’s idolized since childhood. Liz is briefly
inspired to follow in her hero’s footsteps and stand up to the Man—the Man being Jack, an Ivy
League titan infused with the swagger of Fey’s former _SNL_ boss Lorne Michaels—but when doing
so gets her fired, she realizes that Rosemary’s anti-establishment sensibilities have only made
her unemployable—and that the counterculture satire she was famous for in the 1960s has become
incomprehensible with time. (“Don’t you get it?” Rosemary asks after replaying one
particularly inscrutable gag. “The mailbox was Haldeman!”) It takes Liz no time at all to
abandon her principles and beg for her job back, salving her conscience by pledging to send her
washed-up role model a few hundred bucks a month.

Some of _30 Rock_’s reference points have grown more distant with the decades, like the
phone-throwing incident that inspired the Season 5 quip about Russell Crowe throwing a benefit for
the victims of his own mood swings. But its chaotic surrealism has aged like wine. Some critics took
issue with the show’s increasingly cartoonish characterizations over time, which took Liz from
being a middle-aged woman struggling to balance her career and her romantic life to a slovenly wreck
of a person who at one point reveals that she’s wearing a Duane Reade bag as underwear. But Fey
relished the opportunity to be ridiculous, as in the late-series episode when Liz gets so sick she
sneezes out gray matter, staring worriedly at a used tissue as she says, “Hopefully it’s not an
important parg of my blurn.”

Fey also leaned into the opportunity to build a show around a female protagonist who, for once, was
the opposite of aspirational. Liz wasn’t an anti-hero like the brooding male leads of so many
contemporary cable dramas, just a well-meaning idealist whose principles frequently led her in
precisely the wrong direction. In the first season, she discovers that Tracy Jordan (Tracy Morgan),
the Black movie star who has bounced bad to doing television after a very public meltdown, isn’t
reading his lines off the _TGS_ cue cards, and she comes to the conclusion that it must be because
he can’t read at all. Her white liberal concern kicks in so fast she breezes right past the
underlying racism of her assumption, and rather than call her on it, Tracy decides to turn it to his
advantage, all while stoking Liz’s conviction that she’s doing a good thing. “Today is the
first day of the rest of my life,” he crows, “thanks to one very special white lady!” (Over
the run of the series, it’s gradually established that Tracy does have large pockets of ignorance,
not because of his underprivileged childhood but because he lives in the bubble created by his
insane wealth.)

Tracy forgives Liz her soft bigotry, in part because he gets caught exploiting it to skip out on
rehearsals, but the show, at least, doesn’t forget it. Especially at the beginning of _30
Rock_’s run, the subject of race comes up on almost a weekly basis, and Liz is almost always on
the wrong side of the argument. She’s explicitly called out for racism more than once, either by
another character or by herself, perhaps with more regularity than any primetime protagonist since
Archie Bunker. And though she always acknowledges the fault and vows to do better, she rarely does.
Weighing in at the end of _30 Rock_’s run, Ta-Nehisi Coates praised the series for handling race
better than any mainstream show, not for imagining a more harmonious future but because it never
stopped “interrogating whiteness.”

Sitcom characters rarely undergo profound change, because it disrupts the fragile balance that makes
up a classic ensemble. But on _30 Rock_ that constancy takes on a deeper meaning. Its central
characters are all narcissistic monsters of one sort of another: self-righteous (Liz), vain (Jenna),
self-aggrandizing (Tracy), Republican (Jack). And if anything, they grow worse and not better over
the series’ run, at least until its endgame grudgingly allows for a hint of personal growth.
It’s not that they aren’t striving to be better people. But their flaws, as well as the rules of
television, keep holding them back.

All, that is, except for Kenneth (Jack McBrayer), the NBC page permanently assigned to _30 Rock_’s
corridors. A grinning goober with a hillbilly twang, Kenneth loves only two things: “everybody and
television.” And that love isn’t dimmed by learning how network TV’s sausage is made, or being
privy to the manifestly unadmirable qualities of the people who make it. Over the course of the
series, _30 Rock_’s writers reveal that Kenneth has wooden false teeth, that his parents were
cousins (or possibly brothers), and that he is a “flesh vessel” for an immortal being that is
over 200 years old. But whether human or otherwise, his heart stays pure. Whatever troubles arise in
the other characters’ lives, whatever battles they’re fighting—with the network, among
themselves, or with their own dark natures—he reminds them that they have come together for the
most important purpose there is: making a TV show. And while that part is more than a little tongue
in cheek, there’s something to _30 Rock_’s abiding conviction that there is no disagreement, no
difference, so great that it can’t be overcome in service of a common goal, even if that goal
isn’t particularly important. And that spirit, like Kenneth the page and like _30 Rock_ itself,
will never die.
