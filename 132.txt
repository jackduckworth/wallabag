Oobah Butler: Scams for a Better Tomorrow review – these audacious pranks are tell-your-friends funny | Television & radio | The Guardian

====================================================================================================

It feels fitting that on the same weekend Nathan Fielder's much-discussed You Can See Everything
finally arrives in UK cinemas, Oobah Butler: Scams for a Better Tomorrow launches on the small
screen. This wildly entertaining show confirms Butler as the British heir to Fielder's crown as the
clown prince of documentary-making. Butler doesn't even need a gift-wrapped subject like Theranos
grifter Elizabeth Holmes.

If you enjoy satire that punches upwards at the rich and powerful, you're in for a riotous treat. If
you prefer a ghost-faced buffoon from Birmingham donning fancy dress to run rings around idiots,
you're also in luck. He might have a name like a valet-ordering app, but Oobah Butler is a serious
talent.

A gonzo Vice journalist and self-styled "professional chancer", Butler made his name with viral
stunts. Most infamously, he created a fake restaurant in a shed and, by gaming his ratings, turned
it into London's top-rated eaterie on Tripadvisor, despite never serving a dish. He has since spread
his DIY documentarian wings with Channel 4 one-offs such as The Great Amazon Heist, in which he sold
discarded bottles of delivery drivers' urine as a bitter lemon drink on Amazon itself.

Now he makes his full series debut with this audacious, hilarious set of films. Armed with little
more than his laptop and cojones the size of cannonballs, Butler embarks on a mission to tweak the
establishment's nose, pull down the elite's pants and slip ice cubes down the back of The Man. Boy,
it's a bracing exercise.

Each of five punchy, pacy half-hours sees Butler infiltrate a different industry to disrupt it from
the inside. They're framed as "experiments" rather than stunts, and fair enough. There's a genuine
spirit of inquiry and subversive intent behind them. They are also tell-your-friends funny.

"Wellness" gets things under way with a bad science bang. If you're going to pick any sector to
lampoon, the home of vibes-based medicine and miscellaneous woo-woo would come high on any list.
"It's a gold rush of bullshit and I want in," says Butler. He's soon selling a skin serum containing
children's tears ("ethically extracted", of course) and a supplement made of lab-grown human meat.
When Butler consults a $1,500-an-hour attorney, she asks about the severity of his crimes. "Fraud
bad," he admits. "Cannibalism bad. Prison bad."

He pays influencers to plug his pretend products and gets a glowing endorsement from Cameron Diaz
(actually an Hispanic bloke who shares the film star's name). Extending the You Can See Everything
parallels, Patrick O'Neill – former chief creative officer of Theranos – pops up to advise on
marketing strategy. How reassuring.

This is followed by "Wine", in which Butler repackages smoke-tainted swill – rendered undrinkable
by wildfires in Napa Valley vineyards and fit only to make barbecue sauce or hand sanitiser – as
premium plonk costing $500 (£380) a bottle. It includes a glorious scene in which Butler cheats his
sommelier exam by inserting a vibrating butt plug to get hints from a hidden expert. Well, it works
at chess tournaments. Allegedly.

"Dating" sees Butler create a matchmaking app for the AI era. Users communicate with a "fully
conscious chatbot", which will find their soulmate. They are in fact talking to real people,
inadvertently learning how to flirt. Actual human contact is better than relying on tech. Who knew?
"Music" finds Butler taking another swing at AI by manufacturing a fake rapper named Nepo and
getting machine-written tune Who's Your Daddy? to trend on TikTok. He makes it look alarmingly easy,
exposing the algorithm-gaming that's rife in the pop business.

The season ends on a high – and more legal trouble – with "Royalty". After marrying a real-life
Cambodian princess, Prince Oobah samples the gilded life of a royal. He hobnobs at hoity-toity
dinners, blags high-end freebies and is protected by government-funded armed guards. He's soon
getting grilled by police and at genuine risk of jail time for treason.

Is it big and clever? Yes, actually. Hefty HBO budgets means he clocks up serious air miles in
pursuit of mischief. Behind his deadpan humour, Butler is ferociously smart. He not only fizzes with
bold ideas but has the bottle to carry them through to their logical conclusion and beyond. He can
sell anything to anyone, as long as they've got more money than sense. He's easy to root for because
his irreverent pranks are largely victimless. He targets institutions, rather than individuals, like
a hipster Robin Hood.

In the process, he provides a sobering insight into exactly how morally bankrupt the modern world
is. Regulation is laughable. Loopholes are exploited. Respected critics are way too gullible.
Venture capitalists are worryingly keen to invest in Butler's outlandish schemes. Yet it's also
strangely feelgood, somehow restoring your faith in humanity.

With his Warhol-esque hair and emoji-like features, Butler rather resembles a South Park character
come to life. Let's hope his distinctive look doesn't limit his shelf-life as an undercover agent of
chaos. Then again, Sacha Baron Cohen is still getting away with it. Perhaps people really are that
stupid. Pass the kiddie tears and worthless wine.
