Pacino. Scorsese. Oscar Isaac. Why Did These People All Sign On to Such a Terrible Movie?

====================================================================================================

_In the Hand of Dante_, Julian Schnabel’s new movie for Netflix, is a beautifully shot mess, with
as many starry cameos as there are candied cherries in a fruitcake. Oscar Isaac, the lead, plays two
roles, as does Gal Gadot, the love interest of both of Isaac’s characters, and as does Gerard
Butler, who’s cast as both a brutal, grouchy hit man and Pope Boniface VIII. The supporting cast
includes Al Pacino, Martin Scorsese, Jason Momoa (in a gigantic Panama hat and ice cream suit), and
John Malkovich. Either someone cashed in a _lot_ of favors to get these people on board or nobody
bothers to read scripts these days.

The movie is based on an almost entirely deranged novel by the late Nick Tosches, a writer best
known for his biographies of pop culture icons, and a particular favorite of Johnny Depp, who
reportedly bought the author’s literary estate for $1.2 million. Depp told Interview magazine
that he considers Tosches “one of the ballsiest, most poetic, and rage-filled writers,” praise
that shouldn’t necessarily count against the author, despite its source. Tosches’ nonfiction
books—particularly 1982’s _Hellfire: The Jerry Lee Lewis Story_ and 1992’s _Dino: Living High
in the Dirty Business of Dreams_—are awe-inspiring tornadoes of brilliance. His fiction, however,
is a considerably more mixed bag, with 2002’s _In the Hand of Dante_ serving as a prime example.

The premise (of both novel and movie) sounds like the makings of a Dan Brown–style thriller: A
writer named Nick Tosches gets enlisted by some mobsters to authenticate a manuscript stolen from
the Vatican archives that may be the original draft of _The Divine Comedy_, written by Dante
Alighieri himself. Anyone expecting a fast-paced corker full of secret codes, hidden compartments,
and chases down rain-slicked cobblestone streets, however, will be disappointed. The film, like the
novel, jumps between 2001 and the 14th century, where it follows Dante’s life around the time that
he began writing his masterpiece.

Both the Nick Tosches character and Dante are played by Isaac, suggesting a bold comparison between
the two writers that doesn’t hold much water. Tosches, for all his talent, subscribed to a vein of
American culture I call the Anxious Masculine. In a society that idolizes pioneers, cowboys, and
self-made tycoons, this mindset fears that creative pursuits like writing, acting, or painting are
unmanly. It’s essential, therefore, for men who do this work to emphatically demonstrate how (as
Depp puts it) “ballsy” they are by drinking a lot, getting into fistfights, and treating women
in a cavalier fashion, as well as by writing about men who do the same. For his part, Tosches
worshipped the Italian American gangsters he grew up among in Newark, New Jersey, and they often
appear in his novels as stoic fatalists imparting nuggets of flinty wisdom. The film version of _In
the Hand of Dante_ features an operatic but unconvincing heterosexual love story, but the true heart
of the story comes early on, when the Tosches character wins a nod of acceptance and approval from
Butler’s sociopathic hit man for refusing to extinguish his cigarette during their private flight
to Italy.

Butler seems to be the only actor in _In the Hand of Dante_ who realizes how campy his role is, but
then he has the advantage of not having to recite lines like “When I looked upon her beauty, I
realized I was the only stain.” The scenes set during the Middle Ages are even more egregious,
with all the characters putting on vaguely British accents and speaking in cod-Shakespearean blank
verse. “Was not the blood of friendship of greater value than the honeyed wine of verse?” Gadot,
playing Dante’s wife, Gemma, asks at one point. Isaac’s Dante is prone to fits of ecstatic
inspiration, swooning as he gazes up at the sky, which serves as an alternative to the Christian God
and to “monotheism,” which the Tosches character (in a direct quote from the novel) labels
“the root of all evil.”

This—does it even need to be said?—is not how Dante himself saw it. The movie follows the novel
in trying to reimagine the epic poet, with his elaborately delineated categories of sin and very
medieval desire to impose order on the chaos of the world, as a rebellious 19th-century Romantic
poet, all unruly passion and pagan wildness. While the Tosches character in both novel and film
rants about the inauthenticity of the publishing world, with its outrageous expectation that his
work should be edited, the historical Dante played the game. He was an elected politician embroiled
in a bitter sectarian power struggle who depended for his livelihood—after his side lost and he
was driven into exile—on pleasing aristocratic patrons. Many of the individuals depicted suffering
the torments of the damned in _Inferno_ were people who, in Dante’s view, had done him dirty.

Also—and this _really_ shouldn’t need to be said—Dante was a devout enough Catholic to produce
a 14,233-line exegesis on the Christian afterlife. Although his close friend Guido Cavalcanti got in
trouble for his atheistic beliefs, there’s no evidence at all that Dante himself rejected the
faith or the church’s authority. Similarly, Tosches (the fictional version, but apparently also
the real-life writer) wanted to believe that the poet ended up feeling “caged” in by the terza
rima form he chose for the verse in the _Divine Comedy_, because Tosches himself viewed constraining
structures as oppressive. This amounts to a catastrophic failure at imagining himself into the
hierarchy-obsessed mindset of the Middle Ages, a way of thinking about the world that Dante, with
his numbered circles of Hell, as tidy as a wall of post-office boxes, clearly shared.

The contemporary scenes in _In the Hand of Dante_ are a shade more convincing, but it remains hard
to believe that any gangster would launch a clearly expensive rare-document operation using some
random writer as an authenticator. Isaac spouts a lot of impressive-sounding palaver about vellum
types and radiocarbon dating, but he handles the presumably priceless pages with his bare hands
instead of the de rigueur white cotton gloves required by archivists. By the time the movie resorts
to a dream sequence in which Gadot appears naked on a clamshell hovering over the ocean à la
Botticelli’s Venus, the entire film has become so silly that the only thing to do between yawns is
laugh.
