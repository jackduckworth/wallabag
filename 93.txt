

====================================================================================================

Rassie Erasmus's tired ref-blaming act is wearing thin – South Africa deserve better | Robert Kitson

====================================================================================================

So here we go again. Exactly five years have passed since South Africa's head coach, Rassie Erasmus,
first felt the full force of World Rugby's ire for criticising match officials. And guess what? This
most habitual of offenders has just copped another lengthy ban – and deservedly so – for his
latest intemperate comments, notable as ever for their total lack of statesmanship, diplomacy or
good grace in defeat.

To his acolytes his outspoken nature is part of what makes Rassie such a _groot leier_ (great
leader). To many others it simply cements his status as a serial prat. For the avoidance of any
doubt everyone respects the remarkable achievements both of Erasmus and his double World Cup-winning
teams. It is the rest of the package, sadly, that is too often a major letdown.

At times the Boks' patron saint is almost reminiscent of Donald Trump in a tracksuit: crass,
petulant and seemingly immune to the damage inflicted on the "little people" who get in his way. How
else to interpret his latest outburst after South Africa's defeat by France when he suggested that
alleged incidents of foul play not picked up by either the referee or the television match official
amounted to "match-fixing" or "manipulation of the game"?

Dissatisfied coaches are a familiar ingredient of the game's bubbling stew. And, yes, Erasmus
technically has a point when he complains that several knee-drop tackles, recently highlighted as
illegal by World Rugby, were missed in Paris. Rugby is a game of tiny margins and South Africa, as
befits the world's No 1 nation, hate losing more than most.

But there is a much bigger picture involved and Erasmus remains seemingly incapable of appreciating
the insidious toxicity of finger-pointing and ref-shaming in defeat. Back in 2021, when he embarked
on his 62-minute social media rant after the Boks had lost the opening Test of their series against
the British & Irish Lions, he warned the Australian referee Nic Berry he would publicise 36
refereeing errors if the official failed to meet him. The disciplinary panel found him guilty on six
charges, imposed a lengthy ban from all matchday activities, and described his video as "an attack
on the impartiality and integrity of the match officials".

Berry, who will be in charge of England's game against New Zealand this weekend, duly attracted the
online wrath of every keyboard warrior in South Africa and felt that "my reputation as a referee and
a person will forever be tarnished." It did not stop Erasmus from being banned for two more matches
the next year after his social media posts precipitated an internet pile-on for the English referee
Wayne Barnes, also after a South African loss to France.

Which is why Erasmus's latest outpourings, aimed at Matteo Liperini, the television match official
in Paris, on the subject of multiple missed knee-drop tackles, were always going to be costly. "I'm
saying if he didn't see it, it must be a technology problem," suggested Erasmus. "If he saw it and
decided not to act on it, is that not match-fixing?"

[Jasper Wiese of South Africa is tackled by two France players.]
Only in the eyes of a practised _agent provocateur_. Erasmus attended World Rugby's refereeing
summit in Windsor this month when the mantra of penalising only "clear and obvious" indiscretions
was repeatedly stressed, as was a desire to reduce the number of TMO referrals. Yes, there were some
French tacklers who had knees on the ground as they hauled down Bok ball-carriers in the tumultuous
final quarter in Paris but the refereeing team seemed to have decided they didn't want a truly
fabulous contest to be defined by some arbitrary late penalty.

It is possible to pore over any breakdown and find multiple things that could conceivably, by the
letter of the law, be pounced upon. But, by the same token, none of it would have ultimately
mattered had South Africa, with their scrum dominating, been minded to use any other instrument
beyond the bludgeon.

It would have spared us all the tiresome "Why always me?" whingeing that invariably resurfaces
whenever northern hemisphere opponents or referees are involved. There were bound to be teething
problems with the new hip-drop directive, hastily introduced from 1 November to bring rugby union
into line with other contact sports. But to say that Ireland's Sam Prendergast, cited for the
offence after last weekend's Fiji game, will escape punishment because double standards are at work?
Talk about a victim complex.

Like all great conspiracy theorists, Erasmus is also only interested in selective facts that fit his
preferred narrative. Of course he wants the refs to be vigilant when his team face Ireland this
weekend; the strict letter of the law worked nicely for the Boks in Dublin last year. Of course he
wants to spread the notion that South Africa may have been hard done by in the Stade de France and
deserve more sympathy this Saturday. He is by no means the only coach to operate in that way but his
sledgehammer subtlety is in a league of its own.

Maybe in past years he, and his staunch defenders, could have blamed it on English being his second
language. Such excuses, however, are long since redundant. To repeat, South Africa are a remarkable
sporting nation and their rugby players remain first among equals. They deserve to be fronted by
someone with rather more class.
