

====================================================================================================

12 12 12 review – Anthony Mackie and Jamie Dornan's super fun heist hunt

====================================================================================================

A contortionist crawls out of a safety deposit box in the wall of an underground Swiss bank vault,
legs wrapped around his waist, feet resting on his lumbar spine, landing on hands and displaced
knees on the tiled floor. You know what? SOLD. You have just earned my undivided attention for the
next hour of whatever you want to present to me.

Under such highly promising circumstances does the new heist thriller 12 12 12 begin. It stars
Anthony Mackie of the Marvel Cinematic Universe and Jamie Dornan (building a nice career since
2013's The Fall, if we gloss over the 50 Shades movies and the one where he played a romantic hero
who believed he was a bee) as its antagonists locked in an international cat-and-mouse game over
eight episodes. One can only imagine the anguish when no one could make it stretch to 12.

The 12s, it rapidly and disappointingly becomes clear, are the point. The high – heist – concept
is explained to us at the top of each of the eight (like an octet of knives to the producers'
hearts!) episodes. A big heist – and our bendy guy is limbering up to gather $800m in Swiss notes,
a literal ton of money – takes 12 months to plan, 12 hours to execute and if the perpetrators are
not caught within 12 days, they tend not to get caught at all.

But wait! you say. A grand theft could take any amount of time to plan. And to execute. And what do
you mean there's a 12-day cut-off point for capture? That seems … unlikely. To which one can only
reply: did you not just see the _contortionist_ crawl out of a safety deposit box in the wall of an
_underground Swiss bank vault_, legs wrapped round his _waist_, feet resting on _his lumbar spine_,
and landing on hands and displaced knees on the tiled floor? You did? Right, then. Tcch.

[A man and woman face each other across a dimly lit bar counter, talking]
Each episode plays out over the three timelines, so we gradually see how the audacious robbery was
put together, how it went down and how, in the here and now, those 12 days to escape are panning
out. The short answers are: very well, thank you; could have been better, and are you really still
pretending that's a thing?

The longer answers? Well, the heist around which the series revolves was masterminded by Pauly
Galvin (Dornan), son of a New York ne'er do well, brother of Bobby (Jack Kesy) and fiance of a
joyfully pregnant Severine (Agathe Rousselle), with whom he had started a new and crime-free life in
Marseille. Just when you think you're out, of course, is when they pull you back in and Bobby duly
reveals that his gambling debts have placed his own, Pauly's, Severine's and the foetal Galvin's
life in danger and that the baddies to whom he is in hock require Pauly's legendary robbery-planning
services. This is why you should never become a recidivist if you have siblings. Your life is not
your own.

So Pauly reluctantly Hannibal Smiths it and gradually a plan and a team come together. It includes
Severine's father Serge (José Garcia) because Pauly needs a tunnel and Serge is – to give you the
script's full description – "a tunneller".

The 12-hour timeline is the most fun one. The contortionist, an unaccounted for mercury tilt switch
in the floor of the vault, a big underground drill, a diverted subway train for the literal ton of
cash, an Alpine retreat and jet-skis aplenty – none of the suavity of Thomas Crown's affairs
perhaps, but just as much satisfaction.

[Anthony Mackie, in a light blue shirt, aims a handgun in an underground station]
Dominating the 12-hour "Takedown" timeline is Mackie as grieving, embittered, self-loathing FBI
agent Jack Strickland, so called because he is stricken by his part in the death of a fellow agent
when they attempted to foil the Galvin brothers' last robbery together, in Newark. Now he is in
Zurich following up a tip-off that they might be about to strike again, and even the Swiss consider
him to be no fun at parties.

Unlike the complacent native force, Strickland has US street smarts and is soon seeing clues they
have all missed and is closing in on his prey. This starts immediately, which makes even more of a
mockery of the supposed premise, which surely must at least be that – like the "golden hour" after
a murder – you have so long to gather leads before the trail goes cold. Not 12 days/one hour to
put handcuffs on your guy. But again, we remind ourselves of the contortionist and adjust our
suspension of disbelief accordingly.

12 12 12 is fantastically slick, and fantastically well-shot and structured, but once the novelty of
the triple timelines wears off there is the inescapable feeling that that is where all the
inspiration went. Everything else – the troubled cop, the bad guy trying to go straight, the
troublesome brother, the endangered new family, the gang fracturing under pressure – has been done
many times before. It's great fun. But is it 12/12 fun? No. It is three out of five.
