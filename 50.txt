I Helped Launch Salon.com 31 Years Ago. Now It’s Dead. It’s the End of an Era.

====================================================================================================

Thirty-one years ago, I sat in the conference room of a San Francisco architectural firm that was so
down on its luck it had to rent out part of its office space to a bunch of giddy people doing
something unfathomable: publishing a magazine on the World Wide Web. Back then, in 1995, only a
couple of precedents even existed (Feed and Word, you are not forgotten!), none of them a
full-fledged news organization employing a staff of professional journalists. My new co-workers were
newspaper veterans, led by our editor in chief, David Talbot, and until recently employed by the
city’s now extinct evening newspaper. They knew what they were doing—except they knew how to do
it in a very different medium. I sat there, sketching grids on a legal pad, trying to figure out how
this new type of publication would work.

Our “magazine,” as we then thought of it, was Salon.com, an organization that has just
announced its closure after more than three decades of publishing reporting, commentary, and
criticism. At the beginning, we were none of us technological visionaries, but I at least had been
online for a while, since even before the web itself existed. I had email, which only about half the
people I knew could claim, including the most internet-savvy among us, Scott Rosenberg. If you
emailed David, however, you’d have to walk across the office to nag him into looking at it.
Otherwise, it might be days before it occurred to him to check.

At first, still thinking of ourselves as making a magazine, we uploaded full “issues” of a dozen
or so stories over the office modem on Friday nights. This took so long we often didn’t leave
until 11 p.m., making sure to check all the pages first, as if we were proofing a print
publication. We were print people, gradually adjusting to a new way of publishing, and the notion of
a website that would be continually updated throughout the day was unimaginable to us. An early
critic compared Salon to an old-fashioned airship, richly appointed and comfortable but more
slow-moving than an actual plane. As my colleague Gary Kamiya put it, this wasn’t as insulting as
it was intended to be. To someone who cared most about great writing, Salon was a paradise.

Over time, a model for publishing online journalism emerged, one very different from the version I
had tried to map out on that legal pad in 1995. Salon adapted to this model, which traded page views
for display-ad dollars, along with every other web publication, but for a while, during those first
few years, nobody knew what worked or even how to define what it meant for a web publication to
“work,” and so we could do whatever we wanted. At first, there wasn’t even a reliable way to
gauge how much traffic a particular piece got. Investors—a group that included Adobe, Apple, and
Borders—were footing the bills while we experimented.

We published a collection of stories about death (now impossible to find in its entirety,
but here is a standout piece from it, by Susie Bright), for example, just about the most
advertiser-unfriendly topic imaginable. We broke some news and even had to hire security to guard
our offices after publishing a story about the infidelities of the Republican congressman who was
leading the moral charge against Bill Clinton during the Monica Lewinsky scandal. I moved to New
York to help open the office there, and remember rushing to work on the morning of 9/11 to search
for a counterterrorism expert to interview about what I had just seen from the sidewalk in front
of my apartment. That was the first time I heard the name Osama bin Laden.

I was a culture editor, however, so most of the news and politics coverage, with its attendant
dramas and controversies (and there were a _lot_ of those), wasn’t my immediate concern.
Instead, I was thrilled to publish literary idols like Denis Johnson, Mary Gaitskill, and David
Foster Wallace, to find new voices to champion like Colson Whitehead, and to scoop up pitches from
my inbox from writers I’d never heard of before but who filed stories that were immensely
satisfying to bring to the world. Along the way, I got to edit book reviews with Dwight Garner, and
work with colleagues who included Jake Tapper, Rebecca Traister, Dave Eggers, Steve Kornacki,
Michelle Goldberg, Joan Walsh, Sarah Vowell, and so many more.

THE SAME TECHNOLOGICAL CHANGE THAT ONCE MADE IT POSSIBLE FOR A BUNCH OF JOURNALISTS TO FINALLY
PUBLISH THE KIND OF MAGAZINE THEY’D ALWAYS WANTED HAS ENDED UP CANNIBALIZING ALL THAT CREATIVITY
ANDINDUSTRY.
Although I left Salon a decade ago, I’ve never stopped rooting for it. Its fortunes have waned
over the years, and in 2023 it was sold to a performance-marketing company, an all-too-common fate
for many of the publications that once flourished online. I’m told that what finally finished off
the site was the introduction of A.I. into web search, leading to a steep decline in traffic coming
from Google; in Salon’s closing announcement, the unnamed author wrote, “Our publishing model is
no longer sustainable.” There’s a bitter irony to the fact that the same technological change
that once made it possible for a bunch of journalists to finally, finally publish the kind of
magazine they’d always wanted has ended up cannibalizing all that creativity and industry,
destroying its future, and making it much harder for younger writers to have a shot at the same
experience. As one former colleague remarked to me, “The dream of the ’90s internet is well and
truly kaput.”

I was ridiculously lucky to get in at the beginning of all that, and to this day, the first few
years of Salon remain the most fun I have ever had. We were so deep in the making of it that its
cultural impact will probably never be fully visible to me. We were grateful for all our readers but
unaware of what we meant to most of them. Others will have to attest to that. People sometimes tell
me about reading Salon avidly in their youth, how it felt like connecting to something exhilarating
and cool and a bit crazy in the strange new expanses of the internet. All I can say is: That’s
exactly how it felt to work there.
