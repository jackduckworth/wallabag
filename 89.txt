The Nvidia Scandal Is Getting Worse and Worse—for Trump

====================================================================================================

_This story is adapted from the Slatest newsletter. You can_ _sign up here_ _to get it in your inbox
every day._

It’s not every day that one of the president’s Cabinet secretaries goes on national television
to blame one of his colleagues for a ballooning public scandal. Welcome to Donald Trump’s White
House in the aftermath of Jensen Huang’s downfall.

To refresh: Huang, the longtime CEO of the tech behemoth Nvidia, resigned yesterday after
revelations that he’d had multiple extramarital affairs—including one with a suspected Chinese
spy. Huang’s implosion is a problem for the administration because he’s a prominent Trump ally
whose indiscretions appear to have let Beijing get its hands on Nvidia’s advanced semiconductor
chips, boosting America’s chief rival in the race to build powerful artificial intelligence
systems. Now the finger-pointing inside the administration over who’s to blame is spilling into
public view. Let’s get into it.

I SEEM TO REMEMBER YOU WRITING ABOUT THIS ALREADY. WHAT’S CHANGED? 

Good memory. Besides Huang’s resignation, the biggest new development has been the
administration’s increasingly frantic scramble to find a fall guy. On Wednesday, Axios reported
that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had confronted Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick last week
over the latter’s failure to block Nvidia from selling to Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company
that Huang’s paramour reportedly used to funnel chips to China. (Lutnick’s department is
supposed to ensure that overseas companies getting Nvidia chips aren’t a threat to U.S. national
security.) Bessent and other top Trump officials believe Lutnick repeatedly stonewalled their
efforts to cut off Megaspeed, including before the Huang scandal broke. Why Lutnick would do that
isn’t totally clear, but it may have something to do with the millions of dollars’ worth of
Nvidia stock that his sons’ company recently jettisoned. On Friday, Bessent took those tensions
public. “Commerce,” he said when asked about the scandal on CNBC, “has the pen, and you will
have to ask them about their internal processes.” Now even some congressional Republicans are
demanding that Lutnick testify about what went wrong, and the president is reportedly considering
firing him.

ANYTHING ELSE I SHOULD KNOW ABOUT LUTNICK?  

This isn’t the first time he has caused trouble for the administration. As the Atlantic reported
last year, Lutnick has publicly misrepresented trade deals, complicated negotiations with U.S.
allies, and clashed with other Trump aides. Nor is l’affaire Huang the extent of the commerce
secretary’s apparent missteps over Nvidia chips. According to the New York Times, Lutnick’s
department has permitted chip sales to other suspicious firms with ties to China’s government,
too. Through it all, though, Lutnick’s long friendship with the president has been enough to save
him.

SO FAR I’M HEARING SOME NOT-GREAT THINGS ABOUT ONE OF TRUMP’S DEPUTIES. BUT HOW DOES THE
PRESIDENT HIMSELF FIGURE INTO THIS?

At the risk of paraphrasing a different presidential scandal, one major outstanding question is what
Trump knew and when he knew it. Trump, who counts Huang as an ally and reportedly urged him this
week to ride out the scandal, has claimed he didn’t know much about any Chinese espionage
targeting the former CEO. But as the Times has reported, top U.S. officials first told Trump about
it last year—after which he failed to stop Nvidia from selling chips to Megaspeed and appears not
to have told Huang that he’d been consorting with a spy.

SO WHAT SHOULD I MAKE OF ALL THIS??

For all his underlings’ attempts to assign blame, the reality is that the president owns this mess
more than anyone. It obviously isn’t Trump’s fault that Huang decided to cheat on his wife, nor
that he apparently did so with a foreign agent. But Trump has repeatedly argued that the
U.S.–China battle for A.I. supremacy is existential—even as his inattention appears to have let
China continue to access sensitive technology for months. And even if Lutnick did engage in the
stalling tactics Bessent alleges, a president with his finger on the pulse and in full command of
his administration should have been able to stop him—and would certainly have punished him for
endangering U.S. national security by now. As it is, this president seems more interested in the
I-beams going into his ballroom.
