

====================================================================================================

The Altruists review – two of TV's best actors light up this chaotic crypto tale

====================================================================================================

What happens when you put really stupid clever people in charge of the world? Not as presidents
(they currently trend mostly towards the merely stupid) or prime ministers (generally neither really
stupid nor really clever), but in charge of the real world, which is to say the online world, the
one that really governs us? We are watching that play out day by day, but if you have enough slack
left in your dread-system, a cortisol spike yet to execute, why not add a warning from history to
your consumption of horrors?

Welcome to The Altruists, a breakneck breakdown of the brief career of Sam Bankman-Fried (played by
Anthony Boyle) who, with his on-off girlfriend Caroline Ellison (played by Julia Garner), set up the
cryptocurrency exchange FTX with the belief that it would revolutionise finance and – as the
increasingly absurd/dishonest/knowingly satirical Silicon Valley mantra has it – make the world a
better place. A very few years later, FTX – worth $36bn (£27bn) at its peak – collapsed and in
2023 the pair were convicted of various charges deriving from what the trial judge called "the
greatest financial fraud ever perpetrated in this country". Ellison was sentenced to two years in
prison after testifying against Bankman-Fried. Bankman-Fried is serving 25 years and was ordered to
forfeit $11bn (£8.3bn). He says he is innocent and is applying to the supreme court to review his
conviction.

[Caroline Ellison stands beside Sam Bankman-Fried as he speaks indoors]
The Altruists opens in 2016 with an untraditional meet-cute between SBF (as Bankman-Fried was known)
and Ellison when she joins the trading firm he works at. They bond over mathematical strategies,
their Ivy League professor parentage, veganism and their commitment to effective altruism. The last
was a movement that invited people to apply utilitarian principles – where could your money do
most practical good – and divorce charitable donation decisions from emotion and irrationality.
Thus it followed that the best thing you could do was make as much money as possible and then give
it all away. Many in the tech industry approved this message, whether through genuine belief or as a
sop to conscience. It is surely a phenomenon that deserves a thorough examination of its own – the
mix of good intentions, self-righteousness, skewed entitlement, scope for monstrous egos, misuse and
hypocrisy it combines is an almost overwhelmingly delicious prospect and I hope someone has it in
their sights.

Our so far relatively endearing pair of number monkeys begin a relationship – almost entirely on
Caroline's polyamorous terms – while SBF co-founds his first company, Alameda, in 2017, from which
he then forces out his business partner. Caroline comes on board instead and together they start
making profits.

The timeline then starts to split between the rise (2016 in New York), the precarious peaking (2018,
Hong Kong) and the fall (the Bahamas, 2022). Not for the first time I find myself thanking the Mike
Judge sitcom Silicon Valley for giving me at least a small purchase on what the hell is happening,
and wishing that a drama could follow its lead in integrating explanations for the minimally
computer- and fiscally literate lay audience seamlessly into the action so we can properly
appreciate what is going on.

Because – it's a lot. At the heart of the downfall is an $8bn (£6bn) hole in the Alameda/FTX
finances, whose funds should never have been mixed, where customer deposits should be. Before then,
there are technical challenges, clever plays by more experienced (and more corrupt) players,
risk-averse investors and regulatory issues to deal with. This could be simplified without too much
loss to the drama, because the meat of that is really to do with the messy humanity that SBF and
Ellison – and, by inference, the rest of the tech titans – are trying to banish from the trades
and the models they think could be perfect.

[Constance Wang stands with crossed arms beside a seated Nishad Singh near computer screens]
At its best, The Altruists is about the ineffable; it's about trust, about understanding yourself
and others, about being able to gauge moods as well as markets and how inextricably and
infuriatingly they can be linked. Above all, it is about how important self-image and a belief in
that self-image can be, and how much people can lie to themselves and others to protect it. SBF
recruits his friends and betrays them. The pair donate huge amounts to charity but live bounteously
in the Bahamas and are planning to build the $200m (£151m) complex FTX "needs" as bankruptcy looms.

Anchored by brilliant performances from Boyle and Garner, two of the best young screen actors
working today, and supported by fine turns elsewhere (especially from Karan Soni as the most
emotionally brutalised of SBF's victims), The Altruists stays just the right side of coherent. In
the end, it manages to evoke the chaos, the insubstantiality of the algorithmic economy we all
depend on now, and the insanity of the idea that any handful of people in whom such vast amounts of
wealth and power are concentrated could ever be up to the task, without quite itself collapsing. But
it's a high-wire act and you mustn't lose focus for a moment. And I recommend a Silicon Valley
rewatch to prep before you begin.
