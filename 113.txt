

====================================================================================================

England and Ireland provide champagne rugby for north to tee up finals weekend | Robert Kitson

====================================================================================================

Even if the official November Test window had now closed, as used to be the case, this would already
have been a vintage European autumn. Whatever still awaits on the inaugural Nations Championship
finals weekend, it has been a richly enjoyable few weeks for both diehard fans and floating voters,
topped off by more seismic results and overflowing emotions in London and Dublin on Saturday.

Those flocking to Twickenham had their money's worth even before the game started, as Lewis Moody,
fighting the good fight against motor neurone disease, stood shoulder to shoulder with England's
players as they faced up to the All Blacks' haka. A record-breaking 37-12 home win was then swiftly
followed by Ireland defeating the world champion Springboks 20-17 to complete another resounding
northern hemisphere double whammy.

Worryingly for the wounded egos of New Zealand and South Africa, there could be worse to come. Yes,
the Boks are into Sunday's grand final as the best of a chastened southern-hemisphere bunch but,
based on the last two weekends, France will be quietly fancying their chances against opponents who
seem to have stalled tactically. And, as with certain Australia cricketers when the word "sandpaper"
crops up, don't believe an All Black who claims to be itching to be back in the ring with England
this Saturday.

Taken as a whole – along with Wales's first win over New Zealand since 1953 – it amounts to a
significant psychological shift with the 2027 Rugby World Cup barely 10 months away. Maybe the
Greatest Rivalry series between the Springboks and the All Blacks sapped the energy of both sides
more than they appreciated. Equally, though, there is growing evidence that the north-south pendulum
is starting to swing.

Take England. As recently as March they were trailing in fifth behind Italy, to whom they lost in
Rome, having already been beaten 42-21 at home by the Irish. In July they were 17-0 down in Ellis
Park inside 12 minutes and eventually went down 45-21. Most frustrating of all for their regular
watchers was the sense of stymied promise: a good side was hiding in there somewhere, if only it
could consistently uncork the best of itself.

[Lewis Moody stands with England to face the All Blacks' haka]
At last the champagne rugby is properly starting to flow. England have removed their safety goggles
and sought to show their true selves, even in the absence of their two most lethal try-scorers,
Manny Feyi-Waboso and Tommy Freeman. Sometimes a dab of extra belief and ownership is all it takes
and the return of important cogs such as Maro Itoje and George Furbank has also helped to foster
greater assurance and cohesion on both sides of the ball.

The difference it has made for someone such as Henry Slade, finally encouraged to express the
footballing talent he has always had, and the fast-emerging Seb Atkinson has been evident for
everyone to see. So, too, the fierce desire of Ollie Chessum and Ben Earl, among others, to make up
for lost time. Earl's 33 carries on the weekend, including a storming try off the back of a scrum
past a static All Black defence, summed up a stunningly one-sided contest.

Ireland were also full value for their best win since the 2023 World Cup, a result built on grit,
guile and two tries for their gathering threat Rob Baloucoune. South Africa's disappointment was
further reinforced by the forlorn sight of their banned head coach, Rassie Erasmus, sitting in a
deserted room delivering his online "watchalong" broadcast – Goggle-Boks, anyone? – with the
sound mostly turned down. Coincidentally or not, it neatly reflected his side's equally
underwhelming effort.

The opportunity still exists, of course, for the southern sons to rise again. Erasmus will be
reminding his players that an ability to win big games is deep in their DNA, while France have a
lengthy injury list. Much can change inside a week, never mind between now and 1 October when the
World Cup kicks off. The defending champions, currently preparing for their 13th Test since July,
are unlikely to be running on empty then.

[Robert Baloucoune attacks against South Africa despite the attention of two defenders]
Even so, there is no question that South Africa, New Zealand and the hosts, Australia, have all had
an abrupt wake-up call this month. In seeking to bully teams up front the Boks have lost sight of a
timeless truth: one-dimensional rugby is fine until it becomes predictable. As well as assessing
whether some of their ageing pack leaders are slowing down, they need to show more faith in the kind
of all‑court game that England are now pursuing.

New Zealand's Dave Rennie also has some huge calls to make regarding some of his faltering
stalwarts. Rarely has the value of a fit, rejuvenated Ardie Savea been more apparent at the weekend
but Saturday's defeat will do little to dispel fears that the fundamental pillars upon which All
Black dominance has long been based are starting to wobble. Fail to live up to their traditions
again this Saturday, having already slipped to fifth in the world rankings, and it will no longer be
possible to blame everything on whichever poor head coach is in post.

All of which tees up finals weekend at Twickenham beautifully. Let's say, for argument's sake, that
Italy beat Fiji, Andy Farrell's resurgent Ireland defeat Australia, Wales see off Japan and England
intensify New Zealand's current pain. Under the new arrangements that would leave the north with an
unassailable 4-0 lead in the Ryder Cup-style Hemisphere Challenge before the final days' pair of
concluding fixtures. Even if the wounded Springboks can elude the gathering French-speaking hyenas
this Sunday, their pursuers are catching up fast.
