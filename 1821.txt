Welcome to Ozball, the low-key story of a low-key England summer | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

Each age makes its own heroes. Two hours from the end of the final day of the second-last Test at
the fag‑end of the post‑Bazball summer, the cheer from the Lord's crowd at deep fine leg had
something pointed about it, a crackle of event glamour.

Ollie Robinson turned as he ambled back and raised an arm in gladiatorial salute. "Oh Ollie Ollie,
Ollie Ollie Ollie Ollie Robinson" was the response from a scattered, woozy swell, again with
something arch and transgressive in it, the smell of anti-hero. _What are you rebelling against,
Johnny? What have you got?_ Also, if you really want to know, buzzword culture and enforced fitness
regimes. Sometimes, you just get the heroes you need.

Moments earlier, with the Lord's clock at 3.59pm, Robinson had struck Shan Masood on the front pad
and stood with his arms out, bellowing plaintively, the appeal of a man who really, really wants to
get back for Call the Midwife and a peri-peri chicken throb-box meal deal.

From the start this had been a Test that was less about what was going to happen, more just the
question of when. Still, there was drama here, and confirmation of an extraordinary turnaround this
summer for the bad boy of metronomic medium-nip.

Robinson took four of the nine wickets needed to complete England's 2-0 series win
45 minutes before the close. In doing so he took his own tally to 23 at eight across the two home
Test series. Here it is then, the new age, replacement, in double-quick time, for the previous new
age. Welcome to the summer of Ozball.

Robinson was irresistible again en route to his four for 24, rumbling in from the Nursery End,
bulky and quietly menacing, like a wardrobe rolling gently down a hill. It is all so simple in
outline. The easy, high action, the perfect seam release, head up, already feeling the pitch in his
fingertips, seeking out that zinging, fizzing contact. Stump to stump, every millimetre of movement
in play. Simple. You try doing it.

As we enter the new age, perhaps we need a new kind of absurd absolutism, a world where the managing
director of England cricket makes a passionate speech about needing a battery of slow-medium-nip
bowlers, or thumps his palm and declares that _not_ doodling it in with the keeper up to the stumps
has absolutely no place in Test cricket.

The one shame here was that Robinson ended on 99 Test wickets, taking them now at the
astonishing average of 19. He really does deserve a nickname by this stage. The Butcher of Margate.
The Sussex Strangler. Perhaps a variation on the great fast‑bowling monikers. White Drizzle.
Whispering Chronic Illness. The Hove Stopping Train. Pace Like Air Conditioning.

Plus, once Robinson gets to that hundred-wicket mark he will be officially the greatest Test bowler
since the first world war. Kind of. OK, not really. But the numbers are still mind-boggling.

The main case for Robinson-as-GOAT is simple enough. No pace bowler since the 19th century has ended
their career with 100 wickets at a lower average. And yes, others have got to that mark at a
similar rate, but Robinson is 32, he's not got to have any real decline. This is him.

Either way he can now claim to be an all-time Test great, and nobody can really argue, or why even
bother with the numbers? Odd to think Robinson was dropped a few games back because Rob Key likes
pace, along with some not‑being‑the‑right-kind-of-guy stuff. He's very good. Get the best out
of him. That's the job.

Here Robinson took the new ball in the fourth innings, and just kept on looming up to the stumps
like a weather front barreling in off the Ramsgate coast. There was an amusingly forthright send-off
for Abdullah Shafique, knocked over by an unplayable nipp‑backer, the kind of delivery where the
batter's shape makes the moment, turned around, playing for logic, the actual line, and getting
instead magic, weirdness, parallax error.

At that point Robinson had six for 13 in the match, and you thought, well, why not 13 for 13? Would
Pakistan actually score another run here? In the end a total of 194 was built around a splendid 97
from Saud Shakeel, who played beautifully, all swivel‑pulls and courtly, kneeling cover drives,
the kind of shot that brings a digestive mid-afternoon gurgle from the crowd.

Otherwise it was another slow-burn day in a slow-burn summer, a day for asking, what does it mean,
why this, why here? Lord's on an autumn Sunday will do this to you. The entire Lord's industrial
complex is full of places that feel like jarring tautologies. Lord's Museum. Lord's Gin Bar. Lord's
Picnic Garden. It's fine. You can just say "Lord's". What else is it but a gin-soaked picnic museum?
You might as well make a sign saying welcome to the Lord's red-trousered men assembly point, the
Lord's cultural ossification hub.

There is an issue here with the dominance of bowling, and above all a certain kind of bowling.
Earlier in the day Mohammad Abbas could be seen kneeling at the edge of the square and kissing the
reddish scrub of the Lord's outfield, a beautiful spectacle, a man giving thanks to the god of crap
Test pitches, in praise of shit-tip Jehovah, spirit guide of his own remarkable Test figures.

This is obviously unfair. Abbas is a brilliant bowler and a lovely guy. He's on the honours board.
He gets his wickets at 22, and in the best way, all brain, craft and skill. It is a method that has
become very now. Look up the top nine lowest career bowling averages since the end of the 19th
century and four of those nine have appeared in the past six years.

These are: Robinson, Kyle Jamieson, Scott Boland and Axar Patel. All brilliant bowlers. All with the
same basic method: fizzed straight stuff to Test batters who can't deal with it. Look back over the
summer of Ozball, and Abbas, Robinson and Matt Henry together have 45 wickets at 12, keeper stood
up, front pads blowing up, crowd gripped by the spectacle of low-level apocalypse.

Test cricket should be a bowler's game. It is even more this with data analysis, raised seams and
format confusion for batting techniques. As Ian Chappell said recently on the Grade Cricketer, there
is very little financial incentive to become a great Test batsman now, no way we're ever getting
another Graham Gooch spending three winter months just working out how to defend his front pad.

Maybe Bazball was right. Maybe we just need flat, quick pitches you can score on. Otherwise it's
Ozball, the age of high-craft jag and nip, and Test matches that become a race to the end, in more
ways than one.

Not that anyone minded much here, on a day where that Robinson redemption arc was confirmed as the
low-key story of a low-key summer.
