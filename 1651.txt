Will Ferrell’s New Netflix Show Is Not Good

_THIS ARTICLE CONTAINS SPOILERS FOR_ THE HAWK_._

Early on in _The Hawk_, Netflix’s new sports comedy set in the world of professional golf, Will
Ferrell’s and Molly Shannon’s characters share a moment. Ferrell, one of the century’s most
renowned comics, plays Lonnie “the Hawk” Hawkins, a washed-up PGA Tour pro trying to get back on
top. Shannon, an Emmy-nominated, SAG Award–winning powerhouse, plays Ferrell’s estranged wife,
Stacy.

“You haven’t been on the tour in seven years, you dickless cunt,” Stacy tells Ferrell’s
character.

“By definition, a cunt _is_ dickless,” Lonnie counters.

This display of razor-sharp wit is among the most original bits of dialogue in the show’s first
season, which came out on Thursday. Nearly everything else about _The Hawk_ feels lifted from other
golf shows and movies of the past year. That would be one thing if those productions were good on
their own, but given that they were not, _The Hawk_ is difficult to watch unless you are fully in
the tank for anything Ferrell makes.

I am a golf obsessive, and I try to bring an open mind when viewing star-powered sports sitcoms that
don’t look good on paper. I didn’t much care for _Stick_, the Apple TV golf show that depicted
the sport in an uninteresting way but at least had some grit and humor. I didn’t like the _Happy
Gilmore_ sequel, even as I acknowledged the brilliance of the original. These things both had a lot
more to them than _The Hawk_ does. Ferrell’s new show, made with old collaborators Harper Steele
and Chris Henchy, apes these two already-not-that-great works of art to create one that’s even
worse. It is so bad that I think Hollywood’s streamers should adopt at least a one-year moratorium
on golf programming in penance.

In fairness, this is not an easy sport to translate to the screen. It’s even a problem for _live_
golf, with dozens of players on the course at the same time and only so many pixels to work with. In
movies and television, the task grows more challenging. Golf is an acquired taste, moving at a
slower pace than any other game in the world. (Last week, I played a five-hour round, hitting the
ball exactly 85 times, for a total of roughly 220 seconds of swing-related activity.) Making the
game interesting across a two-hour movie or 10-episode series like this one takes not just a
creative mind but a commitment to a lane. You can send a disruptive character into a world full of
straight men, as was Adam Sandler’s job in the first _Happy Gilmore_. You can offer a cutting
portrayal of country clubs with a twist of gambling drama, as with _Caddyshack_. You can even trot
out a rom-com that blends golf and true love, in the style of _Tin Cup_.

The point is, you have to try something. The past few years have brought a bountiful harvest of golf
shows and movies, but they have not been ambitious. Golf became more popular in the wake of COVID,
and that growth convinced business minds and producers that there should be more golf content.
YouTubers have had a lot of success doing that, but Hollywood’s cracks at it have been
disappointing.

Netflix’s _Full Swing_ docuseries, which has had four seasons since 2023, is a PGA
Tour–affiliated answer to _Drive to Survive_, the British series that jolted Formula 1’s
domestic popularity. The former is perfectly fine but didn’t make a cultural ripple outside golf,
because golf is very slow instead of very fast. (Also, golfers hesitate to say anything interesting
on camera, and only a few people submit to being filmed during truly vulnerable moments.) Apple’s
_Stick_, meanwhile,tried to find its niche in character development: Owen Wilson plays a
down-on-his-luck former star who reengages with the sport through his tutelage of a prodigy. Would
you ever believe that Owen lost a young son, or that the up-and-coming youngster’s dad happens to
be a deadbeat? Marc Maron is fun in this one, at least.

Then there was _Happy Gilmore 2_, whicharrived last summer without bothering with any pretense of a
plot. Its aim was to load as many big-ticket cameos and skits as possible into a couple of
hours—and, perhaps, to help Sandler land his role as the officiant at Taylor Swift’s wedding. If
a shirtless Travis Kelce being attacked by a bear didn’t do it for you, the movie had little else.
The genius of the first _Happy_, two decades ago,was that it sent up a golf culture that was ripe
for it. The sequel wanted not to lampoon that culture but to squeeze out guest stars from it.

_The Hawk_ is a rip-off of these unspectacular efforts. Ferrell’s Lonnie is a boorish dickhead who
is one major championship shy of golf’s career grand slam. Get this: He drinks too much and has a
lacking relationship with his son, Lance (Jimmy Tatro, of the great _American Vandal_). Lonnie’s
wife is upset with him over his credit card debt and lack of seriousness about their family, a
classic embodiment of recent golf programming’s hallmark divorced or dead wife. The cast’s most
prominent Black character is Ferrell’s caddie (Jimmy Walker Jr.), whose job is to die right away
to give Ferrell’s character an occasion not to show any introspection. He’s essentially a
stand-in for Carl Weathers’ Chubbs in the original _Happy Gilmore_, the mentor who falls out a
window and dies after Sandler surprises him with a gator head.

Ferrell’s character travels around in an RV, just the same as Wilson’s character in _Stick_. He
meets his new caddie, Sam (the talented stand-up Fortune Feimster), in a Walmart parking lot, and
Sam’s lack of golf knowledge becomes a wrinkle in the story, quite similar to a plotline in
_Stick_. There’s even a Wilson brother: Luke shows up as the golf golden boy whom Ferrell’s
character desperately wants to beat. Nothing feels original.

I imagine that when this show was early in its development, someone thought it would be interesting.
Pro golf is _easy_ to lampoon if someone is motivated. Roughly half of the sport’s biggest names
left the PGA Tour in the early 2020s to play on a morally and soon-maybe-literally bankrupt tour
funded by the despotic Saudi Arabian government. The players who remained on the PGA Tour are, for
the most part, uninteresting figures who demanded to be paid more money after the Saudis distorted
the talent market by barreling in as an irrational nation-state. At its best, golf is a little bit
too stuck-up, with plenty of targets yearning to be hit. And Ferrell is no stranger to this lane: He
did _Talladega Nights_, a mean-yet-loving send-up of NASCAR, in 2006. He knows there’s hay to be
made by taking a real whack at a sport with an insular culture. For some reason, he doesn’t do it
here.

Sometimes, _The Hawk_ headfakes like it will get into that kind of fun, critical mode. Lance is a
star player in the prime of his PGA Tour career, whose fiancée (the singer Katelyn Tarver) is a
controlling wellness influencer who scans as a fun poke at golf’s “WAG” culture. Tatro’s and
Ferrell’s characters do briefly talk about LIV Golf, the Saudi tour, in a way that makes you
_think_ more is coming. (It is not.) Tatro’s character is also addicted to sports betting, an arc
that’s at least timely. He’s _also_ cheating on his partner, something that does not feel out of
place in this sport. But in the fourth episode, the bigwigs at the PGA Tour plot out ways they might
obstruct Lonnie’s path back to the tour, on the grounds that his fans are “beer-drinking idiots
in their cutoff jeans and flip-flops.” This doesn’t land quite right, because the real-life PGA
Tour now tries hard to court loud, drunk fans whenever possible. (If you are not familiar with the
Phoenix Open, aka “the Waste Management,” read up here.) Also, the tour board member with a
grudge against Ferrell’s character is just angry that he slept with his wife.

With 10 episodes to unfold, _The Hawk_ is occasionally creative enough to be a little fun. Its
closing twist, set at Pebble Beach, is a nice metaphor for the mental challenges golf poses. But the
series offers nothing that feels risky in any way, and it treads no ground that other underwhelming
golf shows and movies have not covered recently. Ramy Youssef was attached to this project until
about a year ago, when he left over reported creative differences with the team behind it.
Unfortunately, Youssef made a wise choice, but I’d welcome it if he wanted to take his own crack
at a golf comedy in the future.

Please just wait a spell, though. Golf needs a cooldown period before Hollywood gives it another
shot. The sport has had an embarrassing few years, catalyzed by a farcical desire among overpaid
tour players to “grow the game” by taking Saudi cash to play in obscure tournaments in places
such as Riyadh and Westfield, Indiana. In golf’s gold rush, an excess of people with business
degrees have gotten the idea that the masses are due to fall in love with the sport as soon as they
see how accessible it is. Operating under a weird desire to imbue golf with a sort of fake populism,
too many of these types (and too many film producers) have tried to emulsify the game into the same
unappealing paste. These attempts to translate the sport to the masses need to be shut down for a
while, at least until someone dreams up a more imaginative approach.
