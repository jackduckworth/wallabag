It’s One of the Most Beloved Horror Films of the 1980s, but There’s One Controversy About It That Will Never Die

====================================================================================================

_This article is part of_ _Spielberg Week__, Slate’s seven-day celebration of Steven Spielberg._

By the time I spoke to Tobe Hooper in 2000 about the questions surrounding who really directed
_Poltergeist_, it quickly became obvious that he was tired of talking about it. It’s not hard to
see why. _Poltergeist_, the story of a suburban family harassed by vengeful spirits,was the biggest
commercial hit of the director’s career, but the circumstances surrounding its creation attached
an asterisk next to the film’s place in his filmography even before the movie arrived in theaters.
Asking him to “set the record straight” created the only awkward moment in an otherwise pleasant
interview (to the extent that any discussion of films like _The Texas Chain Saw Massacre_ can be
called “pleasant”). By that point, Hooper had been pestered about it for 18 years. “I’ve
kind of talked that one to death, really,” he replied. “I’ve been asked that so many times
that I feel the record should be straight already.”

Yet even now, 44 years after the film’s release, the matter of which auteur deserves attribution
for _Poltergeist_ feels anything but settled, even though Hooper has always been listed as the
film’s director. In fact, the opening credits couldn’t be clearer, at least up to a point. They
read, on three successive screens, “Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Presents,” “A Tobe Hooper Film,” and
“Poltergeist.” It’s the fourth screen of credits, which appears nearly four minutes later,
that starts to complicate the issue, the one reading “A Steven Spielberg Production.” So who
really deserves credit, and why has this debate endured for nearly half a century?

In 1982, Spielberg was in the process of transforming into a brand. Thanks to _Jaws_, _Close
Encounters of the Third Kind_, and _Raiders of the Lost Ark_—a run of massively successful
blockbusters interrupted only by the disappointing World War II comedy _1941_—he’d become the
most famous director since Alfred Hitchcock, one whose name not only doubled as a mark of quality
but suggested a certain type of technically skilled and emotionally resonant filmmaking. It was
also, he’d discover, a name that meant something even when associated with films he did not
direct.

At the beginning of the 1980s_,_ Spielberg co-founded Amblin Productions with Kathleen Kennedy and
Frank Marshall. By the end of that decade, Spielberg’s name in the credits, with or without
Amblin, would become synonymous with movies that could be described as “Spielbergian”—_The
Goonies_, _Gremlins_, _Back to the Future_, _Batteries Not Included,_ to name a few—while still
retaining the personalities of the directors who made them. But in 1982, that understanding wasn’t
in place, nor was a sense, or maybe just a consideration, of how heavily Spielberg’s name might
overshadow all others on a movie poster.

On March 28, 1982, more than a month ahead of _Poltergeist_’s release, MGM ran a two-page ad in
the Los Angeles Timesfeaturing an American suburb beneath a dark sky that contained three text
paragraphs. The first, filled with dramatic ellipses, touted Spielberg’s achievements, stating he
had “delighted, mystified, and scared more audiences … more imaginatively … than any filmmaker
of our time.” Hooper goes unmentioned until the second paragraph, which includes him as one name
among many, reading:

> As producer and co-author of “Poltergeist,” Spielberg is joined by co-producer Frank Marshall,
> with whom he made “Raiders of the Lost Ark,” co-authors Michael Grais and Mark Victor,
> director Tobe Hooper, and the special effects wizardry of Oscar winner Richard Edlund (“Star
> Wars”) and Industrial Light and Magic.

This was unusual. Also unusual: a New York Times profile inspired by the near-simultaneous releases
of _Poltergeist_ and _E.T. The Extraterrestrial_ that didn’t mention Hooper at all, and a trailer
that describes _Poltergeist_ as a film in which “Steven Spielberg crosses a frightening new
threshold into a world within our own” and that features Spielberg’s name in letters appreciably
larger than those used for Hooper’s. This last detail prompted Hooper to complain to the Directors
Guild of America, which awarded him $15,000 on the grounds that the trailer “denigrated the role
of the director.”

Still, even with Hooper’s outreach to the DGA, all this might be conceivably chalked up to a
series of innocent mistakes, the sort of unintentional credit-grabbing Spielberg would learn to
avoid with future collaborations, were it not for a Dale Pollock–penned article that appeared in
the Los Angeles Times on May 24, 1982 headlined, “ ‘Poltergeist’: Whose Film Is It?” Per
Hooper, this is where the confusion about the film began. “The genesis of it,” Hooper told me,
as he told other interviewers over the years, “came from an article in The L.A. Times”:

> When we were shooting the practical location on the house, the first two weeks of filming were
> exterior, so I had second-unit shots that had to be picked up in the front of the house. I was in
> the back of the house shooting Robbie [actor Oliver Robins] and the tree, looking down at the
> burial of the little tweety bird, so Steven was picking those shots up for me. _The L.A. Times_
> arrived on the set and printed something like, “We don’t know who’s directing the
> picture.” The moment they got there, Steven was shooting the shot of the little race cars, and
> from there the damn thing blossomed on its own and started becoming its own legend.

That sounds reasonable, only it’s not what Pollock’s article describes. Instead, Pollock quotes
producer Frank Marshall as saying, “The creative force on this movie was Steven. … Tobe was the
director and was on the set every day. But Steven did the design for every story board … and he
was on the set every day except three days when he was in Hawaii with [George] Lucas.” Marshall
then goes on to describe a post-production process in which Hooper essentially did not participate,
including in supervising the final edit, the mixing, and composer Jerry Goldsmith’s score.

Per Pollock’s article, this seems to have been an extension, and amplification, of the dynamic on
the set. JoBeth Williams, who played Diane Freeling, the mother of the central family, describes
making the film as “a collaboration with Steven having the final say.” He was also apparently
quite hands-on. Years later, Williams would describe Spielberg wading into the water with her for a
late-film scene in which Diane finds herself surrounded by skeletons in an unfinished swimming pool,
saying, “If you get electrocuted, it’ll kill me, too.” (What Williams didn’t know, at the
time, though Spielberg must have: The film was using real skeletons obtained from a medical supply
company.) Zelda Rubinstein, who played the clairvoyant Tangina, was even more frank in a 2007
interview, saying simply, “I can tell you that Steven directed all six days I was there. I only
worked six days on the film and Steven was there. Tobe set up the shots and Steven made the
adjustments.” (Though theories about why Spielberg would ghost-direct the movie have varied,
citing everything from a noncompete clause in Spielberg’s _E.T._ contract to rumors of a
directors’ strike—the former of which is reportedly real, and the latter of which doesn’t make
much sense as a motive, as it would equally bar Hooper—Rubinstein’s own explanation went out on
a limb by suggesting that Hooper was under the influence of “some unacceptable chemical
agents.”)

Whatever the process—and for every quote from Rubinstein or Marshall, or from first assistant
cameraman John Leonetti, or special effects makeup artist Craig Reardon, saying Spielberg directed
it in all but name, there are others, including from most of the other members of the cast, that
give credit to Hooper—it’s impossible not to see Spielberg’s influence on the film, from the
clutter-filled Freeling home that serves as a companion piece to similar spots in _Close Encounters
of the Third Kind_ and _E.T._ to the editing rhythms of the film’s set pieces. Spielberg’s
presence is never stronger than the moment the Freeling family watches spirits descend their
suburban home’s central staircase with the signature look of wordless wonder Kevin B. Lee would
dub “The Spielberg Face” in his video essay of the same name.

Pollock’s Los Angeles Times article is accompanied by a photo of Spielberg pointing as he speaks
to _Poltergeist_ star Craig T. Nelson as Hooper stands beside him, holding a can of Coke. It’s
easy to conclude that the image pretty much sums up their working relationship and that perhaps
_Poltergeist_ should be considered a Spielberg film in all but name. Even Spielberg’s attempt to
settle the issue via an open letter to Hooper published in the Hollywood Reporter days before the
movie’s release feels a bit qualified thanks to phrasing like “Through the screenplay you
accepted a vision of this very intense movie from the start, and as the director, you delivered the
goods.” It reads less like high praise than a pat on the head.

It’s not that simple, however, and the same Spielberg Face moment also works as groundwork for a
counterargument. A creator doesn’t have to be Spielberg to draw influence from Spielberg. It’s
not like Spielberg needed to be present on, say, the set of _Stranger Things_ for the many
Spielbergian moments found throughout that series’ run. And, of course, even if he wasn’t
standing by his side, Hooper would have to be influenced by Spielberg, an era-defining artist who
was already one of the most powerful people in Hollywood in 1982.

What’s more, Spielberg was personally invested in the film, having commissioned a screenplay from
writers Michael Grais and Mark Victor that he then rewrote. “ ‘Poltergeist’ is what I fear and
‘E.T.’ is what I love,’” Spielberg told the New York Timesin the paper’s Hooper-less
profile. “It’s me when I was scaring my younger sisters half to death when we were growing
up.” It only makes sense that the film’s elements would bend Spielberg’s way. Even
Goldsmith’s score resembles the work of Spielberg’s usual collaborator, John Williams, in ways
not evident in the inventive composer’s other work.

And yet, for all the scares found in films like _Jaws_ and _Jurassic Park_, Spielberg has never made
a proper horror film, unless the pre-fame 1972 TV movie _Something Evil_ counts. He seems to have
needed Hooper to explore that genre with him, if not for him. Though Spielberg gets, and undoubtedly
deserves, the film’s story credit, Jacob Trussell’s 2023 book _Poltergeist_ describes a
Hooper-Spielberg collaborative process that goes beyond Spielberg handing the director a script and
marching orders.

The two first met after a screening of _The Texas Chain Saw Massacre_, and Spielberg later
approached Hooper about working on _Night Skies_, the story of aliens menacing a family that
Spielberg had conceived as a kind of evil twin to _Close Encounters_. Spielberg had commissioned a
_Night Skies_ script from John Sayles that he ultimately decided not to make. But he did farm one
element of it out to screenwriter Melissa Mathison to develop into its own film: a subplot involving
a friendly extraterrestrial that would become _E.T._ That left the family-under-attack scenario to
be recycled. Via conversations with Hooper that included talk of Robert Wise’s _The Haunting_, a
favorite of both directors, _Night Skies_ evolved into the supernatural horror movie _Night Time_
and then into _Poltergeist_.

Spielberg had good reason to turn to the Texas-born Hooper for the project. Released in 1974, _The
Texas Chain Saw Massacre_ is a justly revered horror masterpiece, one rarely equaled in intensity,
dread, and just plain ickiness. Hooper’s 1976 follow-up, _Eaten Alive_, proved less distinguished,
but he’d gotten past that to direct an eerie 1979 miniseries adaptation of Stephen King’s
_Salem’s Lot_ and then _The Funhouse_, a thoughtful, offbeat twist on the slasher movie released
in 1981. Whatever Spielberg’s role in the creation, production, and postproduction of
_Poltergeist_, it would doubtlessly have taken a much different form without Hooper’s
influence—if it was made at all.

Attempting to write Hooper out of _Poltergeist_ also does a disservice to the directorial skills
evident in Hooper’s other work, including his immediate _Poltergeist_ follow-ups, a trio of movies
made for B-movie specialists the Cannon Group. None match _Poltergeist_ or _The Texas Chain Saw
Massacre_, but each makes a case for Hooper as a gifted stylist with a puckish sense of humor all
his own.

Released in 1985, _Lifeforce_ became instantly infamous as the film with the naked space vampire,
and understandably so. As “Space Girl,” Mathilda May begins her conquest of Earth in the nude
and stays that way for much of the film. But the fact that _Lifeforce_, co-written by Don Jakoby and
_Alien_’s Dan O’Bannon, serves as a fun, visually impressive riff on campy ’50s sci-fi and the
apocalyptic British strain of the same genre, got overlooked amid the giggles. Also largely
overlooked: Hooper’s 1986 remake of the ’50s classic _Invaders From Mars_ (adapted from another
O’Bannon-Jakoby script), which doubles down on the original’s Freudian symbolism with the help
of first-rate effects by Stan Winston and John Dykstra. Released the same year, _The Texas Chainsaw
Massacre 2_ attracted more attention for its graphic violence than the way Hooper and writer L.M.
Kit Carson reinvented the original’s cannibal family as the ultimate product of the consumerist
’80s. “Tobe isn’t what you call a take-charge sort of guy,” Spielberg told the Los Angeles
Times in the same article Hooper saw as the source of all the confusion about his role in making
_Poltergeist_. But these films suggest otherwise. Given a chance to take charge, Hooper produced a
string of strange, memorable films that were distinctly his own.

So, to return to the central question: Who directed _Poltergeist_? No thorough accounting suggests
that, per the strictest definition of a director’s job, anyone but Hooper deserves the credit. But
the same accounts also suggest that Spielberg played a part in the making of the film that went well
beyond the bounds usually observed by producers and screenwriters. Nonetheless, Spielberg and Hooper
would work together again, on both _Amazing Stories_ (Hooper directed the series finale) and the
Spielberg-produced alien-abduction miniseries _Taken_. Hooper, who died in 2017 at the age of 74,
never stopped referring to Spielberg as a friend.

In the end, it seems like the question itself might be flawed. No matter how strong a stamp a
director might put on a film—and Spielberg’s stamp presses deeper than most directors’—a
film is a collaborative process, one born of the contributions of many. Maybe Spielberg hit the mark
in his open letter when he referred to the two having a “unique, creative relationship.” Maybe
it’s better to put discussion of who the film belongs to to rest. Regardless of who shaped a given
shot or influenced a particular performance, the product speaks for itself. We can wonder what
conflict rumbled inside it, or we can simply look at it from the outside, admire the result, and
remember that some things are possessed by more than one creative spirit.
