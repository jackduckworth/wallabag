He Made One of 2024’s Breakout Hits. His New HBO Series Is Another Magnum Opus.

====================================================================================================

I don’t envy Richard Gadd the task of following up on his smash hit show of 2024, _Baby Reindeer_.
Gadd’s debut, a drama/thriller that billed itself as a “true story” about a man’s
entanglement with a stalker, shot to the top of the Netflix charts unexpectedly and threw its
creator into the limelight in queasy ways when the woman on whom the show’s stalker was apparently
based revealed her identity and went on Piers Morgan’s show to address what she saw as an
unacceptable and public character assassination. The noise around the real-life origins of _Baby
Reindeer_ became louder than the noise about the series itself, and cemented the show as a
must-watch cultural phenomenon, one that has been rewarded with six Emmys. So a second series from
its creator and star Gadd carries both the weight of a need to prove that _Baby Reindeer_ wasn’t a
lone bolt of lightning and a need to reach escape velocity from the discourse about the muddy
ethical issues of making a show based on real life.

Now we have that second show: _Half Man_, which premieres on HBO on Thursday night. The six-part
drama is a chance for Gadd to show the world who he is as an artist and what his ongoing concerns
will be after the publicity maelstrom that surrounded his first show. Like _Baby Reindeer_, _Half
Man_ is a very well-made and well-written series that I am glad I saw and never want to see again.

_Half Man_ tells the story of two brothers—although not actually related—called Ruben (Gadd) and
Niall (Jamie Bell, of _Billy Elliot_ fame), following them from their late teens until roughly their
40s. Every episode begins and ends at Niall’s wedding in the present day. Ruben has suddenly
turned up, determined to engineer a showdown of some kind with his brother. In between these scenes,
we learn about the events that led up to this day. Ruben and Niall grew up in Scotland, where they
found themselves sharing a bedroom in their late teens after their mothers begin a relationship and
Ruben gets out of a young-offender institute. Ruben was serving time for an act of violence, the
first in a series of incredibly brutal crimes that he will go on to commit in the show. Niall, a
mild-mannered, closeted boy, is both terrified of and infatuated with his new brother, who in turn
becomes fiercely protective of him—so much so that when Niall goes off to study at university,
Ruben comes to visit and does something with terrible, life-changing consequences for several
characters. From here, their lives are bound together in a visceral love–hate relationship. They
go through several reversals of positions, with one doing well while the other struggles, and their
relationship ebbs and flows with these changes.

Gadd has taken on several meaty topics here, most of which were also part of the mix in _Baby
Reindeer_: masculinity, sexuality, the lasting effects of trauma, and class, to name a few, and all
of them are depicted as sites of intense and bleak struggle. Gadd’s worlds are brutal, and there
are times in _Half Man_ when it feels laid on a little too thick. One character’s failed suicide
attempt is one such moment when I thought, _Good grief, maybe that’s enough misery to pile into
one man’s life story_. A little well-judged comedy lifts the mood fractionally here and there
(Ruben, reflecting on Niall’s performance as Hamlet in a student play: “At one stage, you were
talking to a skull; the cunt’s dead, what are you talking to him for?”), but what saves the show
from feeling gratuitously dark is the richness of the way it weaves all the sorry elements of the
two men’s lives together. That, plus the feral brilliance of the two central performances from
Bell and Gadd himself, who has undergone a radical physical transformation since his lanky _Baby
Reindeer_ days into a muscle-bound bruiser.

The clearest through line from _Baby Reindeer_ is a bold and often uncomfortable insistence on
refusing clear morality. Gadd, it seems, has a sustained interest in making work that doesn’t
offer easy lessons. Ruben does monstrous things. Aside from his grotesque violence in fits of rage,
he spits at a woman who flirts with him and then justifies it to Niall by saying, “If you give
your body away so easily like that, don’t be upset if it comes back with spit on it.” We also
learn of the terrible suffering Ruben went through as a child, and we see him do things like
volunteering with vulnerable boys at a youth center. These scenes don’t justify Ruben’s crimes,
but they do complicate our picture of villainy and victimhood in general. It’s unsettling, no
doubt intentionally so. But it feels true to life, albeit a truth that it is generally simpler not
to try to grapple with on television.

If _Baby Reindeer_ tested the limits of audience sympathy with its central character, _Half Man_
disregards those limits entirely. It doesn’t feel important to the show whether we like Ruben, who
of course we don’t, or even Niall. Niall does plenty of monstrous things of his own, though his
form of violence is psychological rather than physical. What is important is that we understand the
fatal dance in which these men find themselves. There is a vein of Greek tragedy in the story of two
brothers inseparably bonded and destined to destroy each other. Where Gadd goes now after _Half
Man_, to follow what feels like another magnum opus, is anybody’s guess. But I recall thinking
that after watching _Baby Reindeer_ too, and look at the astonishing work he gave us next.
