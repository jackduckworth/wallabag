One of the Greatest Franchises of Our Time Has Had a Good Run. It’s Time to Lay It to Rest.

====================================================================================================

The latest _Hunger Games_ film premiered last week to potential record-breaking sales and tons of
fanfare, keeping the decadelong passion for the grisly young-adult dystopian franchise alive. This
latest installment, _Sunrise on the Reaping_, is a prequel that tells the origin story of how young
Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) becomes Katniss Everdeen’s jaded mentor that we know and love
when we meet him at the start of the original trilogy. This new chapter in author Suzanne Collins’
world serves as a bridge between her 2020 prequel novel and its 2023 musical-ish movie adaptation
_The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes_, and the original trilogy. If _The Ballad of Songbirds and
Snakes_ details how the in-world Hunger Games began, and the original _Hunger Games_ trilogy shows
the revolution that ended them, _Sunrise on the Reaping_ shows how the first seeds of that rebellion
were sown, catching us up to present day with an epilogue tying this prequel to the original
trilogy. It fills the gaps in the larger story, effectively closing the loop on the entire series.

But is _The Hunger Games_ actually concluding after what feels like the final chapter of this
series? Well, not if you ask some fans who believe that there’s actually room for Collins to do
even more. One of the leading theories among _Hunger Games_ die-hards is that Collins might continue
on with the story of Plutarch Heavensbee. When we first meet Plutarch in the second book and film,
_Catching Fire_, where he is played by the late, great Philip Seymour Hoffman, he is the head
gamemaker of the Hunger Games, which were designed by the wealthy Capitol to keep the outlying
districts in check by sending their children and teens to fight to the death in a televised
competition every year. However, Plutarch uses his insider position to collude with revolutionaries,
becoming a key member of the rebellion. In _Sunrise on the Reaping_, we see when Plutarch (here
played by Jesse Plemons) meets Haymitch for the first time, persuading the young man to join him in
an attempt to break the Games’ arena, sparking hope of rebellion among the districts for whom the
Games were instated as punishment for a previous failed insurrection. Because the film dives deeper
into Plutarch’s story than any installment has before, including scenes in the film that are not
in the book (which is written from the perspective of Haymitch), fans believe—or hope, at
least—that Plutarch will be getting the prequel treatment next.

It’s a compelling idea, I have to admit. But as a fan, I must put my foot down and say: It’s
time for _The Hunger Games_ to end.

Look, I get it. _The Hunger Games_ remains one of the best dystopian young-adult series of our time.
It’s certainly better than some of the other offerings of that dystopian-film-franchise boom
(sorry, not sorry, _Divergent_) that dominated the 2010s. _The Hunger Games_ felt frighteningly
timely when the first book was released in 2008, inspired by a moment when Collins was channel
surfing and found herself flipping between coverage of young people competing on a reality show and
young people fighting in the Iraq War. Collins took the topical themes of the time and crafted a
story that managed tothread the needle between gruesomely dark themes, whimsical world-building, and
actual politics made legible to younger audiences. Given that the books follow a revolution, they
are stuffed with interesting characters both big and small—the types of characters you would
indeed read whole other books about. And to top it all off, the original films were actually _good_
(well, most of them). In 2026 it’s not hard to look at the state of the world and see how timely
Collins’ grisly tale still is.

But at this point, Collins has given us the story behind the beginning, middle, and end of the
Games, as well as the beginning, middle, and end of the revolutionary spark that vanquished the evil
totalitarian dictatorship and its bloodthirsty entertainment. What more could there possibly be to
show? Characters like Plutarch are fan favorites and infinitely interesting, as many who fight
oppression are, but we already understand the role they play in the insurrection that leads to the
end of the ruling class’s cruelty. If Collins keeps going, the well-crafted story she birthed
might overstay its welcome and the important political message she imbued within that story might
lose its potency.

I wouldn’t begrudge Collins the right to continue playing in her own sandbox. She built this
universe and deserves to reap the rewards. She already has, clearly: _The Hunger Games_ has become a
leading franchise that has sold millions of books and made millions more dollars in movie-ticket
sales. We never thought Collins would return to the world of Panem after putting the original
trilogy to bed, but a decade after publishing _Mockingjay_, she returned with _The Ballad of
Songbirds and Snakes_, and _Sunrise on the Reaping_ just five years after that. Why? She still
hasn’t said much on the matter, but it’s hard to ignore that this series she created is highly
successful both monetarily and critically. Who among us wouldn’t want to return to the well of
prosperity if there were even a drop left at the bottom? Whether Collins will publish yet another
prequel after this remains to be seen; when asked about future books in a recent red carpet
interview, she merely replied with a demure “We’ll see.”

But the best storytellers know when to call it quits—and I think of Collins as a pretty astute
storyteller. It’s time to let the extensive world that she crafted rest for good, lest we cheapen
the value of its nuanced morality, its taut narratives, and its memorable characters. The series
asks us to be skeptical of spectacle for spectacle’s sake. With that in mind, let’s lay _The
Hunger Games_ to rest before our favorite story becomes that which it critiques.
