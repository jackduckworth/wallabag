

====================================================================================================

He Started as Just Another Marvel Hunk, but Now He’s Once Again Revealed Himself to Be a Stunningly Sensitive Actor

====================================================================================================

This time around the subject, loosely inspired by real-life events, is the clash of cultural values
that can emerge in the wake of immigration. The Gheorghiu family has recently relocated from
Bucharest, Romania, to a remote village in northern Norway. Mihai (Sebastian Stan) is Romanian,
while his wife Lisbet (Renate Reinsve) is Norwegian-born. They’re returning to her hometown so
that her mother can help care for the couple’s five children, who range in age from mid-teens to
infancy.

The Gheorghius are Christian fundamentalists whose lives are organized around their faith. Though we
never learn exactly what religious sect they belong to, it’s established early on that Mihai and
Lisbet are strict but loving parents whose children are forbidden to use the internet, play video
games, or dance. As the film opens, their oldest child, teenage Elia (Vanessa Ceban) is apologizing
to her father for some infraction she’s committed, the nature of which the audience is never
shown. “You need to learn to admit when you’re wrong,” he tells her sternly as she gives him a
tearful hug. That will prove to be a tough assignment for every character in this movie, child or
adult, especially since, as the viewer is soon to find out, the question of who exactly is in the
wrong gets increasingly harder to resolve.

The Gheorghius enroll their children in the local public school, a seemingly welcoming and
progressive institution whose principal is their friendly next-door neighbor Mats (Markus Scarth
Tønseth). Mats’ rebellious teen daughter Noora (Henrikke Lund-Olsen) takes a shine—possibly a
romantic one—to the pious Elia and persuades her and her younger brother Emmanuel (Jonathan
Ciprian Breazu) to sneak out of their house for stealthy late-night joyrides on her family’s
motorboat.

The family seems to be settling well into their secular but close-knit new community, until the
school gym teacher notices some bruises on Elia’s back during wrestling class. It’s never clear
that these are the results of parental abuse—earlier on we witness a shoving match between Elia
and Noora’s jealous ex-best friend, and the gym class itself seems rough enough to have
potentially caused the marks. But Norwegian laws about child abuse are strict enough that within
days a representative from the state child welfare office (Ellen Dorrit Petersen) has ordered the
placement of not just Elia but all of the Gheorghiu children, including the still-breastfeeding
baby, into three different foster homes while the parents’ child-rearing practices are
investigated.

This setup is established less than an hour into _Fjord_’s two-and-a-half-hour run time; with
pacing that’s deliberate but never drags, Mungiu’s patient camera (the cinematographer is Tudor
Vladimir Panduru) observes the repercussions of these events, both within the Gheorghiu family and
throughout the broader community. Eventually that scope will expand to include the global religious
right, as the outraged Mihai begins to attract a large social-media following protesting what he
perceives to be faith-based persecution. During a civil hearing to assess their parental fitness,
Mihai and Lisbet are cross-examined not only about their attitudes toward spanking as a form of
parental discipline—a practice Mihai defends as a part of his traditional culture—but about
their refusal to give their children cellphones and their insistence on daily prayer. Even as you
start to believe it’s true that the couple’s conservative beliefs might be harmful to their
kids’ development, you find yourself taking their side against the officious child-welfare
bureaucrats who seem convinced that a single interview with two confused teenagers—both of whom
say that they love their parents and want to return home—constitutes evidence enough to make all
five Gheorghiu children wards of the state and possibly put their parents in prison.

Sebastian Stan may be most familiar to your average American moviegoer as Captain America’s
sidekick–turned–Winter Soldier Bucky Barnes (a role he will be reprising in the upcoming
_Avengers: Doomsday_), but he has since made his name as a serious and ambitious actor, delivering a
performance of great comic brio as Tommy Lee in the miniseries _Pam & Tommy_ and bringing an
unexpected pathos to the thankless role of a young Donald Trump in 2024’s _The Apprentice_, a role
that earned him his first Oscar nomination. Stan spent the first eight years of his life in Romania
and subsequently grew up around an extended family of Romanian-speaking immigrants; without knowing
the language, I can only say that he switches with utter believability between that language, which
Mihai speaks to his family, and the Romanian-accented English he uses to communicate with his
Norwegian neighbors. Stan’s resolutely unglamorous performance as Mihai, a soft-spoken but
steel-willed man with huge reserves of pride and occasional flashes of frightening anger, is what
gives the film its fascinatingly unstable center of gravity, and it’s not hard to see why he might
be on his way to nomination No. 2.

As for Renate Reinsve, while she doesn’t disappear physically into her role as completely as Stan
does into his—she’s still recognizable as the gorgeous, willowy star of _The Worst Person in the
World_ and _Sentimental Value_—Lisbet is a very different character from the kind we’re used to
seeing Reinsve play, not a spiky individualist but a devout and dutiful wife and mother. Her big
scene, a sit-down with child-welfare authorities during which she gradually comprehends that her
whole world is about to be ripped apart, is a master class in acting as reacting, and may very well
earn her a second Oscar nod as well.

Mungiu’s script economically establishes that, though the Gheorghius’ marriage operates by more
or less traditional gender roles, the couple’s bond to each other and to their children is genuine
and deep. In the film’s most affecting scene, the camera observes Mihai from behind as he looks
out the window at his wife in their driveway, buckling their infant son into his car seat as he’s
about to be taken away by a worker for child protective services. Neither the father’s nor the
mother’s face is visible during this long take; it’s only through their body language (his rigid
stance, her slumping shoulders) that we see how wrenching the moment is for them both.

_Fjord_ is less concerned with reconciling the tension between secular and faith-based value systems
than with making the audience sit with the fact that even the most nominally progressive modern
democracies are constructed around what may be an unbridgeable gulf. Like the beautiful but
threatening snowdrifts that cover the mountains around the village—avalanches are frequent enough
that the school has a fire drill–like protocol for hustling the kids indoors when the snow starts
to slide—the social order Mungiu shows us appears constantly on the verge of collapse. In the
film’s final shot, the sacred and the profane seem to converge in an image of quasi-supernatural
beauty. You’ll come out of the movie wondering what the filmmaker was asking you to believe about
that closing image, and suspecting that his real goal all along was just to keep you asking.
