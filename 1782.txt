

====================================================================================================

Pep's final act of passion: Amazon's revealing Guardiola series pulls no punches

====================================================================================================

Having occupied a front-row seat for the final two seasons of Pep Guardiola's gilded Manchester City
tenure, the subject of Amazon's A Beautiful Obsession, the surprise to me is how unfiltered the
four-part series is.

Particularly, the telling of the torrid 2024-25 campaign when the wheels came clattering off in the
bleakest of mid-winter plunges that featured nine defeats and one victory in 13 aura-shattering
games.

To be shown from Wednesday on Prime, this series controlled and filmed by City is no hagiography of
Guardiola or his players that seeks to paper over the great Catalan's most harrowing season. Neither
is the decision to let go of the club's greatest modern-day player, Kevin De Bruyne, soft-soaped.
And the anger of Khaldoon al-Mubarak, the chair, at Guardiola's odd decision to change 10 players
for last season's 2-0 Champions League loss at home to Bayer Leverkusen is not left on the
cutting-room floor.

Instead, the viewer witnesses up close and maybe too personal, when his divorce is cited as
motivation, Guardiola flailing for solutions he fails to find. And shown, too, is how the ruthless
business of football reduces a peerless manager and De Bruyne to the commodities they are, as City's
"machine built for winning" (Khaldoon's description) must thresh on.

Guardiola talks about his marriage breaking down after a 2-0 defeat by Juventus in the Champions
League on 11 December 2024. It was City's seventh reverse in 10 games, so to try to rouse the team
from their slump a disgusted Guardiola unloads about what went wrong between him and Cristina Serra.

[Manager City manager Pep Guardiola had several scratches on his head and a cut on the bridge of his
nose which were visible when he was interviewed after Manchester City's Champions League clash with
Feyenoord on 26 November 2024.]
"I'm fucking divorced from the most beautiful woman on your planet," he says. "I love her
unbelievably, but we lost the passion. How do you play football, because you are paid? Or because of
something inside? Where is the passion?"

This is an interesting choice. If Guardiola has every right to talk about personal issues, the
decision seems counterproductive: a crossing of the professional/private line that could,
conversely, make him appear weaker before his team, as normal managerial modes of motivation have
lost their power. As he says later regarding what makes a good No 1: "You have to convince your
players [that they would] go to the penthouse, the roof and jump down [for you]."

A year later, after the 10 changes against Leverkusen, Guardiola again says: "I leave my life, my
divorce and my family is away, and everything for all of you. And what do you give back?"

Manuel Estiarte, in the second episode named The Rebuild, compounds the sense of a man running out
of ideas when Guardiola's close friend, and City's director of first-team operations, reveals he
told the players about the divorce, to let them know this is why "Pep is anxious, sad, different".

Estiarte continues: "I said to the team: 'Please, you have to understand, you're not playing with a
coach who can be a bit impulsive, intense, sometimes a bit up and down. You're seeing a human being.
He's getting a divorce.' It was necessary for the team not only to visualise Pep as a manager but
for the first time as a human being."

[Bayer Leverkusen's Álex Grimaldo (left) celebrates scoring their side's first goal in their 2-0
Champions League victory over Manchester City at the Etihad Stadium.]
First, you wonder why Guardiola did not impart this sensitive information himself to the players, as
this might have aided the intended impact. Then, you ponder if Khaldoon and Ferran Soriano, City's
CEO, discussed whether the manager's private life might be affecting his ability to do his job.

Interestingly, we cut next to a jovial Guardiola. "In my life I made a lot of changes – I got
divorced and I have to settle a bit. But no complaints at all honestly," he says before, tellingly,
insisting he is a happy man – regarding his work. "In my job, yeah," the manager says.

The theme of interpersonal relations is a leitmotif of the documentary – and so, by extension,
Guardiola's management style (episode three is titled: Love and Hugs).

In "The Desert", part one, he is close to tears after Kyle Walker abandons the teetering ship in
January 2025, precisely when City need their captain. Walker did not behave like their leader,
Guardiola tells the players, before a cutting rhetorical question: as they had voted him as the
captain, why did they not pin him to the wall and demand more loyalty, he asks.

The scene that precipitates the defender's departure is beautifully filmed and comes in the Anfield
dressing room after Walker's error leads to Luis Díaz winning a penalty converted by Mohamed Salah
in the 2-0 loss.

Guardiola mentions this, causing Walker to fire back that the finger is being pointed at him "every
meeting". It ends with the manager in tears, delivering a soliloquy about how his signing of a new
contract, only 11 days before, in the midst of the disastrous run, was because of a desire to fight.
Juxtaposed in the shot, in a moment of cinematic panache, is Walker, now silently reflecting on his
role in this. Guardiola says: "If captains first, then the other ones, for example [feel] that
there's a problem, tell me. It's not going to change the love I have for all of you. I'm sorry."

More regrets start episode two, after De Bruyne is not offered a new deal, and Soriano stresses to
Khaldoon that "Kevin gets the respect he deserves as the greatest player in the modern history of
City". Guardiola reveals 'Super Kev' broke down in his office but also that there had to be coldness
in the decision to offload him, before the Belgian is shown crying when asked what will be missed
most. "My kids don't want to go," he says.

[Pep Guardiola hugs Kevin De Bruyne as the Belgian midfielder prepares to come on for his last
appearance for Manchester City.]
Here, the viewer is reminded how those in elite sport can feel emotion, that tapping into passions
is vital for success. And that beyond the game-face displayed on the pitch, and in front of the
media and fans, are real relationships which bring joy – and hurt.

More admirable honesty is offered up when Guardiola's 10 changes for that November 2025 defeat to
Leverkusen are criticised. "Our attitude with Pep Guardiola should not be too negative. But it
shouldn't be too kind or accommodating either. This is his mistake this time," says Soriano. To
which Khaldoon says: "To pick an entire new [outfield] team in a game this important is probably one
of the lowest points we've had. I can't speak to him – I'm so angry."

Yet, by the end of part four – "Nothing Is Eternal" – all seems comparatively rosy again. The
Carabao Cup and FA Cup are claimed to make it 20 trophies (including three Community Shields) in a
decade for Guardiola, and City finish second. With his rebuild done he receives a fitting send-off,
before the start of the rest of his life. As he says: "Life should not just be about football. It's
too much to be honest."
