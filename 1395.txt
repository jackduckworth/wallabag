We Have a New Worst Star Wars Movie

====================================================================================================

The good news about _The Mandalorian and Grogu_ is you don’t have to have seen all three seasons
of the streaming TV show to enjoy the story of a ruthless bounty hunter and his little green
sidekick. The bad news is I don’t know what you’d have to do to have a good time at this
lifeless, half-hearted movie, which brings _Star Wars_ back to movie theaters for the first time in
seven years, since the similarly dreadful _The Rise of Skywalker_. Take an edible? Guzzle cough
medicine? Or just set your expectations so low you could trip over them? Perhaps a nice pre-film
lobotomy would do the trick.

When it arrived in 2019, _The Mandalorian_ felt like a blessed reprieve from the lore-encrusted bent
of _Rogue One_ and _Solo_, small-minded stories crammed into the gaps in the original trilogy like
wads of paper beneath a wobbly table. The stories were largely self-contained adventures, with the
occasional deep-cut reference for hardcore fans, but you didn’t need to know a jawa from a
tauntaun to follow along. This didn’t last, of course: The mysterious mercenary acquired a name
(if not often a face) and Baby Yoda got one, too, as the story grew more complicated and
consequential—or so I gather, because when the first season ended with a revelation that was only
intelligible to viewers versed in the animated series _The Clone Wars_, I was out. It seemed as if
the show and its creator, Jon Favreau, had glimpsed a way to a brighter future, one that looked back
to the series’ inspiration in featherweight _Flash Gordon_ serials, and purposefully turned away,
or had his hand forced by a studio that was mostly concerned with compelling fans to keep consuming
and unwilling to run the risk of having a satisfying ending.

The movie, directed by Favreau, who also co-wrote the screenplay with Dave Filoni and Noah Kloor,
represents yet another fresh start. Though it brings back a handful of characters, including the
warlord Janu (Jonny Coyne) from _The Mandalorian_ Season 3, and Jabba the Hutt’s twin cousins from
_The Book of Boba Fett_, knowing that isn’t necessary to follow along. (If you know the
Mandalorian and Grogu, you can follow _The Mandalorian and Grogu_.) But though the movie doesn’t
get ensnarled in past story, it neglects to come up with one of its own. There’s the outline of a
plot, in which Mando (voiced by Pedro Pascal, but played largely by a helmeted Brendan Wayne and
Lateef Crowder) is sent to hunt down a foe of the New Republic and winds up being hunted himself.
But it never develops beyond a rough sketch, lazily meandering through two hours and change as if
the mere presence of familiar characters is enough to keep the audience from drifting away.

If _The Mandalorian and Grogu_ isn’t the worst _Star Wars_ movie—and it might be—it’s
certainly the least: the least essential, the least engaging, the least necessary. Say what you want
about _The Phantom Menace_, but George Lucas had something he wanted to get off his chest. (Also,
the pod-racing scene is pretty neat.) From its opening sequence, Favreau’s movie displays a
near-total lack of urgency, which might be fine if we were simply in hangout mode. The movie’s
environments, from a neon-lit netherworld to a remote jungle fortress, are rich and detailed, with
the lived-in quality that distinguished Lucas’ world from so many other sci-fi sagas. But the
characters in them are stiff and shapeless, their dialogue flat and functional.

It’s the latter that proves the movie’s most genuinely shocking lapse. Lucas’ dialogue could
be strained and unnatural—as Harrison Ford once told him, “You can type this shit, but you
can’t say it”—but it at least strove for a sense of occasion. Favreau, Filoni, and Kloor’s
script doesn’t even try to sing. As part of their initial mission, which involves trading favors
with the Hutt twins in order to secure information vital to the New Republic, Mando and Grogu visit
a down-and-dirty planet where Jabba the Hutt’s son, Rotta (voiced by Jeremy Allen White), has been
pressed into service as a gladiator, fighting creatures in the arena until he has paid off his
debts. (If you’re wondering how a giant slug can fight, this one has—and I wish I were making
this up—bulging biceps and six-pack abs.) Mando offers to free him, but Rotta declines, because
he’s also hoping that his victories will help him make a name for himself. He laments, “Do you
know how hard it is to be your own man when your dad is Jabba the Hutt?” It certainly doesn’t
help that this movie’s Hutts inexplicably speak perfect English, a jarring departure from
Jabba’s rumbling Huttese, or that White’s vocal performance is somehow both affectless and
unrecognizable. But the problem is with the line itself, a terrible clunker that only leaves you
wondering what “being your own man” means to a character who isn’t a man to begin with.

It’s not surprising, then, that the best parts of _The Mandalorian and Grogu_ are those in which
there’s almost no talking at all. Although he’s little more than a cuddly sidekick for most of
the movie’s first hour, Grogu gets to take center stage when Mando is sidelined, and the sequences
from his point of view—which is to say, that of a curious, occasionally willful child who also has
quasi-magical powers—are a true, if far too brief, delight. (There are, technically, a few words
spoken in these segments, but since the semi-comprehensible utterances belong to a trio of adorable
mini aliens voiced by Shirley Henderson, Babu Frik herself, it’s a bit of a gray area.) Baby Yoda
may be a puppet, but he’s still more expressive than a man in a helmet (or even, frankly, than
Pascal, for the one scene in which we get a glimpse of his face). It’s like seeing a now-familiar
world through a fresh pair of eyes, and for a few minutes, you remember why this fictional universe
has endured for so long, drawing in generation after generation. But then Mando takes charge again,
and you’re back in our universe, where they just keep throwing _Star Wars_ at the wall, and
nothing seems to stick.
