Nobody in American Politics Can Figure Out When to Back Down

====================================================================================================

_Sign up for the Surge__, the newsletter that covers the most important political nonsense of the
week, delivered to your inbox every Saturday._

Welcome to this week’s edition of the Surge, Slate’s politics newsletter that encourages you to
get some rest this August. It’s going to be a long fall campaign season talking about the only
issue that matters in America: Hasan Piker.

Today we review some results from a big week of primaries and explore what could have possibly
compelled Jeanine Pirro to tell the truth in a legal filing. Todd Blanche’s attorney general
nomination came down to one famous Senate pushover. Is it possible he could get pushed over?

Let’s begin, though, with a look toward next week’s most-watched primary, and Fox News’ next
big celebrity-in-waiting.

1.

FRANCESCA HONG

The national spotlight is now fully thrust on Francesca Hong, the democratic socialist and
front-runner for Tuesday’s Wisconsin Democratic gubernatorial primary. The topic of the week was
Thanksgiving, after a 2020 tweet of Hong’s calling to “cancel Thanksgiving” resurfaced. In a
CNN interview this week, then, she was asked whether she still believed that the holiday should be
canceled. She did not give a firm answer. Another day or so later, she told Politico that
Thanksgiving is her favorite holiday, that “I don’t want to cancel it,” and that “I actually
love it.”

Hong rose in the polls by consolidating left-wing primary voters behind a populist economic platform
of expanding social services, taxing the rich, and crushing data centers—all against a splintered
opposition. But now that she’s at the top, everything she has ever said or posted will be under
national examination.

We don’t have any advice for Hong on how she should answer these sorts of questions about past
tweets sent from deep inside the bubble. We would point out, though, that governor’s races are not
like Senate or presidential elections, in which pure partisanship ensures that each candidate in a
swing state like Wisconsin starts at 47 percent. General election voters are more flexible in
state-level races, which don’t have any effect on whether Donald Trump can continue getting judges
confirmed. And we’re hard-pressed to think of anyone who’s won a governor’s race after
struggling to answer a question about whether Thanksgiving should be canceled.

2.

ABDUL EL-SAYED

Some great news: This will be our last entry about the Democratic Senate primary in Michigan,
because guess what? It actually happened this week, after what felt like 47 years of campaigning. As
the polls predicted, progressive Abdul El-Sayed defeated moderate Rep. Haley Stevens. But as the
polls did _not_ predict, it was a squeaker: El-Sayed won by only a point.

Our first reaction was that the lack of a lopsided verdict meant that El-Sayed would have to work
that much harder to bring the party together. But then we saw that as a potential positive. El-Sayed
admits he can be cocky, and the result called for an immediate conciliatory tone to welcome
skeptical Stevens voters. Among the principals, everyone—El-Sayed, Stevens, Chuck Schumer, the
Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee—quickly got on the same team. Republicans, lining up
behind former Rep. Mike Rogers, immediately revealed that their campaign strategy would rely on
scaring voters out of electing a Muslim. Shocker. Do you think we’ll be able to skip this race in
next week’s edition? If only we could promise such luxuries …

3.

BILL CASSIDY

By the time you read this, it’s entirely possible that the Senate will have voted to confirm Todd
Blanche as attorney general. Because, by midday Friday, Trump’s pick appeared to have the votes.

Early in the week, the two senators who were holding up Blanche in committee—Thom Tillis and John
Cornyn—signed off on his nomination after Blanche formally rescinded the order establishing
Trump’s $1.8 billion “anti-weaponization” fund. Once out of committee, the administration had
to secure the support of either Bill Cassidy, Lisa Murkowski, or Susan Collins. Collins quickly said
she wouldn’t support him. (We should’ve known, then and there, that Blanche would be fine.)
Then, early Friday morning, Murkowski said she wouldn’t either. So Cassidy had a free shot on an
open goal. With one vote, he could stick it to the president who had cost him his reelection bid. He
could salvage his reputation following his infamous vote for Robert F. Kennedy Jr. He could, in
short, live a little bit.

Cassidy announced, in a Senate floor speech late Friday morning, that he would support Blanche after
all. It came down to something we mentioned a few weeks ago: fear of the alternative. “The choice
is not between perfection and Mr. Blanche,” Cassidy said. “It is between Mr. Blanche and another
acting attorney general who may not run the department effectively under President Trump, and who
indeed may not be as good as Mr. Blanche.” A normal administration might be grateful for
Cassidy’s support, under the circumstances—he’s going to get an earful for flinching, once
again—and proceed cautiously. But we’d soon expect _this_ Justice Department to find a way to
rub it in his eye as a parting gift.

4.

MAX MILLER

One House Republican—North Carolina Rep. Chuck Edwards—dropped his reelection bid this week
under a growing cloud of scandal. Here’s the problem for House Republican leaders: They needed two
members to pack it up, and Ohio Rep. Max Miller has not budged. Details of his messy divorce and
child custody battle, which include allegations (which Miller denies) of mental and physical abuse,
have reached a breaking point for the party. Miller’s former father-in-law, Sen. Bernie Moreno,
broke his long silence over the matter. “As he has admitted privately,” Moreno posted on X,
“Max Miller needs serious psychological help. He is a danger to my daughter, and I hold my breath
every minute he has custody of my granddaughter.” He called on him to quit and injected further
tales of alleged cruelty into the discussion. Numerous other colleagues of Moreno’s have as well.

Miller has blown through one deadline to withdraw from reelection, missing the Wednesday cutoff that
would trigger a special primary election to replace him on the November ballot. If he withdraws by
Monday, though, local Republican officials in his district could approve a new nominee. Miller has
shown no indication that he’s willing to do that. As he explained in a deeply uncomfortable CNN
interview this week, “My daughter is going to grow up one day, and she’s going to see all of
this, and she’s not going to see that her father caved to political and social media pressures for
something that he never did.” But that Monday deadline—and more pressure from Trump, who has
waffled about this publicly but would not like to lose the House—could focus his otherwise
scattered mind.

5.

JEANINE PIRRO

U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia Jeanine Pirro crossed a bright red line late last Friday
when she did the unthinkable: She told the truth in court (!) about what had happened with the
Reflecting Pool renovation. In seeking to dismiss a felony charge against David Hearn, the former
Olympian charged with ripping off a piece of new pool liner, Pirro explained in a filing that the
pool’s “damage was the result of a botched installation and not vandalism.”

This shocked us at first, as Pirro would have known better than anyone ahead of time that Trump
would take this poorly. And he sure did, fuming at her publicly and privately for several days in a
row. But Pirro had a plan to keep her job. She loaded boxes of evidence into a White House meeting
with Trump and Interior Secretary Doug Burgum to prove to the president that it was Burgum who had
caused this problem for the president. The plan worked well enough that Pirro—for now, at
least—still has a job. Apologizing to Trump will not work. It’s weak, after all. Anger
redirection, though, has some potential.

6.

THOMAS SMITH

One of the more bewildering primary results of the year came this week in Michigan’s 8th
Congressional District, a swing seat currently represented by Democratic freshman Rep. Kristen
McDonald Rivet. Amir Hassan, the Navy veteran backed by Trump and GOP congressional leaders, was
flattened in his primary by a certain Thomas Smith, a rando who had suspended his campaign (and
endorsed a third candidate, Al Lemmo). To give you a sense of the credibility of Smith’s
candidacy, his only campaign finance filing to the Federal Election Commission was a handwritten
note stating that he had raised $1,850 and that he couldn’t file it electronically “because I
have had no success logging in and have not been able to get a response from the helpdesk.”

Our first reaction to this story was that it was extremely funny. After further thought, though, it
nets out to merely above-average funny. There’s only one reason a credible, Trump-backed candidate
named Amir Hassan would have lost this Republican primary, and it’s not pretty.

7.

ANDY OGLES

Tennessee held its primaries on Thursday, because Thursday primaries are just how Tennessee rolls.
The main news of the evening was that Tennessee Rep. Andy Ogles, a Freedom Caucus member and
shit-stirrer, lost his primary to former state Agriculture Commissioner Charlie Hatcher. Ogles had
Trump’s endorsement; Hatcher had the endorsement of Tennessee Gov. Bill Lee.

Alongside a history of campaign finance investigations, Ogles can be a jerk. He tweeted on separate
occasions this year that “Muslims don’t belong in American society” and that “homosexuality
has no place in America.” (The latter he deleted and blamed on a staffer.) So why, in spite of
those remarks, did he lose a Republican primary in Tennessee? One factor was that Hatcher outraised
and outspent Ogles. But redistricting played a big role too. The state redistricted this year after
the Supreme Court nuked another section of the Voting Rights Act, eliminating the Memphis-based
district held by Democrats. That meant that Ogles was running in a new, hideously gerrymandered
district snaking from just outside Nashville all the way to Memphis. The Associated Press estimated
that 82 percent of the 5th Congressional District’s voters were new to it. And these voters
decided that Andy Ogles would have no place in, and would not belong to, the next Congress.
