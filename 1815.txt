

====================================================================================================

It Got Exploded, Pummeled, Anviled, and Left for Dead. But Now It’s Back to Life, and It’s a Delight.

====================================================================================================

In _Coyote vs. Acme_, Wile E. sets his sights on the company whose malfunctioning products have done
him unspeakable bodily harm. In the live-action world inhabited by Kevin Avery (Will Forte), the
small-time lawyer who agrees to take the coyote’s case, Acme’s wares seem to work well, or at
least well enough that no one questions why their cellphone and their car are the same brand. But
the cartoons who live alongside the humans don’t fare so well. The coyote’s detonator explodes
in his face rather than setting off the dynamite meant to reduce the roadrunner to ash; his backpack
releases a fistful of silverware rather than the parachute meant to slow his descent to earth. Not
even less risk-taking toons are safe. Poor old Porky Pig is just walking down the street—in
Albuquerque, naturally—when he’s smashed into a wall by a wrecking ball with the Acme logo.

Perhaps Wile E. has never connected the dots and realized that the Acme corporation was the author
of all his pain, or perhaps it simply never occurred to him that he could do anything about it. When
Chuck Jones and Michael Maltese’s cartoons first started appearing in the late 1940s, Acme was a
satire of low-rent brands, the kind of generic knockoffs you might order from a small-print ad in
the back of a magazine. But by the 2020s, Acme isn’t the bargain choice; it’s the only choice.
Kevin, who is named after the legendary Looney Tunes animator Tex Avery (his law-firm partners share
the surnames of the coyote and roadrunner’s creators), makes a viable if unglamorous living
settling small-claims suits against Acme on behalf of injured cartoon clients. When he first meets
Wile E., he pegs his injury as “a run-of-the-mill $300 rocket-skate case.” But Kevin’s
settlements come with a boilerplate clause stipulating that none of this is Acme’s fault, and
they’ve apparently got deep enough pockets that they can keep hurting cartoons and paying out
penny-ante claims for eternity. The coyote cleverly manipulates him into filing a massive lawsuit
all the same—they don’t call him Wile E. for nothing—and Kevin is eventually won over to the
cause. But they’re going up against the biggest company there is, one that seems to unofficially
own the few things, like judges or the U.S. Congress, that don’t already bear its logo.

The feeling of being powerless to slow the depredation of a massive megacorp is, of course, not just
the story of _Coyote vs. Acme_ but the story behind it. In 2023, producer Christopher DeFaria was
informed that Warner Bros. had no plans to release the completed $70 million film—not in
theaters, not on demand, not to its own streaming platform—and that because the company’s CEO,
David Zaslav, had decided to write the film off as a complete loss for tax purposes, it might never
be seen at all. It was, Zaslav modestly concluded, a decision that took “real courage.” (Even
more courageous was the nearly $50 million in compensation Zaslav took home that same year.)

It wasn’t the only film Zaslav boldly decided to erase from existence; a fully shot, if not
entirely complete, movie starring Leslie Grace as Batgirl was canceled after poor test screenings.
But unlike _Batgirl_, which DC Studios co-head Peter Safran called “not releasable,” _Coyote vs.
Acme_ was said to be good, certainly good enough to be seen—test audiences ate it up—and its
value to the studio as a whole would have extended beyond its viewership, reminding HBO Max
subscribers that unrestricted access to the Looney Tunes catalog was only a few clicks away. It
turned out, however, that Zaslav didn’t value the original cartoons either; as with _Sesame
Street_, he viewed “children’s programming” as a distraction from the streaming platform’s
strengths, and so they let the icons of animation history go in March 2025. (You can now watch them
on Tubi.) But unlike _Batgirl_, _Coyote vs. Acme_ became a cause célèbre, and Warner eventually
agreed to sell the movie to the little-known indie distributor Ketchup Entertainment, which has
finally put it into theaters this week.

The original Looney Tunes were born out of a particular creative ferment, a combination of wild
talent and executive disinterest, and re-creating the golden years of Termite Terrace on an ad hoc
basis has never entirely worked, in part because the original cartoons were the products of numerous
teams working in their own distinct, if not quite incompatible, styles. So it’s wise that while
_Coyote vs. Acme_ features oodles of brief appearances by familiar toons, Samy Burch’s script
(based on a legendary 1990 Shouts & Murmurs column by the New Yorker’s Ian Frazier) hews largely
to its title character, although it does feature a substantial supporting role for a certain
carrot-smacking leporid. (Eric Bauza, who voiced Daffy Duck, Foghorn Leghorn, and others in _Space
Jam: A New Legacy_, takes on many of the animated characters here, although the Road Runner’s
_beep-beep_s are mostly recycled from the original cartoon shorts.) Since the coyote and
roadrunner’s struggles are entirely nonverbal (the former does brandish the occasional handwritten
sign), that leaves most of the talking to Forte and John Cena, who plays Acme’s intimidating chief
counsel, along with a supporting cast that includes Lana Condor (_To All the Boys_), P.J. Byrne
(_The Wolf of Wall Street_), and Luis Guzmán.

Forte feels a little constrained by having to play the straight man, but Cena has a ball as Acme’s
Buddy Crane, a ruthless corporate shill who loves doing Acme’s dirty work and has been cleaning
Kevin’s clock since they went to law school together. Cena plays it relatively straight as
well—it’s a bad move to go for antic when you’re acting opposite cartoons—but it’s more of
a supercharged deadpan, as if he’s too full of himself to acknowledge the fundamentally ridiculous
world in which he’s triumphed. When he presses Tweety Bird, who’s been compelled into spying
against his own kind, for information about what he’s seen, he fumes, “I don’t pay you to
tink. I pay you to taw.”

I wish _Coyote vs. Acme_ were as good a movie as it is a cause. Zaslav’s attempt to junk a
finished film for the sake of a tax break was appalling behavior for the head of a studio, and its
release is a triumph over the corporate indifference to art—a story that now has extra resonance
because of how it parallels its characters’ battle to make a faceless behemoth take note of their
existence. But for all its commendable zeal, the movie doesn’t have the anything-goes spirit of
the best Looney Tunes, or even _Who Framed Roger Rabbit_. Burch’s script, written before her
Oscar-nominated _May December_ went into production, is sentimental and moralistic, qualities that
simply don’t belong in this universe, and while the animators do a solid job of emulating the
original cartoons’ style, they can’t possibly match their inventiveness.

That’s an absurdly high bar to clear, especially compared to the lifeless cynicism of the average
Hollywood IP grab, and the movie does manage to connect the coyote’s Möbius-strip pursuit of his
eternal adversary to the equally hopeless battle against corporate consolidation. I just found
myself longing for a little more anarchy, and little less third-act resolution. Screenwriting dogma
insists that characters have to progress, but what makes the cartoons so eternally satisfying is
that nothing ever changes. The coyote takes a million routes but always ends up back at square one,
and though he is endlessly frustrated, his determination never flags. One must imagine Wile E.
Coyote happy.
