Anne Hathaway’s Nasty Little Dinosaur Movie Is Better Than Any Jurassic Park in Years

About a third of the way into _The End of Oak Street_, there’s a scene where Audrey Platt (Maisy
Stella) sneaks away from her family to have a minute to herself. Given that they’ve all been
transported from 1982 to the Mesozoic era, where rampaging allosauruses and pterosaurs have torn
some of their neighbors to bloody shreds, she’s understandably a little bit shaken and in need of
some comfort. So she slips into the garage, quietly turns the key, and pops in a cassette of Steve
Winwood’s “Valerie.” There’s no power in the past, no gasoline or batteries, so every minute
she listens brings her closer to a time when the last ties to the world she knew will be gone
forever, and there’s a decent chance the sound of the music might attract some sharp-toothed
predator that will make a quick meal of her. But the movie doesn’t punish her need to carve a
brief moment of beauty, however fleeting, out of the terrifying battle for survival her life has
abruptly become. She sits behind the wheel, singing along to Winwood’s carefree hit, and we just
sit with her, grabbing the chance to catch our breath.

I dwell on this moment because _Oak Street_ does, and because it’s part of what makes David Robert
Mitchell’s movie stand out from the summer blockbuster field. Mitchell’s take on the family
adventure is leisurely and sincere, lavishing a third of its length on the slow build to a
revelation that the posters give away in an instant, and taking the possibility that Audrey’s
brother Brian (Christian Convery) might get his ass kicked by a neighborhood bully as seriously as
it does any dino attack. It’s also, at times, brutally blunt and darkly funny, serving up some of
its grisliest deaths with tongue savagely in cheek.

The Platts are a middle-class family on the verge of falling apart. Greg (Ewan McGregor) works as a
pizza delivery driver to supplement the income from his regular job; Denise (Anne Hathaway) sneaks
off to the basement at night to work on a novel about a woman escaping her dead-end life. They’re
unhappy in the most ordinary of ways—she worries about everything, while he insists everything
will work out for the best—but it’s been eating away at them for a long time, and their reserves
of goodwill are nearly tapped out. They live on a suburban cul-de-sac full of happy-looking
families, but perhaps their proximity to all that domestic bliss makes their attempts at pretending
fall short by comparison, or maybe they just don’t know how to unite against a threat that can
always be ignored for one more day.

Then, well: Dinosaurs. Mitchell has access to astronomically more advanced technology than Steven
Spielberg had when he made _Jurassic Park_ more than 30 years ago, but he deploys many of the same
strategies, to great cumulative effect. We hear a rumble, see a bush move, catch a glimpse of a bony
ridge peeking up behind a two-story house or a reflection in a window that vanishes just before the
camera comes into focus. Unlike most of Spielberg’s imitators, Mitchell remembers that the father
of the modern blockbuster could be a sadist as well as a magician, tightening the screws with a
giggle or playing mortality for a laugh. As much fun as _The End of Oak Street_ is to watch in a
theater, it’s even better to listen to, as the audience chuckles at a slavering monster creeping
up on its victims unawares, then falls hushed as the punch line arrives. (You’ll also want to make
sure you have company when Anne Hathaway whips out a rifle.)

Convery (_Sweet Tooth_) and Stella (_My Old Ass_) are two of the best actors in their age bracket,
and they’re particularly good at zeroing out any hint of internet-era self-consciousness.
As far as they’re concerned, they might be the first people in human history to experience the
emotions they’re feeling, and they take us back to a time before audiences got so pleased with
spotting common tropes that they forgot how to give themselves over to a story. That might allow
Mitchell to employ a few more shopworn developments than he ought to—while the characters live in
1982, we do not—but it also frees him from the compulsion to constantly wink at his audience, to
signal that he’s better or smarter than the story he has chosen to tell. There are things you
don’t see coming, of course, but like that swiftly approaching theropod, they’re hidden in plain
sight.

For a movie involving space-time wormholes and prehistoric carnivores, _The End of Oak Street_ is
surprisingly straightforward. It’s not an air-quotes throwback or a simple nostalgia
piece—there’s at least one detail you would _never_ have seen in a wide-release studio movie in
1982.
But it’s the work of a filmmaker who believes that movies can take us by the hand rather than
grabbing us by the shoulders, even when we’re running for our lives.
