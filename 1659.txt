The World Cup Finale Was a Disaster. Until It Became Kind of Perfect.

====================================================================================================

Maybe it was gravity. Maybe it was the soccer gods wanting to punish Lionel Messi in a substantial
way, finally, for not paying his taxes when he played for Barcelona. But in the first minute of the
second period of extra time on Sunday, something bad finally happened to Argentina. More
descriptively, a bad thing happened that Argentina could not inexplicably fart their way out of.

Spain solidified itself as one of the game’s great national teams, shutting out Argentina as they
had six of their other seven opponents in the World Cup. The suffocating nature of this one was
historic. Argentina didn’t get off a shot until extra time, later than any World Cup finalist
since they started tracking these things in 1966. At no point did an Argentine shot reach Spanish
keeper Unai Simón, whose work was limited to playing the ball, grabbing a cross or two, and calmly
watching a Messi ball into the boxcross float onto the top of the goal in extra time. Those
suffocating statistics are a testament to the team’s brilliance, but the best one is just this:
Messi, even looking a touch dilapidated at 39, was not able to come up with some absurdity in the
nick of time to get Argentina off the hook. The Spanish killed world football’s unkillable team.

For some great number of soccer fans, Argentina evoked a feeling of a group that were getting away
with something. It has become an article of faith for hundreds of millions, perhaps billions, of
people that match officials favored Argentina to give Messi a golden sendoff. I’m not persuaded,
but then, it’s not just fans who felt that way. Egypt’s manager was among the competitors in the
tournament who saw it the same way. And, look, it’s true that the Argentines were frequent
beneficiaries of borderline calls and overreaching tech. The fact that all of those calls were
defensible and some of them were obvious, on replay, is irrelevant.

Messi’s team offended my sensibilities in a different way. The Argentines were simply pulling a
Houdini act in too many consecutive games, and dodging consequences at the last moment, for me to
deem them an honorable football outfit. It happened again and again, with shocking consistency. Cape
Verde, the beloved upstarts in their first World Cup, dragged them to deep water and were closing in
on a coin-flip penalty kicksshootout before conceding a cursed own goal in the 111th minute.
Argentina then trailed Egypt 2-0 deep into the second half before scoring a ridiculous three goals
in 15 minutes to escape. Egypt felt they’d been wrongly denied a goal on review and a penalty kick
after an entanglement later. (My advice would be not to give up three goals in 15 minutes, but I do
not begrudge coach Hossam Hassan his fury.)

It just would not end, though. Their quarterfinal win over Switzerland was maddening. Argentina were
the better side in the first half, but they were suffering against a Swiss onslaught and conceded a
tying goal. The match then turned on a chaotic sequence in which a yellow card on Argentina became a
_second_ yellow on Switzerland’s Breel Embolo. The call was correct, but typical of the world’s
exasperation with Argentina. Yes, the Swiss player had taken a dive, but, _goodness,_ had VAR gotten
annoying. Argentina finally broke through, twice, in the second period of extra time. Surely, that
would be the last time the team cheated death, and they’d have to either play a quality 90 minutes
the next time or be eliminated.

Or not. Alternatively, Argentina could fail to get anything going for 60 minutes in a semifinal
against England, fall behind 1-0, and then benefit from a confounding tactical decision: English
manager Thomas Tuchel’s choice to totally pack it in, park the bus, and (I guess) just pray that
Messi and his friends would not break through at some point. Goals in the 85th minute and stoppage
time sent Argentina through once again. Usually, describing a team’s World Cup in such lengthy
chronological terms would have a dulling effect, but it still feels surreal to see all of these
results listed out in order.

Wow, oh wow, did it look like Argentina was about to pull off more of these shenanigans on Sunday.
They were getting their heads bashed in throughout the match, unable to generate anything that
resembled an attack. But Spain couldn’t find the net, even as it controlled more or less all of
the play. Even after the sendoff of Argentina’s Enzo Fernandez in stoppage time, the 10-man
Argentine dam wouldn’t break. Keeper Emi Martinez set a men’s World Cup final record with 11
saves. An apparent Spanish goal came back for a (legit) foul, reinforcing the perception of some
kind of karmic force field around Messi’s team. Even after Fernan Torres tallied for Spain in the
opening seconds of the second extra period, there were glimmers of Argentine survival: Spain’s
Nico Williams getting too cute in the box and losing a ball out of bounds, a too-greedy Torres
leaning offside by a shoulder’s width to eliminate a second goal. Messi even threw a ball into the
box in the waning seconds, off a set piece, that had every chance to find the goal.

Spain stood strong in a way that felt oddly relieving. It wasn’t that Argentina was villainous but
that I have drunk from a poison well of sports discourse the past few years. Long ago, there was a
time when teams pulling off such high-wire acts were regarded as fun stories of human perseverance
and team strategy. We now live in a different time, in which sports fans (me included) find it cheap
and degrading when teams win games despite not having the best stuff. Hence a social media panic
over “ethical hooping” in basketball, baseball fans finding deep comfort in screenshots of the
league’s Statcast analytics pages, and me finding it morally offensive that Argentina kept getting
away by the hair on their chins. The irony here is that “analytics” thought Argentina was
playing well. By expected goals, they had clocked all of the foes they narrowly beat, including
England. The offense the masses took at their success had to be more vibes-based, or because a Messi
conspiracy thing was good fodder for casuals.

Anyway, I was taken in by this critique of the Argentines. Each narrow slithering away from trouble
shifted my internal monologue to that of the great poet Jesse Pinkman, the _Breaking Bad_ character
who once sobbed of his meth-cooking antagonist Walter White, “HE CAN’T KEEP GETTING AWAY WITH
THIS.” Eventually, Walter couldn’t, but until five minutes into stoppage time of the second
period of extra time of the World Cup final, you could have been forgiven thinking that Argentina
could.
