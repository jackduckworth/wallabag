There’s Only One Man I Want to See Win Best Actor This Year

====================================================================================================

The Irish playwright-turned-filmmaker Martin McDonagh returns to a familiar well in _Wild Horse
Nine_, but that well is deep enough to contain more than one good movie. As in McDonagh’s 2008
directorial debut _In Bruges_, _Wild Horse Nine_ follows two men whose partnership in a dangerous
profession finds them stuck in a picturesque location, awaiting an act of violence that could come
at any time. And like both _In Bruges_ and 2022’s _The Banshees of Inisherin_, this new film is a
black-humored exploration of the intricacies of male friendship. It’s too bad that, like the 2017
semi-misfire _Three Billboards Outside Ebbing, Missouri_, McDonagh’s latest is at times guilty of
using other people’s tragedies as a backdrop for the redemption of messed-up white guys. But since
_Wild Horse Nine_ is largely about that very phenomenon—specifically, the colonization of global
politics and culture by America in all its pigheaded glory—McDonagh’s barbed dialogue at least
punctures the right targets this time around.

It’s 1973, and CIA agents Chris Carlisle (John Malkovich) and Lee Chesterfield (Sam Rockwell) have
spent a year stationed in Santiago, Chile, preparing for the U.S.-backed overthrow of the
democratically elected Marxist president, Salvador Allende. Just weeks before the planned coup,
Chris and Lee take a vacation from the daily grind of plainclothes spying to visit Easter Island,
the Pacific locale famous for its monumental sacred statues—or, as the culturally insensitive
Chris insists on calling them, “the big heads.” It’s a sight Chris has always wanted to see
before he dies—an event that, as established in an early conversation between Chris and Lee, is
likely to happen very soon. Chris has terminal cancer, along with short-term memory loss that may be
a result of either the disease or the morphine he’s using to treat his pain.

Chris’ trip to the remote tourist site is both a dying man’s last wish and a convenient location
for that man’s planned execution. You see, Chris and Lee’s boss at the CIA, the spectacularly
surly MJ (Steve Buscemi), has decided that Chris knows too many secrets to be left alive even for
the brief time he has left. A veteran agent who has been with the CIA since the beginning of the
Cold War, Chris is now bent on writing a memoir of his life in spycraft, and is already dictating
highly sensitive secrets into a recorder he carries with him everywhere. Not only that, but he has
long been rumored to possess a rifle that played a key role in the Kennedy assassination, and MJ is
determined to find that weapon before the frail spook kicks the bucket. So Lee—who over the course
of a decade of bickering partnership has developed a deep if thorny friendship with the jaded
Chris—is saddled with the miserable task of spying on his own mentor before taking him out of the
game forever.

At the airport, Lee and Chris have a brief, prickly exchange with a pair of young Chilean women,
Monica (Mariana di Girolamo) and Alicia (Ailín Salas). These left-wing student activists are put
off when Chris, his tongue loosened by a combination of cognitive decline and I’m-dying-anyway
bravado, boasts about being an American spy. The women at first seem to take this elderly tourist
for a crank, an impression encouraged by Lee’s strategic eye-rolls. But once Lee and Chris check
in to their side-by-side rooms on the island, they realize that the women are also on a tourist
visit to Rapa Nui—the “Indigenous” name they use for the island, to Chris’ open mocking.
“You shouldn’t even know the word _Indigenous_ at your age,” he scoffs, as the beleaguered Lee
tries to end the conversation.

MALKOVICH IS A REVELATION IN THE KIND OF ROLE HE HAS TOO RARELY GOTTEN THE CHANCE TO PLAY OVER A
50-YEARCAREER.
_Wild Horse Nine_ is part black-humored buddy comedy, part existential Western (Lee’s cowboy hat
and the roaming packs of wild horses lend the setting a John Ford vibe), and part satirical critique
of American imperialism. It’s that last element that feels the most awkwardly wedged in, such that
McDonagh’s treatment of the Global South’s violent colonial history can come off as troublingly
glib. But you don’t come to a McDonagh movie for sophisticated sociohistorical analysis. You come
to watch two guys busting each other’s chops in the funniest, rudest, most melancholy and profound
manner possible.

This writer-director specializes in a style of comic dialogue that’s heard more often onstage than
in film—speech that’s slightly heightened, dependent on repetition and rhythm in a way everyday
conversation isn’t. The reliably brilliant Rockwell has now starred or co-starred in three
McDonagh films, so he is a past master at delivering the director’s trademark pitter-pat badinage.
But Malkovich, new to the McDonagh-verse, is a revelation in the kind of role he has too rarely
gotten the chance to play over a 50-year career: not a menacingly icy villain or an eccentric
oddball but a fragile, complex, deeply guilty man whose long-abandoned Catholic faith has him
questioning, as death approaches, what kind of eternity he will face after a lifetime of propping up
dictators and engineering political assassinations. Malkovich has been blessed, or cursed, with a
distinctive voice that tends to make his every utterance sound ironic. Here he deploys that
instrument in an unexpected way, almost whispering some lines, pausing in unexpected places during
others, finding the poetry in riffs about everything from the ethics of killing moths to the
connection between America’s history of colonial domination and its inferior prepackaged cheese.
Malkovich is currently the favorite for Best Actor in predictions of this year’s Oscar
race—though he’s been nominated twice, he’s never won—and much as I dislike reducing
performances as artful as this one to statistics on a betting site, I would be surprised and
disappointed to see the award go to anyone else this year.

Though _Wild Horse Nine_ is the kind of movie that relies more on dialogue than on imagery, its
striking setting on Rapa Nui gives the film a memorable visual simplicity, as the doomed Chris and
the tormented Lee play out the final days of their friendship amid seaside cliffs presided over by
the stone-faced figures of the Moai. For all the talk of hidden rifles, bombs in briefcases, and
plots to overthrow democracy, the story seems to be unfolding in a mythical landscape that makes it
feel more like a moral allegory than a spy thriller. In McDonagh’s best work—as in the work of
the Irish playwright he seems most indebted to, Samuel Beckett—the sacred and the profane become
inextricable as the characters’ conversations ping-pong between existential reflections on
mortality and raunchy insult comedy. If nothing else, see _Wild Horse Nine_ for the final scene,
with Lee and Chris trading loopy reflections on the lyrics to a Hank Williams song as they sit at
the base of an implacable rock sculpture, waiting—just as we all are—to see how the end
they’ve been expecting all this time will finally come.
