The Anthony Bourdain Biopic Is Delicious

====================================================================================================

The Canadian filmmaker Matt Johnson has been a presence on the indie scene since 2013, putting out a
string of well-received low-to-mid-budget comedies without ever attaining (or, it seems, seeking)
the profile of a mainstream director. His first two features, _The Dirties_ and _Operation
Avalanche_, were microbudget indies filmed guerrilla-style around his native Toronto and starring or
co-starring Johnson himself, a shambling presence who specializes in playing deluded dreamers.
2023’s _Blackberry_ slyly subverted the “biopic of a startup” formula of movies like _The
Social Network_ and _Air_ to tell the deglamorized real-life story of the rapid rise and steep fall
of the proto-smart device named in the title. And earlier this year there was the hard-to-describe
and even-harder-to-resist buddy comedy _Nirvanna the Band the Show the Movie_, the final form of a
project that began as a web series before being turned into a series for Canadian TV and finally
developed for the big screen. If any through line has tied together Johnson’s projects to date,
it’s the writer-director’s nimbleness at remixing familiar genre elements in fresh ways. His
comic tone tends toward the satirical, but it’s a gentle kind of satire, with one eye always
trained on the human drama beneath the antic proceedings.

Johnson’s latest film, _Tony_, has him working for the first time in a more conventional genre
mode: It’s a coming-of-age biopic, the origin story of a famous figure whose life seems rich
enough to contain several more movies within it. _Tony_, loosely based on the early chapters of
Anthony Bourdain’s bestselling 2000 memoir _Kitchen Confidential_, portrays one formative summer
in the life of the 19-year-old celebrity-chef-to-be. This is the first Matt Johnson film in which
the director does not appear as an actor, though he still co-produced the movie and co-wrote the
script, in collaboration with Matthew Miller, Todd Bartels, and Lou Howe. After Dominic Sessa (Paul
Giamatti’s memorable teenage foil in Alexander Payne’s _The Holdovers_)was cast as the young
Bourdain, Sessa and Johnson embarked on another round of rewrites, hammering out changes to the
screenplay as they tried to adapt the book’s quick sketch of the teenage Bourdain into a character
substantial enough to have a movie-length arc of his own.

In a phrase from early in the book that’s quoted in the movie’s epigraph, Bourdain describes his
college-dropout self as “a spoiled, miserable, narcissistic, self-destructive and thoughtless
young lout, badly in need of a good ass-kicking.” Johnson has said that the movie is intended to
be that ass-kicking. The boy we meet in _Tony_’s opening scenes fits Bourdain’s self-description
to a T, while remaining so evidently lost and vulnerable that we can’t help but root for him to
find a way out of the dark place where he’s landed. Tony’s depression, dissatisfaction, and
restlessness don’t seem to spring from any direct cause we’re shown in the movie. His parents
(Dagmara Domińczyk and Rich Sommer) seem stable and loving enough, even if his brainy mom is a
little judgy of her son’s inability to pronounce _bildungsroman_ with the proper German flair.
When Tony, back in suburban New Jersey after his second year at Vassar, fails to win a writing
fellowship he’d been counting on, he simply lies to his family that he did win the prize, steals
the contents of his father’s wallet, and takes off for Provincetown, Massachusetts. He chooses
that destination on a whim, solely because Nancy (Emilia Jones), the classmate on whom Tony has an
unrequited crush, has mentioned that she’s working there for the summer.

The real-life Bourdain had at least marginally better planning skills than his cinematic double. As
he tells it in _Kitchen Confidential_, his first summer in the Cape Cod beach town involved renting
a shared house with friends. But within hours of arrival, movie Anthony has 1) learned that the
object of his longing already has a boyfriend, 2) gotten drunk, 3) been kicked out of a restaurant
for hitting on a waitress, and 4) wound up passed out on the sidewalk outside said restaurant, where
he is promptly robbed of all his cash. The proprietor of the place, Ciro (Antonio Banderas), takes
pity on the feckless youth and lets him spend the night in the hammock outside his beachside
cottage. The next morning, Ciro wakes Tony up with a bucket of seawater to the face, informing him
that if he can show up every morning on time, he can have a job washing dishes at the Flagship, an
old-fashioned seafood joint on the town’s main tourist strip.

For the sake of narrative compression, the three summers Bourdain spent working in Provincetown
kitchens have been turned into a single season at one restaurant. The kitchen staff, too, is reduced
from the book’s sprawling cast of louche, swaggering knife-slingers down to a few key players.
There’s Stavros (the comedian Stavros Halkias), a witheringly sarcastic line cook who immediately
sees through Tony’s false claims of restaurant experience; Tyrone (Michael Jibrin), a stoic chef
whose burned and scarred hands require no oven mitt to handle red-hot cookware; and Sal (Leo
Woodall), a prep cook with a hair-trigger temper who becomes Tony’s mentor in the after-hours
world of Provincetown debauchery. Together the two go on drunken joyrides, attempt to pick up
women—an effort at which only Sal finds success—and smoke, snort, or, in Sal’s case, inject
whatever illegal substances come their way. A montage of Tony and Sal’s exploits, which are not
just self- but other-destructive, gives the audience a vicarious adrenaline rush, even as we worry
for these young men’s physical and mental safety—a concern that proves justified, as first Sal
and then Tony face the harsh consequences of their antisocial behavior.

The movie’s central relationship turns out to be the one between Tony and the circumspect
restaurateur Ciro, who lets the kid extend his stay on his porch for the remainder of the summer and
gradually begins to trust him with more of the work in the Flagship kitchen. It’s the chemistry
between Banderas and Sessa that makes this gentle, low-stakes story—a summer movie in which, for a
refreshing change, no one is murdered or menaced by a supernatural threat—simmer along like a
cauldron of chowder. Ciro’s quiet dedication to his profession, even at a joint where the menu
seldom goes beyond fried fish and oysters on the half shell, slowly breaks down the brittle shell of
Tony’s adolescent nihilism. Banderas’ performance is a quiet miracle; we learn virtually nothing
about his character’s past until two-thirds of the way into the movie, and even then get only a
glimpse into his backstory. But Banderas shows us by example what Ciro’s core values are, and
they’re the same ones that will motivate the adult Bourdain: curiosity, hospitality, and an
insatiable appetite for food, drink, and good companionship.

A late plot development involving the fate of Ciro’s restaurant feels a bit like a contrivance
invented to give the final act a jolt of suspense. But it’s hard to hold a grudge against this
lovely, lyrical small movie, which may break no new cinematic ground but which also gets almost
every detail right. The lush cinematography (by Michael Bauman, who also shot Paul Thomas
Anderson’s _One Battle After Another_) beautifully evokes the atmosphere of P-town’s bohemian
beach-bum culture in the summer of 1975. The moody piano score is by Jay McCarrol, the Canadian
musician, composer, and actor whom Matt Johnson fans will recognize as the director’s longtime
close friend and collaborator on both sides of the camera. The vintage needle drops, too, are as
choice as they come, including the Brothers Johnson’s “Strawberry Letter 23” and an especially
well-placed usage of Walter Murphy and the Big Apple Band’s disco rendition of Beethoven’s Fifth
Symphony.

Eight years after his still-shocking death by his own hand, Anthony Bourdain remains a cult figure
whose work remains as beloved as his final act was tragic. This sets the bar near impossibly high
for any actor attempting to play him, but the 23-year-old Dominic Sessa rises to that challenge with
a performance of brilliant understatement and slow-burning charisma. It’s easy to forget, watching
this bright but troubled young man struggle to find his place in the world, that he will grow up to
be a figure so many people looked to for guidance on how to forge their own paths. Even if _Tony_
were not a biopic at all, but simply the fictional story of one screwed-up kid’s transformative
summer working in a restaurant kitchen, it would be a movie worth watching. But in the closing
scene, when the slightly older and wiser protagonist introduces himself to an esteemed chef (played
by real-life Bourdain buddy Eric Ripert) using his full name, there’s a shiver of recognition as
we remember who it was he was becoming all along.
