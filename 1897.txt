John Mulaney Had One Moment at the Emmys Too Hot for NBC. It Was the Best Part of the Show.

====================================================================================================

Move over, ladies, because tonight is a night for oppressed men! Or so John Mulaney would have you
think with his crowd-crushing bit at the 78th Primetime Emmy Awards. The ceremony—which aired on
NBC on Monday night instead of the usual Sunday to avoid a scheduling conflict with _Sunday Night
Football_—had a lot of surprising moments. For starters, the evening was hosted not by a comedian
but by the one and only Mariska Hargitay, the longtime _Law & Order: SVU_ star lately known for her
supporting role on the sideline of the Knicks’ championship run. Then a bunch of other unexpected
things happened: _The Diplomat_’s Allison Janney won best supporting actress in a drama over _The
Pitt_’s Katherine LaNasa; an award was presented in High Valyrian; and Megan Stalter of _Hacks_
was dressed like an Emmy, which is to say she was painted head to toe in gold body paint, which
must’ve made for an interesting shower later on.

But the most shocking moment of the evening was Mulaney absolutely stealing the show with what may
be the funniest bit ever performed to present an award.

Mulaney went onstage to hand out the trophy for Outstanding Lead Actor in a Drama Series, but
instead of doing it straight or performing a stale skit, the stand-up comedian decided to dedicate a
few minutes to a tongue-in-cheek speech bringing awareness to men’s oppression in Hollywood.
“Finally something for the fellas,” Mulaney started. “What a crop of prom kings we have.
They’re not soft like the dad from _Bluey_, no.” He deadpanned: “Since the creation of
television, men have been historically underrepresented,” a line that nominee Sterling K. Brown
seemed to find particularly hilarious.

“For the first three years TV existed, there were no men on it. It was just beauty contests and
children talking to hand puppets, until the early ’60s, until a man walked out and said,
‘Enough! My name is Fonzie, and the president’s been shot!’ ” Mulaney joked, playing on
Henry Winkler’s _Happy Days_ character and Gaeton Fonzi, who investigated the Kennedy
assassination.

“But let’s talk about these bros tonight, these sweat hogs,” he pivoted, before ribbing on the
annoyance of male actors’ propensity for method acting, saying, “Some of the men in this
category do method acting, and some do regular, less annoying acting.” Mulaney didn’t stop
there, going on to mock the variety of roles the nominees of the category represent, stating, “And
they can play anything, these nominees, not just doctors. They can play a cop, or an FBI agent, or a
secret agent, and then that’s it, no other examples.”

But that wasn’t the real moment that stopped the show or the one that seemed to send nominee Noah
Wyle’s wife hiding for her life. No, the crown jewel of Mulaney’s bit were his props and his
references, especially his reiteration—which will go down in Emmys history—of an infamous debate
sparked by the hip-hop producer DJ Khaled: “I believe it was DJ Khaled who said, ’Real men
don’t go down on their wives.’ But these nominees know that that is BS. They know there’s
often less pressure on them if they do that first.” (Hear, hear! The Emmys, naturally, bleeped the
moment.)

The gags didn’t stop there. Mulaney then pulled out a book, which he had borrowed from the
Glendale Public Library. That book was, of course, a self-help treatise written by Frank
Vincent—known for his work with Martin Scorsese and on _The Sopranos_—who in 2006 published _A
Guy’s Guide to Being a Man’s Man_ (the cover of which is exactly what you’d expect it to be).
Mulaney read an excerpt from the text: “A man’s man can be wearing the best clothes, drinking
the finest wine, sitting with the most beautiful woman in Las Vegas, but if he doesn’t treat
everyone around him with respect and class, he’s not a real man’s man, he’s just a jabroni in
a good suit.” And with no transition, Mulaney then announced the nominees.

Wyle would go on to win the award for his work on the hit medical procedural _The Pitt_, but Mulaney
would go on to win the night for his hilarious subversion of today’s culture wars debate about
misogyny and masculinity—_and_ for supporting his local library.
