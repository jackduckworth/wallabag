Blade Runner 2099 review – every scene of this TV version is packed with terrifying visual beauty | Television | The Guardian

====================================================================================================

Memory: the mother of the muses, and the heart of the Blade Runner franchise. Science fiction is
forever asking what it means to be human and, in the future dystopia first painted by Ridley Scott's
1982 movie, the answer is that we are what we remember: we are the experiences we preserve as a
collection of short films inside our heads. In the original Blade Runner, Rachael (Sean Young) is
devastated to learn that her childhood memories aren't real, and she is therefore a robot
"replicant"; in Denis Villeneuve's sequel, Blade Runner 2049, K (Ryan Gosling) knows he's a
replicant but hopes his memories are a sign that he is something close to human.

Blade Runner has always been relevant to the age of AI, in that its starting point is that enslaved
robots have rebelled. But the new version, the story's first expansion into television, is acutely
topical in an era where we're increasingly anxious about whether what we're seeing – or reading
– is the work of machines. Fifty years on from the events of the last movie, the replicants have
won: "Blade runners" are now replicants hunting down disobedient humans rather than the other way
around, and the capture and storage of human memories is illegal. As humanity dies, what it
remembers has become valuable contraband.

Since it is ultimately about the video fragments in our minds, Blade Runner has, from the start,
presented its conception of the future as more of a mood than a story. One of the many things Blade
Runner 2099 creator/writer Silka Luisa gets right is keeping that allusive wooziness, prioritising
images over details and vibes over the desire to build a complete, logically functioning world. It's
important to, uh, remember that before diving in.

But what images, and what vibes! Switch off all the lights and get drunk on Blade Runner 2099's rich
neons and fathomless, paranoid shadows, its abandoned spaces and urban centres that teem like an
unfamiliar capital city too packed for you to find your bearings. Retro cathode-ray screens flicker;
dangling wires and botched concrete hang haphazardly from ceilings; to a soundtrack that mixes
shimmering synths with insistently soulful saxophone. Anyone unlucky enough to be alive in 2099,
human or not, determinedly copes as well as they can with everything being broken, while dreaming of
something better.

Our protagonists are Olwen (Michelle Yeoh), a veteran blade runner nearing the end of her lifespan,
and Cora (Hunter Schafer), a human posing as a replicant who infiltrates the blade-running operation
and becomes Olwen's sidekick. For different reasons that may not need to make perfect sense, both of
them have the same quest: to track down Olwen's former colleague/lover Marek (Johnny Harris) –
who, along with a group of replicants, appears to have gone rogue.

Blade Runner 2099 is a fear-of-discovery thriller, with Cora constantly required to react to
situations in an authentically replicant way on pain of death, and an Andor-style political
allegory, as a fragile network of rebellious humans plot their resistance, swapping intel in rainy
alleyways and backstreet bars. Like Andor, it likes to throw in a British actor when it needs a bit
of extra irony or intrigue: as well as Harris there's Tom Burke, Daniel Rigby and, as an inscrutable
administrator who enjoys being unhelpful from within a scratched glass booth, Tim Key.

The striking visuals are indelibly vivid, from the backstreet pleasure parlour where replicants
cosplay as humans, hiring skin suits with puckered flesh and distended pregnancy bellies, to the way
humans placed under arrest have their heads smothered by sacks with built-in intubation tubes. Death
scenes include a forced hanging after a crunching hand-to-hand fight, and an eerily pretty
immolation. Every scene has something gorgeously unsettling.

As Olwen and Cora's shared mission progresses, it becomes clear – not very clear, but as near to
it as Blade Runner has ever felt the need to be – that their quest is an emotional one, since they
are both chasing elusive memories. The franchise tradition for grandly philosophical dialogue,
perched just on the edge of absurdity, is kept up: "You're so alone you're not a person any more,"
Cora is told as she's sent under cover, while Olwen's summary of her own insignificance is a smart
twist on the immortal "tears in rain" monologue delivered by Rutger Hauer's dying replicant in the
first movie. "I've been to the stars," she says, wearily. "I know how small I am. Even the sun
dies."

So it's not a laugh a minute, and perhaps Blade Runner 2099 could have done slightly more to conform
to an episodic structure without losing its elliptical end-times cool. But its beautiful doomsday
visions are unforgettable.
