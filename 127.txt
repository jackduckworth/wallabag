A Guest at Miley Cyrus’ Wedding Wore the Exact Wrong Thing. Good.

This past Saturday, at Miley Cyrus and Maxx Morando’s wedding, the guests wore black. Or actually,
of the approximately 110 attendees, over 99 percent wore black. One woman did not.

Seated among the other guests in a gleaming saffron gown with full sleeves worthy of the
Renaissance, she stuck out like a little jewel nestled among some poppyseeds. This fashion faux pas
fomented a combination of fury, disbelief, shock, and laughter in the days that followed. “Showing
up to a wedding out of the requested dress code is soooo tacky,” wrote one person on X. “I would
honestly leave and go home!!” said another. Others called the look “rude” and
“embarrassing.”

Weddings are some of the only places where dress codes still have power—and therefore one of the
only places where violating the code can cause a true upset. Elsewhere, it seems not to matter.
Office dress codes are dying out, school uniforms have practically disappeared, and the
once-stringent sartorial rules laid out by airlines, restaurants, and venues are rarely enforced.
Most of the time in mainstream America, you can’t _really_ wear the wrong thing. There are fuzzy
slippers in the grocery store aisles, they shuffle around alongside shiny patent-leather heeled
boots; and the people wearing both barely glance at each other. This makes for fascinating aesthetic
scenarios, but it also means that, save for extreme examples, norm-breaking feels a little
unimaginable.

At the same time, wedding dress codes have become more specific and rigid, often to the point of
parody. They’re like theme parties: “Western romance” and “disco heaven” and “barn
chic.” Dress codes from one wedding to the next might not share anything in common, except that
they’re overplanned, overdetermined, and often tensely controlled. Weddings are the rare instance
where it’s socially acceptable to be this exacting—you’re king for a day, so you get to
enforce the wardrobe. If you want “elevated garden party” in the morning and “beach goth
rave” at night, that’s exactly what you should get.

But, kings should be warned that the more tightfisted the control, the more evident and powerful
little rebellions and defiances will be. When we require rigid dress codes for weddings, we actually
_invite_ transgressions—and this is the most fun, frivolous, low-stakes drama there is! When
there’s clear disobedience, you can be like, “_Did you see that? His shirt was not a gentle
pastel. We were specifically told gentle pastels! Gasp!_” I love to gasp. We rarely gasp these
days. And the yellow dress made me—and the rest of the internet—do just that.

There is some mixed reporting about whether this guest, Belgica Vargas, knew about the all-black
dress code—so far, it seems she had no idea. Or maybe she did. Either way, Cyrus was lucky to have
her. Every wedding should be so fortunate. A wardrobe faux pas is a great social service—it bonds
the rest of us and gives us something to talk about. What could possibly make me care about Miley
Cyrus’ wedding? Someone breaking Miley Cyrus’ rules, of course!

It all reminds me of a comment about wedding dress codes from an interview with filmmaker and
comedian Julio Torres. His primary fashion rule, he told New York magazine, is to wear whatever he
wants, at all times. He doesn’t own a tux, a suit, or a tie. He doesn’t believe in dress codes.
If a restaurant has one, he intentionally violates it. For weddings, he insists, “I show up
wearing whatever the hell I want, if I go at all.”

I adore people like Torres, because it could never be me. I constitutionally fall in line with dress
codes and the general desires of the people getting married. (I _really_ understand the desire for a
cohesive photo.) I also _live_ to getdressed up, I love a theme, and I love black tie. And yet,
these dress codes can and should be broken. _Someone_ has to be the Torres of the event. Let me
gorge on the defiance! Preferably from afar!

That said, there’s a lot less intrigue when the wild card knows that they’re breaking the code,
or is seeking attention. (Torres doesn’t _want_ to, for example: He _must_. That’s his creative
principle. We _expect_ him to break the rules.) Celebrities, who famously love attention, do this
all the time, and to little fanfare (Dua Lipa, for example, has worn white as a guest to _multiple_
weddings). It’s also not that interesting when the dress code is broken because someone is too
lazy to figure it out. No, the disregard must either be accidental or have the force of personality
in order to be exceptional. I know someone who went to a wedding where one of the betrothed’s
mothers Oedipally wore a _barely_ off-white dress. I asked this person if she thought that the
mother was aware of what she was doing. “Not at all,” she replied. “Everyone else was!” As
was the case with the yellow dress, it likely wasn’t intentionally attention-seeking. But there
will always be the question. That’s what makes it the perfect conversation piece.

When dress code violations like these break the social construct, it confirms there was a construct
to begin with. Shouldn’t that make us want to stick a finger in the pie? I think the desire for a
wedding to be a perfect day, a therefore flawless and frictionless day, is strange. It is an
_important_ day, and important days should bring out the wobbly, the daring, the unexpected, the
messy, the romantic, the bursting with emotion, the big reckonings, and the disruptions. Weddings,
having become far less binding than they used to be, are basically modern performance art. (Comedian
Jacqueline Novak once called weddings “American Kabuki”—the _real_ theater, the only one we
have.) And what’s a performance without a little drama?

The reports from the guest’s sister were that the fellow guests were gracious about her fashion
choice. Though, no one was as gracious as Cyrus herself, who reportedly told the woman: “You look
gorgeous!” The chance to demonstrate your regal benevolence to your disrespectful, clueless
subjects on the day of your very own nuptials? What a great wedding gift.
