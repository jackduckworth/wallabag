

====================================================================================================

Ted Lasso season four review – TV's most wholesome football comedy is back

====================================================================================================

Having transported a flailing AFC Richmond to the upper echelons of the Premier League with his
wholesome, folksy and highly simile-reliant management style, the finale of Ted Lasso's third season
saw our titular coach return to the US to spend more time with his son in Kansas. At the time
(2023), the general impression was that we were waving goodbye for good. But could this intensely
American protagonist really do the most British thing of all and bow out at the height of his
sitcom's popularity? Nope. Last year it was announced that its star and co-creator Jason Sudeikis
had caved to market forces and was reprising his colossally popular alter ego.

It was the logical thing to do: the 11-time Emmy-winning show was the most-watched streaming
original of 2023. Not that logic features heavily in this fourth season. Before we discover how Ted
has been lured back to south-west London, we find out what he's been up to in the interim. Did he
parlay his success into US soccer? Return triumphantly to college football? Sit back with his
millions and rest on his laurels? Nope, nope and nope again. Instead, he's working in his local
supermarket, warmly greeting his customers by name, providing moral guidance to younger colleagues
and generally radiating his trademark howdily-doodily energy. Why? Because he's a good man without
ego who loves to connect and make a positive difference in his community! This is Ted Lasso we're
talking about!

Back to the central head-scratcher. Ted left the UK at the top of his game, ultimately choosing
fatherhood over riches, professional triumph and beloved colleagues. What could possibly change his
mind? Richmond's owner Rebecca (Hannah Waddingham, as aspirationally imperious as ever), her deputy
Higgins (Jeremy Swift, as satisfyingly square as ever) and marketing manager Keeley (Juno Temple, as
unsettlingly childlike as ever) think they've cracked it: they fly to Kansas to offer him the job of
managing the women's team, who are languishing in the second division. Obviously, Ted declines. But
then his mother – the same mother who last season convinced him to come home – encourages him to
go back. So he asks his son and his estranged wife if they fancy coming with. With little
explanation, they agree.

If you are already a Ted Lasso ultra, you may be inclined to gloss over the twee nonsensicality of
this fourth season's setup and simply sink back into the show's anodyne atmosphere, one that
revolves around messages of positivity, togetherness and how good it is when your football team
wins. If you bristle at such blatant emotional button-pushing, you might come to another conclusion:
Ted Lasso is fake and phoney, to use the kind of Americanism this show loves putting into the mouths
of its British characters (who calls it "the John Lewis Christmas commercial"?!). From the vague
transatlanticism and fairytale London to the football-based inaccuracies and stiffly unreal vibe,
Ted Lasso rarely bears any relation to reality. This is not a show for pedants.

That said, the shift in focus to women's football does at least mean entertaining some hard truths.
Ted's new assistant coach Alice Chilton (Sex Education's Tanya Reynolds) is a dour taskmaster
furious at having been passed over for the top job in favour of a man. And although Ted quickly gets
to work in his signature style – improving morale by drilling down into the players' psyches –
his efforts are hampered by the chronic lack of investment in women's sport, plus injury, pregnancy,
motherhood and the fact he's barely allowed inside the changing room for propriety's sake.

Still, in the four episodes made available to reviewers, the tension never properly builds. Ted sees
the care and industriousness behind Alice's stony-faced exterior, which is destined to crack
imminently. Rather than establishing the new team as individuals (beyond one hard-as-nails goalie
and two squabbling Irish sisters), the show often falls back on comfortingly familiar dynamics: the
will-they-won't-they between Roy Kent, still manager of the men's team, and his ex Keeley among
them.

For all its irritatingly ersatz qualities, the appeal of Ted Lasso is as clear as ever: the
signposted comic beats, the predictable gestures towards kindness and reconciliation, the bright
colours, the clean lines. A sense of quality entertainment still hangs about the whole well-oiled
endeavour. Think of it as the TV equivalent of Taylor Swift: ruthlessly efficient, reassuringly
dorky, blatantly basic and as monocultural as any Apple TV offering is going to get. While I suspect
season four will shed some viewers – primarily those who prefer the glamour of the men's game over
the more injustice-focused angle any show about women's sport must take – fans will be relieved to
hear the show has by no means embraced gritty naturalism. Ted Lasso is still saccharine business as
usual and, no doubt, still a lot of people's cup of tea.
