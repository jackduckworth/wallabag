

====================================================================================================

The Raygun documentary ducks a big question about the Olympic fame of a 'dorky white academic'

====================================================================================================

Early in Netflix's new documentary, Untold Raygun: Breaking Badly, Australian B-girl Rachael Gunn,
recounts a session she had with her sports psychologist prior to the 2024 Paris Olympics.

"Right before the Olympics, I went to my therapist, panicked," she says. Her therapist asked her
what was the worst thing that could happen. And she said, "The whole world could laugh at me." Her
therapist responded: "Do you think that's reasonable?" Cue Gunn's hysterical laughter.

Anyone who has ever gone to a therapist will be amused at this anecdote. Nine and a half times out
10, the therapist would have been right. No, they'd say, the worst outcome you can imagine probably
won't come to pass. But in Gunn's case, what she feared the most did, in fact, happen. Her
shenanigans – the sprinkler, the kangaroo hops – in the cypher made her go viral almost as soon
as she was done with her sets. (Perhaps if Gunn had been explicit about what she was planning to do
in her sets, her therapist would have reacted differently.) Gunn's performance launched a thousand
memes, more than a couple of late-night TV segments, several conspiracy theories, and even a musical
at the Edinburgh Fringe Festival.

"She became the most famous breaker on planet Earth overnight," Flix, a fellow Australian B-girl,
says in the documentary. All without winning a single vote from any of the nine judges across her
six rounds.

Untold Raygun, which premieres globally on Netflix on Tuesday, really doesn't dwell on what it means
for breaking, an art form created by Black and Brown youth in the South Bronx, that its first
Olympic foray will be primarily – if not exclusively – remembered for a white woman from
Australia doing the sprinkler instead of the dynamic performances of the rest of the contenders.
It's mostly focused on Gunn herself with little time spent on the greater context of the art form
and how it ended up in the Olympics. The documentary features interviews with Gunn, her parents, her
husband who introduced her to breaking, and a few dancers who know her from the Aussie scene.
Through them, we get a coming of almost middle-age story about an academic who meets a B-boy working
at a call center, falls in love with him and the art form he's passionate about, and somehow ends up
centerstage at the Olympics.

[Australia's Rachael 'Raygun' Gunn competes during the women's breaking round-robin at the Paris
Olympics in August 2024.]
Gunn is likeable in the documentary. She comes across as (mostly) self-aware and self-deprecating.
Her parents are charming, laughing heartily when asked if they had ever previously associated
"Olympian" with their quirky daughter. Sammy Free seems like a sweet fellow who is completely
besotted with his wife. (We should all be so lucky to find someone who loves us the way that Free
clearly loves Gunn.) But the most useful voices in Untold Raygun are the two Australian B-girls who
offer some critical perspective on the place of women in the breaking scene and Gunn's performances
at the Games. They're harsh, at times, but also fair.

Tinylocks, one of Gunn's contemporaries, repeatedly invokes "No easy props," a term that's something
of a hip-hop ethos about having to prove your skill to earn respect. (The phrase is often associated
with American B-girl Asia One, who founded a crew called No Easy Props in 1997.) "It was such a male
dominated space and B-girls were really fighting to be seen but as more women started entering
breaking, it was felt that some girls were getting accolades too early in their careers. The crowd
would cheer just for participating," she says.

It is within the context of "no easy props" that Tinylocks offers her assessment of Raygun's
pre-Olympic performances, as she was coming up in the small Australian scene. "There were one or two
times when I was like, 'Oh yeah, that was fresh.' Maybe not so much the other times."

Tinylocks decided to pass on trying to earn the Oceania qualifying spot. "I'm interested in breaking
as an art form," she says, which is a sentiment I heard from other dancers in the scene after the
Olympic trajectory was announced by the IOC and the World DanceSport Federation (WDSF). Flix gave it
a go at the Oceania qualifier, but said that, at 41, she didn't expect much. Gunn was surprised that
she kept advancing through the bracket until she was in the final battle against B-girl Molly. If
you go back and watch the Oceania qualifier, you won't see any of Gunn's "iconic" moves from the
Paris Olympics in that final. What she threw down against Molly doesn't stand out in any way –
either for good or for bad. Yes, her movements were slow and lacked sharpness, her sets not
particularly intricate. But she showed fairly standard breaking foundation movements. If she had
done those sets in Paris, Gunn wouldn't have advanced out of the preliminary rounds but she also
wouldn't have become a global sensation.

[Rachael 'Raygun' Gunn competes during the women's breaking competition at the Paris Olympics, where
she failed to win a single vote from the nine judges across her six rounds.]
The documentary explains the change in her set composition: she realized how far behind she was the
top B-girls she was going to be battling at the Olympics. She went to a couple of European battles
in the months before the Games and performed poorly in all of them. Gunn and her husband decided to
change their approach. Instead of trying to up the difficulty content of her sets through power
moves – the acrobatics like headspins and windmills that laypeople associate with breaking –
they would go all in on originality, one of the five categories breaking judges evaluate at the
Games. (The other four were technique, vocabulary, execution and musicality.) They had gone from
hoping to win one battle or even one round to trying to win a single vote from one of the nine
judges. Gunn and Free said they looked to Australia for movement inspiration from its famous
wildlife (kangaroo) to its dance moves (the sprinkler). They even added a nod to swimming, an
Olympic sport at which Australia excels. Despite this strategy, Gunn didn't get a single vote in
Paris.

There's a world in which this approach could have worked for Gunn but it's a world where she is much
better at breaking fundamentals, where her footwork is sharp and clean, her movements swift but also
unhurried. The breaking scene has had its share of well-respected dancers who bring humor and
personality to the cypher. B-boys and B-girls often call this "character." (In fact, one of them,
Jeffro, was competing on the men's side in Paris.) The issue with Gunn's attempts at character is
that those gimmicks were basically all she had. The bunny hop or the kangaroo weren't simply moments
in an otherwise proficient set that ticked other boxes, like technique, execution or musicality. The
gimmicks were the main event.

As a point of contrast, let's consider another athlete who became a meme during the Paris Games:
Stephen Nedoroscik. The pommel horse specialist doffed his glasses, Clark Kent style, before he
saluted the judges. There the tenor of the online discourse had none of the mockery that suffused
the Raygun tweets because Nedoroscik was actually world class in his event. He was the 2021 world
champion on pommel horse and won bronze in the event at the Olympics. Something similar took place
in the sport shooting event where Turkey's Yusuf Dikec became a meme due to his nonchalant stance
and lack of gear. But because he was actually good at his sport – he won silver – the online
hijinks never turned mean.

"People have a lot of opinions about what women do, what women should do, sometimes from women,"
Gunn says of the reaction online to her performance. Generally speaking, there is truth to this
assertion. Women do get singled out for criticism in a way that often feels disproportionate to the
offense. Gunn didn't commit a crime. She didn't murder anyone on live TV. And she didn't cheat her
way to the Olympics as many people falsely claimed after Paris. (This is one of the conspiracy
theories that Untold Raygun takes pains to dispel.) But it still felt disingenuous for Gunn to
invoke this defense as a response to her Olympic performance. The response was not simply about her
creative choices but also about the gap between herself and her competitors. She put herself on an
Olympic stage where spectators expect elite performance, not a professor who is simply doing her
best. The Olympics aren't really a vehicle for self-expression. They're supposed to be a vehicle for
excellence.

[Gunn poses for a portrait in Sydney in April 2024, four months before making her Olympic debut in
Paris.]
Gunn isn't the first person to compete at the Olympics whose skills are decidedly subpar. There was
Eddie the Eagle, the British ski jumper who competed at the 1988 Olympics in Calgary, who definitely
did not fly through the air with the greatest of ease. Eric Moussambani competed in swimming at the
2000 Games though he could barely swim the entire length of an Olympic pool. And in 2018, Elizabeth
Swaney, representing Hungary, did nothing of substance in the halfpipe competition, putting together
the most basic of runs. What distinguishes Gunn from the other Olympians is that they competed in
sports that were already well-established. Though they became well-known, they didn't become the
_face_ of their respective sports. But breaking was new to the Games in 2024 and most viewers
weren't familiar with it except from pop-culture products of the 80s. Gunn left Paris as the most
famous breaker in the world. The average person on the street couldn't tell you who won the breaking
competition in Paris but they can probably remember Raygun, if not by name, then at least by
description of what she did on the floor.

There was no way for Gunn to have predicted this outcome, even if she, on some level, feared it
before she got on the plane for France. But it would have been welcome to see her reckon with it and
what it means for the culture and breaking's chances of getting on the program of a future Games.
(The documentary doesn't mention this so I'll do it: that breaking won't appear at LA2028 has
nothing to do with Gunn. The decision was made months before she hopped across the cypher.)

Ultimately, Untold Raygun seeks to reassure the viewer that Gunn has survived her very public bout
with global humiliation with her spirit and sense of whimsy intact. "I'm a dorky white woman who is
an academic," she said. "And I'm OK with that."

Last week WDSF submitted breaking for consideration for the 2032 Olympics, which will be hosted by
Brisbane. If the local organizing committee decides to give breaking another shot at Olympic glory
then perhaps we'll see Gunn again, but hopefully not at the center of the cypher.
