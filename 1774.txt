Why Hayden Panettiere’s Sudden Death Is So Devastating

====================================================================================================

On Sunday evening, just before midnight on the East Coast, the world heard the shocking announcement
of the death of 36-year-old actress Hayden Panettiere from what is now being reported as an
“apparent overdose.”

To say that my generation—younger millennials and elder Gen Zers—grew up watching Panettiere
feels like an understatement. She was on our screens as our vicarious youthful representation at
every step of the way to adulthood. She was only 10 years old when she stole the hearts of audiences
by playing Sheryl Yoast, the football-obsessed daughter of a white football coach, in the beloved
Denzel Washington–led 2000 film _Remember the Titans_. She was 12 years old when she appeared in
the final season of the revered Y2K legal dramedy _Ally McBeal_ as the titular character’s
long-lost daughter. If you’re in the Disney Channel Original Movie generation, you might have
first seen Panettiere playing Bill Pullman’s daughter in 2004’s _Tiger Cruise_, a DCOM that
navigates 9/11 from aboard a naval ship.

Panettiere’s teen years on screen were just as prolific. It was her co-leading role in the 2005
cult-classic ice skating movie _Ice Princess_, where she stars opposite the late Michelle
Trachtenberg (who died of natural causes suddenly last year at 39), that cemented her as a star in
the eyes of young girls everywhere. It was her performance in the third film of the cheerleading
franchise _Bring It On_, 2006’s _Bring It On: All or Nothing_, that endeared her to my generation,
but especially Black viewers. In _All or Nothing_, which is largely considered the second-best
installment of the franchise, after the iconic first movie, Panettiere plays a teenager who is
forced to relinquish her title as cheer captain at her predominantly white school when she moves
from her 90210-coded affluent neighborhood to an inner-city, predominantly Black and Latino one. At
her new school, where she’s a Barbie-esque fish out of water, Panettiere’s character ends up
working with the captain of their cheerleading squad (played by a young Solange) to combine Black
dance styles, like krumping and hip-hop, into their routines to give them an edge on the
better-endowed competition. The narrative setup of _All or Nothing_ could feel trite and even
offensive, but, instead, it was refreshing to see Panettiere have the maturity to understand that,
in order to make the story work, she would have to start out by being the butt of a few jokes.

Even if you weren’t part of the generation that grew up identifying with Panettiere, it’s likely
you came across her work elsewhere. Making the jump from child and teen star, she would become known
as a standout part of the NBC superhero drama _Heroes_, which aired in the back half of the 2000s.
Her role in the show—as Claire Bennet, yet another high-school cheerleader, but this time with
regenerative powers—was the center of the show’s mantra: “Save the cheerleader, save the
world.” As she adjusted to her powers, hid from the villains trying to experiment on or kill her,
and learned that she was the key to saving the world, Claire was the audience’s proxy to the
show’s heightened setting. A few years later, Panettiere would come to find her footing again on
screen as Juliette Barnes in ABC’s musical drama _Nashville_, a character Panettiere would play
for most of her 20s. _Nashville_, which dramatized the troubling life of fictional country music
stars, gave Panettiere more to work with as an actress than ever before. Of course, she had to sing
as Juliette, a Carrie Underwood–type country-pop sensation who would arrive to take the crown of
reigning country music queen from Connie Britton’s character, Rayna James. But Juliette was more
like a star burning so bright it was constantly on the verge of imploding. The character was a
troubled young woman who was suffering from growing up with a drug-addicted mother, and from
experiencing eventual sexual exploitation, grief, postpartum depression, and even a turn as a cult
member when the show’s narrative eventually jumped the shark, as many soaps do.

Panettiere’s role in _Nashville_ was significant, both because it gave her meaty material to chew
on as an actress and because it seemingly best mirrored the dark sides of her real life. In her
memoir released just this May, _This Is Me: A Reckoning_, Panettiere recounted a great number of
struggles and incidents of abuse that are reminiscent of the many stories told by child actors whose
early introduction to stardom and the pressures of Hollywood created a difficult path to adulthood.
In her memoir, Panettiere confronted her battle with severe postpartum depression, intense alcohol
abuse used as a vice to quiet her trauma and depression, and an addiction to Klonopin. She also
detailed some deeply traumatic instances of abuse she faced in the industry, like being introduced
to “happy pills” by her management team at the age of 15, network executives kissing her without
her consent, and a deeply upsettingallegation about being taken onto a yacht when she was 18 years
old by someone she trusted, only for that person to purportedly place her in bed with an unnamed
older male celebrity. She bared a lot about her personal hardships as well, like the devastating but
necessary decision she made to give up full custody of her daughter to her ex-fiancé in 2018, the
pain of going through withdrawal while in treatment in 2020, how understandably difficult her
younger brother’s untimely death in 2023 was for her, and how she experienced abuse from her
ex-boyfriend.

For most of my life, Panettiere was the wondergirl who could do anything. On screen, she was a
top-tier ice skater, a country music star, a cheerleader, a superhero. She was the girl everyone
desired in the 2009 teen rom-com _I Love You, Beth Cooper_, and she was a hailed final girl in the
_Scream_ franchise. In an industry where harsh scrutiny is more of a guarantee than a theatrical
release, Panettiere proved once again that she could do the impossible, braving the public eye this
year with her lengthy list of bombshell revelations, many of which could have ruined her career
forever. Her divulgences in her memoir, her return to the spotlight—all of this felt like her
coming clean so she could come back in some way, potentially blessing our screens once more. As _One
Tree Hill_ actress Bethany Joy Lenz, a longtime friend of Panettiere’s, expressed on Instagram in
bewilderment following the news of Panettiere’s death: “Everything seemed to be turning around.
She was coming BACK to life, wasn’t she?”

Going back decades, Panettiere played the friend who was always there, the girl’s girl we all
hoped we’d meet at school. As an adult, she was a complicated woman—both onscreen and off—but
we loved her because of that, not in spite of it. I had hoped, as I’m sure many others did, that
she would return to portray, yet again, another complicated woman, a character that she would
approach with care and without shame or judgment. Whether she was portraying a white girl trying to
krump, a misunderstood ice skater, or a famous woman on the brink, Panettiere brought something real
to the role. She was effervescent, and in our collective memory, she will be forever more.
