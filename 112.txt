

====================================================================================================

'One area of the tent was known as fart corner': what it's really like to be on The Great British Bake Off

====================================================================================================

The Great British Bake Off is just about to finish its best season in years. Like a cloud of Swiss
meringue buttercream on a slightly sunken cake, Nigella Lawson has added glamour and indulgence to a
format that needed saving. But this year's contestants have brought the joy, too: talented,
charismatic individuals who have interrupted their lives to spend three months with bits of dough
stuck to their elbows and indigestion from all the recipe testing.

We spoke to five previous contestants to find out why they put themselves through it, and what they
feel they achieved in the process. The scale runs from "a very nice level of X-list celebrity" to "a
better version of me", with a lot of life lessons (and a fart corner) along the way.

'I BAKED A CAKE FOR JIMMY CHOO'S BIRTHDAY'

SYABIRA YUSOFF – _Series 13, winner_

[Syabira Yusoff with her showstopper on The Great British Bakeoff.]
A couple of years after I moved to the UK [from Malaysia] I watched an episode of Bake Off where
they were making brownies for the technical challenge. I thought: "I can make brownies, I can be on
the show." That's it! I didn't know how intense it is and how crazy it was going to be. No
expectation, nothing.

[After I won] people started asking me: "Could you be like Nadiya Hussainwith her TV show?" and I
was thinking: "Yeah, maybe I'll give it a shot." I did try getting more involved on social media and
putting myself out there, but the more I did it, the more I missed my job [in genetic research]. I
decided to go back to work and maybe one day open a bakery or something. But for now I need
stability and sanity! I like to dream really big, but being a researcher suits me better than being
in the spotlight.

I enjoy doing food festivals – a full tent with everybody watching you baking gives you an
adrenaline rush. I got invited to Buckingham Palace to see the queen and the king – I still have
the invitation with my handwritten name on it in my drawer. I didn't bake for them, but I did make a
cake for Jimmy Choo, the shoe designer, for his birthday last year. It was a black forest pandan
cake. He paid well!

I now understand when people say "believe in yourself", because if I hadn't trusted myself in the
middle [of the series] when I hit rock bottom, I don't think I would have come out as a winner. Not
only did I get flowers and a cake stand, I came out as a better me.

'I'M NOW A LOCAL CELEBRITY – I DO WOMEN'S INSTITUTE TALKS'

ABBI LAWSON – _Series__ 14, __10th place_

[Abbi Lawson smiles as she mixes dough with a spatula on the show.]
I have always entered various baking categories in our village show, and the theme one year was its
70th anniversary. I made a cake of the village show marquee with all the entries inside and sheep
outside and it won best in show. That's what gave me the confidence to apply for Bake Off.

There were a lot of tears when I was practising. I'd given myself the extra challenge of using
seasonal, foraged ingredients, so I was having to practise with substitutes. It's a lot of work!
It's really fun, but you're trying to fit that in around everything else in your life, and you can't
tell anyone.

After I went out on bread week, I made a different type of bread every week for a year. It was
really hard to not feel as if I'd let myself down. It's a really stressful environment, and there's
so much going on. I learned a lot about my passions and my creativity; my career path is much more
food-focused than I ever thought it would be.

I'm now a local celebrity. I live in a rural area, so there's not loads going on, but I do Women's
Institute talks, I do stuff at the county show. I'm really happy. I didn't want to leave my veg box
delivery job behind, I didn't want to be going down to London every week to do TV appearances. It's
a very nice level of X-list celebrity.

'I'VE ENDED UP DECORATING MY HOUSE LIKE A GIANT CAKE'

KIM-JOY– _Series __nine, runner-up_

[Kim-Joy at work in the Bake Off tent.]
Because I grew up with selective mutism, I always felt as if I needed to get to my future self. I
felt very trapped in my head, so every single thing I did was about challenging that. Bake Off was
almost like exposure therapy, like if you're scared of snakes and you put yourself in a tank of
snakes. Although Bake Off is much better than a tank of snakes.

I was terrified the first week. I cried the whole time during the showstopper challenge, for no
reason. They kept asking me why I was crying, and I was like: "No reason really, just leave me to
it." They didn't put it in the edit, which I'm glad about. Last year I got an autism diagnosis,
which possibly tied into it: the unfamiliarity was particularly difficult, and one of the things I
found hardest was how bright and how hot it was.

Bake Off is the best thing I've ever done. I'm writing graphic novels now, working on another baking
book, going to comic conventions. I've managed to get into interior design, decorating my house like
a giant cake. That's not to say everything is perfect. I have anxiety and depression so it's hard.
But I'm very grateful for all the opportunities, and I try to just embrace that and do as much as
possible.

'I GOT OFF THE TRAIN AND 20 PEOPLE SURROUNDED ME'

CHRISTIAAN DE VRIES – _Series 15, runner-up_

[Christiaan de Vries smiles as he poses for a press shot that shows him sieving some flour into a
bowl.]
My boyfriend and I started watching Bake Off on Sundays when we were a bit tired and hungover. I
worked as a fashion designer at Alexander McQueen, but when my whole team was made redundant I
started baking. I watched Matty win series 14 and then on the screen it said: "Do you want to be in
the tent?" I was like: "You know what? I've got nothing to lose. For lols, let's just sign up." I
didn't even think I was going to get through to the second round.

In fashion you would always hear, "This is not good enough, scrap this, redo that," and that's what
I was expecting with Bake Off too. I was bracing for impact all the time, but it's not like that at
all. Everyone is really supportive and it has done a great deal for my self confidence.

But in the moment I was just really stressed! Sometimes Prue and Paul would be like, "I don't like
this," when I really loved it, and my mother-in-law, my boyfriend, and my friends had tasted it and
said it was great. That was such a big life lesson for me. I don't care any more, basically.

Everything that's happened since has been a rollercoaster. When the show was on, I remember getting
off the train at Victoria in London and one person recognised me, and then another person came to
stand with them for a photo, and then another one, and all of a sudden there were 20 people around
me. It was insane. I mean, I enjoyed it. I'm not gonna lie. But it was crazy.

'SOME AWFUL PERSON SAID WE WERE THE YEAR WITH THE WORST TEETH'

HOWARD MIDDLETON – _Series __four__, __seventh place_

[Howard Middleton in a tuxedo.]
As a child I was bullied – the enamel never formed on my teeth so they were discoloured. When I'd
been working for a while I could afford to have them done, and that gave me a lot of confidence.
Bake Off was a way of challenging myself; I didn't need to keep my mouth closed any more. Although
some awful person did review our year as being the year that had the worst teeth generally …

Within 20 minutes of being in the tent, I managed to cut my thumb quite badly. Off screen I went to
A&E. Then, in week two, Sue Perkins accidentally put her elbow in my muffin during a technical
challenge, and then week three was Custardgate ... just my luck, really. But I was thinking: "It's
good TV."

Part of the reason people do TV is to get the behind-the-scenes experience, and I heard the full
uncensored version of Mel and Sue. The rudeness of them! Swearing at Paul and things like that. Sue
is openly rude, Mel is a bit more subtle.

There was an area of the tent which was affectionately known as fart corner. Mel had a vegetarian
diet or something like that, so they created a little area where she could go and fart. At one
point, when the judging was taking place, she wandered off to the corner and wandered back and Mary
Berry's face was just … [grimaces].

I now do some food writing and host online classes with a company called Bake With a Legend – we
get so many people from America. The first demo I did I was so nervous, but then I realised people
aren't that bothered about what you're doing, they just want a bit of humour and a bit of
engagement. That was a revelation. People love it when things go wrong and I very rarely disappoint
them. I've found a niche.
