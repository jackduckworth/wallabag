The Avatar Debate That Needs to End

====================================================================================================

The most successful director in the history of movies has done it again. In its third weekend in
theaters, _Avatar: Fire and Ash_’s box-office take rose to $1.08 billion, making James Cameron the
first director with four movies to earn $1 billion or more. That’s not just four total but four in
a row, beginning with 1997’s _Titanic_, which means there are now people in their 30s who
weren’t born the last time one of Cameron’s movies failed to make 10 figures.

Film for film, that’s a level of consistent box-office dominance no other auteur can match: not
Steven Spielberg, not Christopher Nolan, not even the Russo brothers, the only directors other than
Cameron to have multiple movies in the all-time box-office Top 10, with their two _Avengers_movies.
And yet, even as someone who spends the last six weeks of the year fielding queries about what’s
worth catching up with, I can’t remember a single person asking me about the third entry in a
franchise that has sold hundreds of millions of tickets worldwide, defying widespread skepticism to
draw audiences back again and again. You can’t log onto the internet without hitting an elaborate
fan theory about the ending of _Stranger Things_, but apart from some sporadic online lusting for
Oona Chaplin’s warrior chieftain, you’d be hard-pressed to know that _Fire and Ash_ even exists,
let alone that it’s the biggest movie in the world.

THE PROBLEM WITH THE “NO CULTURAL IMPACT” ARGUMENT AROUND THE _AVATAR_ SERIES IS THAT IT IS
ALMOST ENTIRELY VIBES-BASED.
For nearly as long as the _Avatar_ movies have been atop the box-office charts, people have been
debating their lack of staying power. Not even five years after _Avatar_ became the highest-grossing
movie of all time, Forbes argued that the movie had left “no real pop culture footprint,” and in
2022, the New York Times set out to investigate “The Mystery of the Vanishing Blockbuster,” only
a few weeks before _The Way of Water_ began a box-office run that would take it to third place on
the all-time list. The second movie’s $2.3 billion gross, accrued 13 years after the first’s
$2.8 billion, would seem to have provided a definitive answer to the question “Does anyone still
care about _Avatar_?” And yet even after the third movie debuted with an opening weekend of nearly
$350 million, the internet was still swarming with people hot to make the case that the movies have,
to use their preferred phrase, “no cultural impact,” as an (I thought) innocuous Bluesky post
dropped over Christmas vacation quickly made clear.

The argument, if I can reproduce it fairly, goes something like this: Where are the quotes? Where
are the memes? When was the last time you heard someone drop an _Avatar_ reference, or one of its
concepts worked its way into everyday speech? Does anyone remember what happened in the last movie,
or even the names of its characters? Everyone knows Luke Skywalker, Iron Man, what it means to say
someone acts like a Slytherin. And yet the average person on the street couldn’t tell you one
thing about these supposedly globe-conquering movies. They have, to paraphrase a movie whose
cultural reach far outstrips its box-office rank, failed to make fetch happen.

The problem with the “no cultural impact” argument around the _Avatar_ series is that it is
almost entirely vibes-based. Scott Mendelson, the author of the 2014 Forbes article, didn’t
conduct a poll before asserting that “most general moviegoers couldn’t tell you the name of a
single character from the film, nor could they name any of the actors who appeared in it”—he
just presented it as fact. And while that might square with his experience, or even with mine, I
can’t help thinking of the legendary film critic Pauline Kael’s observation that she only knew
one person who voted to reelect Richard Nixon in 1972. The quote often gets recirculated as evidence
of the coastal bubble which mainstream cultural critics inhabit, but rarely with the knowledge that
that’s how Kael meant it in the first place. She didn’t mean that no one voted for Nixon, but
that those who did lay outside the “rather special world” in which she lived.

I don’t know anyone who’s obsessed with the _Avatar_ series, at least outside of colleagues who
are professionally required to pay attention to them. But I also don’t know anyone in mainland
China, where _Fire and Ash_ closed out the year dominating the box office, earning more than $137
million to date. What little quantitative inquiry has been done into _Avatar_’s cultural
impact—like the Ankler’s analysis of how Google searches for the franchise have declined over
time, or how the number of fan wiki pages devoted to its lore fail to measure up—focuses
exclusively on English-language culture, or at the very least doesn’t provide any specifics about
the series’ profile in other countries.

What we do know, beyond a shadow of a doubt, is that people keep buying tickets to _Avatar_ movies.
And while interest may have flagged somewhat—_Fire and Ash_ took 18 days to cross the $1 billion
mark, one day longer than the original movie and four days longer than _The Way of Water_—even the
most conservative projections have the new movie clearing $1.5 billion worldwide, which would make
it the 16th-highest grosser of all time. And that’s the worst-case scenario, one that doesn’t
factor in Cameron’s decades-long history of inspiring repeat viewings and proving his doubters
wrong. It’s even more impressive when you consider that the two sequels’ ticket sales come
against the backdrop of what is looking more and more like a permanent post-COVID decline in the
audience for theatrical moviegoing. Even when you adjust for the elevated price of seeing an
_Avatar_ movie in Cameron’s preferred large-format 3D and look at raw admissions, the first
_Avatar_ is estimated to rank slightly below the original _Star Wars_ and a bit above _Gone With the
Wind_.

To avoid having to stand outside the nearest multiplex and quiz exiting moviegoers about where you
put the apostrophe in “Lo’ak,” I will concede the point that _Avatar_’s impact doesn’t
resemble that of other movies of its size; a reference to Toruk Makto doesn’t have the same
cultural currency as a mention of _Wingardium Leviosa_, as evidenced by the fact that I only had to
Google how to spell one of them. But it also seems impossible that hundreds of millions of people
would pay to see the first movie in 2009, a second 13 years later, and a third three years after
that because they felt no connection to the series. _Something_ keeps bringing them back.

If _Avatar_ feels less ubiquitous than other franchises, it’s in substantial part because it
simply is. The reason there are nearly 200,000 wiki pages devoted to _Star Wars_ and barely more
than 2,000 devoted to _Avatar_ is because the former’s expanded universe encompasses hundreds of
hours of movies and TV shows, spinoff novels, and comic books, including those no longer considered
part of the official canon, whereas virtually everything you need to know about the _Avatar_
universe is contained in the three movies themselves. (There are, technically, a handful of video
games and comics, but the movies make few if any reference to their storylines.) Most Hollywood
studios are desperate to keep their precious intellectual property in the public eye, terrified that
audiences will lose their connection to the world and not feel compelled to keep up with each new
installment. But Cameron isn’t afraid of giving them a little bit of a break, because he’s
confident he can lure back the audience anew each time, and he’d rather delay a movie for a year
or more to ensure every frame is finished to his exacting specifications. He’s also, of course,
one of the only directors who can simply inform a major movie studio that their billion-dollar
investment is going to take a little longer to bear fruit.

It is admittedly odd that the _Avatar_ movies have offered up so little in the way of quotable
dialogue or meme-worthy moments, especially considering that they’re made by the man who gave us
“I’ll be back,” “Hasta la vista, baby,” and “I’m the king of the world!” But it also
seems unlikely that the writer of _Aliens_, _Titanic_, and the first two _Terminator_ movies has
simply forgotten how to write punchy action-movie quips—especially since he’s now co-writing
with some of the best franchise screenwriters of the 21st century, including the new _Planet of the
Apes_ series’ Rick Jaffa and Amanda Silver. But he and his writers have embraced a more
simplified, broad-stroke approach to character and dialogue, one which puts the movies right on the
border between telling stories with universal appeal and simply rehashing familiar tropes, including
many copied over from Cameron’s previous films.

In practical terms, it’s been stunningly effective. The generic storylines and subtext-free
dialogue have allowed the _Avatar_ films to resonate with a massive global audience, one whose
emotions are driven more by the movies’ visual imagery than the details of their plots. And
that’s the key to why their appeal is both so enduring and so elusive. If it’s true that many
viewers can’t remember the names of Jake Sully and Neytiri, it might be because that’s not who
they came to see. It’s a movie-critic cliché that a movie’s location can be a character in its
own right, but in the case of Pandora, it’s textually accurate, since the entire planet is linked
in a Gaia-like hive mind. When I think back on the _Avatar_ movies I’ve seen, what comes to mind
is not pithy dialogue or emotional turning points, but images: the ashes of the decimated Hometree
drifting softly through the air, the majesty of psychic whales swimming through alien oceans,
enormous airships lifted by billowing balloons that shimmer like mother-of-pearl. It’s one reason
why, as much as I value the movies and esteem Cameron’s work, I will never see an _Avatar_ in
anything but theatrical 3D, because any other format would rob the movies of their most, perhaps
their only, exceptional qualities. You can watch an _Avatar_ movie at home, the same way you can
look at a postcard of _Guernica_, but it won’t give you an inkling of its real power.

If the _Avatar_ series’ impact starts diminishing the moment the movies leave theaters, that might
be because they’re not meant to be seen any other way—and that might not be such a bad thing.
One reason that _The Way of Water_ did so well is because the 13-year gap between movies actually
gave audiences a chance to miss them. (It took Marvel years of diminishing returns to realize even
superfans might need the occasional breather.) All but the biggest hits fade with time—when was
the last time you heard someone mention _The Sting_?—and that forgetting is far more natural than
the forced drumbeat of awareness that modern franchises impose on their increasingly worn-out
audiences. Apart from their box-office haul, the most concrete evidence of _Avatar_’s lasting
impact doesn’t have anything to do with the movies’ story, or even their images: It’s the fact
that if you went around the back of a major multiplex in December 2009, you could find their 35mm
projectors sitting out with the trash. The movie industry had already put its chips on the
transition to digital projection, but _Avatar_ made the case more powerfully than any shareholder
presentation. Its bleeding-edge technology called out for the latest and greatest in theatrical
experiences, and Cameron, as is his wont, sweated every imaginable detail, creating dozens of
variations of the film so that it could be optimized for every conceivable theatrical environment.

The result was so stunning that some viewers experienced what came to be known as “Post-_Avatar_
Depression Syndrome,” a feeling of acute despair at being forced to leave Pandora’s
breathtakingly tactile world behind. Even 16 years later, the first _Avatar_ still looks better than
almost any effects-driven movie made since. If few have tried to copy its success, it might be
because they simply lack the ability to follow in its four-toed footsteps.
