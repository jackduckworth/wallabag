I Loved the Book. Ridley Scott’s Big New Movie Adaptation? Not So Much.

====================================================================================================

_THIS ARTICLE CONTAINS SPOILERS FOR_ THE DOG STARS_, BOTH THE BESTSELLING NOVEL AND THE NEW FILM
ADAPTATION._

A third of the way into Peter Heller’s 2012 postapocalyptic novel _The Dog Stars_, the dog dies. I
wondered, going into my screening of Ridley Scott’s new adaptation, whether he and screenwriter
Mark L. Smith would save poor old Jasper, as a sop to the moviegoer’s sensibilities. The answer is
no: The taciturn mechanic, pilot, and protagonist Hig, played in the film by Jacob Elordi, still
must suffer the loss of Jasper. But instead of dying gently in his sleep and becoming a focal point
for Hig’s all-consuming grief, Jasper (played in the movie by a Belgian Malinois named Raksha)
expires from his wounds after cinematically defending Hig and his partner Bangley (Josh Brolin) from
raiders in the night.

People who check Doesthedogdie.com before committing to a movie may not care much about this
difference. But the change says it all about what Scott and Smith have done to Heller’s sad story.
The novel is meditative, slow, spare, like the depopulated world it describes. Hig speaks in first
person, and Heller omits quotation marks, which has the effect of bringing you deeper into the mind
of a survivor who lives mostly in the past, and whose present-day interlocutors are few and far
between. (Or maybe the writer just really loves Cormac McCarthy.) The film, set in Colorado but
filmed in Italy, wants to impress you, introducing tribes of raiders and cannibals and demented
leaders right out of _Mad Max_, serving up action set pieces and last-minute escapes. Poor Elordi is
left trying to do all the subtle grieving with his big brown eyes—a tall order, even for a very
tall guy.

The magic of Heller’s novel lies in the contrast between the majestic, empty landscape—the
ultimate playground for the last pilot alive in the Rockies—and Hig’s endless sadness. This
balancing act gave the book its particular savor, and it was helped a lot by an element the movie
ditches completely: Heller’s book is kinda, sorta cli-fi. The story is set about a decade after a
pandemic kills most people on Earth, and Heller imagines a Colorado where nature, even when relieved
of the burden of humanity, is far past healing. “The old seasonal benchmarks were mostly
nostalgia,” Hig explains near the beginning of the novel. “We had snow in the mountains this
winter but there were two years running where the peaks were dry holding almost nothing. This scared
me more than attacks or disease.” On Hig’s fishing trips, a habit he retains from his
pre-pandemic days, he catches only “suckers and carp,” who have filled the niche left by the
trout who’ve gone extinct. Memorably, Hig describes the “revulsion” he feels when he catches a
sucker, “the sluggish resistance that couldn’t be called a fight, and the distended lips and the
scales. I made myself get used to the taste and the bones.” Later, he realizes that even the carp
are starving: “The trout are gone the elk the tigers the elephants the suckers. If I wake up
crying in the middle of the night and I’m not saying I do it’s because even the carp are
gone,” Hig says. The landscape makes Heller’s book a Western, but this sense of impending loss
makes even the gorgeous landscape seem unstable—here today, gone tomorrow.

This changing climate is also the reason that the characters of Cima (played in the movie by
Margaret Qualley) and Pops (Guy Pearce) leave their well-hidden canyon ranch with Hig, rather than
(as in Scott’s treatment) because of an exciting surprise invasion by raiders on horseback. (The
movie never alludes to climate change at all.) “Last summer the creek almost dried up. We had to
dig in the streambed to pool enough water just to drink. Half our cattle died. Pretty much been
getting worse every year. Getting warmer. Just like they said it would,” Pops says, explaining to
Hig why he wants to tag along on Hig’s quixotic mission to Grand Junction. In the film, instead,
the trio hops into Hig’s Cessna on very short notice, pursued by tomahawk-wielding raiders in war
paint on horseback. (As I said, the story is a Western, but the movie doesn’t seem to think very
deeply about running back this kind of imagery.) As the plane struggles to lift off, Pops shoots
one, then another raider off the plane; one even loses his head to the propeller, just like in the
old adventure movies.

Every human threat to Hig and Bangley is far smaller in scale in the book than in the film. The
fighting force that attacks Bangley while he’s left alone at the Erie airfield is not the swarms
of tattooed berserker War Boys who flood Brolin in the movie, but only 14 people, four of them armed
(“dressed like goddamn Mongols … six of them female,” the book’s Bangley says). The danger
Hig, Pops, and Cima discover at the Grand Junction airport isn’t a whole bunch of cannibal freaks
reliving the 1950s in a perfectly maintained airport lounge, led by an insane ex–flight attendant
(played by Allison Janney), but just two paranoid, round-the-bend old people who’ve laid a trap
for incoming aircraft by stringing a taut line across the runway. Heller’s novel is always careful
to understate this world’s violence, so that Hig and Bangley, who kill every person who approaches
their redoubt (including, early in the book, a young girl traveling alone with only a kitchen knife
for a weapon), look about as monstrous as anyone else who’s survived this long. (In the novel, Hig
even feeds Jasper jerky made from the bodies of fallen intruders.) Not so in Scott’s movie, full
of swarms of evil people who obviously deserve to die so our heroes can survive.

These adaptive choices feel like they may have been made in service of an imagined audience weaned
on the postapocalyptic worlds of _The Walking Dead_ and _The Last of Us_. To Scott and Smith,
Heller’s novel may have felt too minimalist, given what we’ve been accustomed to in the way of
grizzled survivors, threatened fortresses, and paranoid gun nuts who were right all along. The happy
ending in Heller’s novel is Cima and Hig getting together, Hig realizing that although trout and
elk are gone, there are still “plenty of crows” and other species filling their niches, and
Hig’s epiphany that life is inherently transient. The happy ending in Scott’s film is a party
with Hig and Bangley’s neighbors, a band playing music, Bangley picking up a guitar, Hig and Cima
dancing. In trying to give us what they think we want, Scott and Smith took away what this story
needed.
