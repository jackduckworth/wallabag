Winter review – Richard Armitage's new cop drama is as ferociously watchable as it is daft

====================================================================================================

Dr Ethan Winter is a maverick, a wildcard, a lone wolf who won't play by the rules. "Winter!" gasps
DI Lauren Bell as the muscular outsider bursts into a police interrogation room and proceeds to
subject a tremulous pensioner to the full force of his waxed cagoule. "You can't do this!" But he
can do this. And he will do this. Why? Because he's Dr Ethan Winter, dammit, one of the greatest
forensic pathologists of his generation; a bearded genius whose approach to obstacles is as
uncompromising as his approach to tremulous pensioners.

And yet. Dr Ethan Winter is troubled. We know this because Winter – the new six-part ITV police
procedural that shares his starkly unseasonable surname – keeps cutting to scenes in which our
protagonist paces silently around his fashionably underlit Bristol flat while scowling into the
middle distance. Has he misplaced the keys to his vintage sports car? No, they're over there, next
to the gruesome autopsy photos he keeps on the kitchen table in the hope they'll prompt visitors to
ask what made him want to be a forensic doctor ("I became a forensic doctor … _to mend sores_").
Could it be, then, that the source of his malaise is … complicated? That it could in fact have
something to do with the murder, a decade ago, of his fiancee – a case which, though ostensibly
closed, continues to raise enough questions to provide the series with its narrative backbone? While
leaving sufficient time in each episode for the agitated hunk to solve a new, self-contained crime?
It's certainly possible!

Dr Ethan Winter is played by Richard Armitage, in accordance with the scriptures. His presence here
(in his sixth – sixth! – TV thriller since 2021) serves as a seal of quality, or at least an
assurance that the ensuing brooding will be accompanied by a) gratuitous demonstrations of
upper-body strength, and b) a voice like a Twix melting in a bra fire.

Anyway, the body of a young woman has been found in a freezer lorry so it's time for some plot.
Prickly DI Bell (Annabel Scholey), who has recently moved to Bristol after a mysteriously
self-curtailed career with the Met ("It's a long story"), suspects a local recidivist. Bejumpered
sidekick DS Ellis Daldry (a wonderfully rumpled turn from Jamie Kenna) knows better. Best leave the
solving stuff to our perturbed hero, he tells her. "Ethan can," after all, "read a body like other
people read a book." And he can. If by reading a body you mean slowly circling a cadaver while
huskily whispering things like "hello, stranger" and "fly eggs". Still, it seems to work, and soon
we're bounding after him as he tears excitedly through the woods, nostrils flapping at the prospect
of another crime scene at which he can be a maverick.

"Can you smell that?" he gasps. We cannot. Tell us more, corpse man. "FAECES," he bellows, hurtling
towards a disused well like a cannonball in a Barbour. "ROTTING MEAT … IT'S A DEAD BODY!"

[Armitage with Annabel Scholey as DI Lauren Bell.]
At this point, there is a strong chance you're thinking, "This sounds like an absolutely terrible TV
programme." And you would be absolutely correct. But only partially. The script may be a thing of
string and glue but the dead fiancee stuff is gripping and Winter's production values are uncommonly
high; the direction, soundtrack and lighting (so many burnished brass sconces!) conspiring to paint
Bristol as a glowering storm cloud of polo-necked pain and longing. Based on long-running French
thriller Balthazar, the aesthetic here shares a similar, 'ow you say, narked grandeur.

The upshot? It's a potboiler in a belted trenchcoat. Silent Witness saying "bof" while eating an
onion. And yet even the many, many ridiculous bits work, folded as they are into a soufflé that is
as ferociously watchable as it is unnecessarily expensive.

The fact of the matter is this: sometimes you want prestige drama that roils with nuance and
complexity. And sometimes you want Richard Armitage shouting, "FAECES!" while sprinting through
Bristol in a cagoule. Let us give thanks.
