

====================================================================================================

Bavuma's nine-hour masterpiece was art we risk losing in Test cricket's great purge | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

Have you read George Orwell's review of Mein Kampf? This might not sound like a grabby reference, or
a killer hook for the algorithm. But Hitler is always hot. Hitler is legacy brand. Hitler is
eyeballs. Also, it's just a great review, even more so because it was written at a time when the
world didn't quite know what Hitler was "all about".

True, you get a vibe off people. The invasion of France. The ranting, visceral hatred. There were
signs, clues, indications that this guy, this guy is _not my kind of guy_.

But the full Hitler-verse had yet to reveal itself. And for a while you worry Orwell's going to get
it wrong, not go hard enough, misread the full history as we now know it. He writes about how much
he likes Hitler's face because he looks pained and sad, like a wounded dog, giving off that sense of
eternal injustice the German people really went for.

By this point you're trying to catch his eye, coughing loudly, saying "Holocaust" into your fist.
No, George. Don't start talking about charisma.

Luckily, it all turns out fine. Orwell says that he would definitely kill Hitler if he got within
arm's reach of him. Then he describes, better than anyone before or since, the terrifying nature of
the world Mein Kampf is proposing, above all its utter boredom, the eternal grey repetitions, same
struggle, same triumph, same boot stamping on same face, same pitch of willed and empty hysteria.

And yeah … you're thinking about T20 franchise cricket, aren't you? I didn't say it. You did. It's
fine. The mind goes where the mind goes.

You're picturing a year-round cycle of brain-flooding noise. An endless will to power. The Kansas
City CheeseSteaks v the Reykjavik Unmentionables live from the Jakarta T5 Megascream, injected
straight into your eyeballs by a robot replicant Danny Morrison.

It's fine that you want to make this connection. It's even deeply fitting given the greatest story
of the sporting week by an absolute mile, a story so big, so sweeping, so viscerally personal, that
most people seem to have completely ignored it, comes from the polar opposite place.

This is of course Temba Bavuma's match-winning nine-hour hundred in the second Test against
Australia in Gqeberha, one of those episodes where sport and the life of the nation seem to cross,
blend and become indistinguishable.

Mainly Bavuma in Gqeberha was an incredible feat of skill, resilience and constant adaptive
intelligence, in the way only Test Cricket can deliver. Bavuma walked out to bat with the summer
running away from him. Thrashed in the first Test, South Africa were 12 for two, faced with one of
the great bowling attacks.

Bavuma has been a transformative Test captain, with 12 wins in 14 matches, four hundreds, a world
championship mace, all stretched over three broken years. But he is still always, always chasing
legitimacy and affirmation, in a way that is unique to cricket, but also unique to this man in this
role in this sporting nation. First black middle order batsman. First black captain. How is this
going to fall? How strong is that ice underfoot?

[Temba Bavuma celebrates his century]
Two days later Bavuma was last man out for 179 in a total of 425 for nine, sprinting off with a kind
of light around him, waving his bat giddily, 36 years old, wife and kid in the crowd, the greatest
moment of his sporting life.

There were so many parts to this. First, the survival period, with that endearing tic of gently
jutting his chin out at the bowler, mixed in with the odd deliciously clean whip or push or clump.
Bavuma has really quick hands. I remember seeing him in east London in a white-ball game against
dark skies and low stands, and all you could really see was his hands whizzing with thrilling speed,
like fireflies in the night.

In Gqeberha he was all poise and measured severity. When he plays a cover drive he seems to lean at
45 degrees, knees bent, entire body easing into the oblique, perfectly contained in his own little
square of time and space.

The game changed with the partnership of 106 with Marco Jansen before lunch on the third day,
brilliant counterattacking Test cricket, but also an amazing tableau. At one end the gangling Dutch
farmer with the easy, guileless stride, a cricketer who always seems to be running in with a bushel
of radishes over one shoulder and a bolt-action rabbit gun in his waistband. At the other the tiny,
super-smart black captain. It's no more than an optic, unrelated to the nation's real-world
struggles. But it's also real. Sport is, as ever, saying: how about this version of things?

Bavuma got to his hundred with a release of pent-up power, swivel-pulling Pat Cummins through
midwicket, holding the shot, one of those moments where the batter feels what has happened a second
before anyone else, allowed in that microsecond to exist all alone inside his own little pocket of
private triumph.

But then Bavuma has always had to be aware, to hold himself a certain way. There was surprise in
some corners that he turned out to be such an unflappable captain. Try being Temba Bavuma for a bit.
You think this stuff is going to faze you?

Bavuma is a child of the transition period, born three months after Nelson Mandela left Victor
Verster Prison, the same day (get this) the government finally opened up all its public hospitals to
black citizens. He grew up a township boy in Langa, playing in the street, learning which strips of
local tarmac had the most carry and bounce.

Plucked out aged 10 on a scholarship to one of South Africa's top schools, he struggled. Ten is
relatively late to start trying to become an elite batter. How about still perfecting your English,
learning how to act, what to put on your plate at dinner? Even his height, which we must always talk
about, is a thing. Bavuma is the 10th shortest man ever to play Test cricket. Yeah, pour it on. What
else have you got?

There was the slow-burn start to his career. For years the quota talk circled, the weird dual burden
of innate outsiderdom and suspicions of favour. Then suddenly you're the first township captain of
what has always been an anglophone, military-elite kind of sport.

It sounds overwhelming. Right up until you hear him speak, at which point you just want him to talk
about all the stuff in that soft voice, gentle but deliciously insistent. It's no surprise to learn
Bavuma has a normal life away from this, that his wife is founder of a luxury real estate firm, he
takes his kid to school, doesn't do celebrity, that he's never really made a point of making a
point, but made it all the same by being relentlessly himself.

Why talk about this here? Why bring endless grey, smothering repetition into it? First because even
without the Bavuma story this was just an epic piece of sport, one of those occasions where every
detail feels meaningful.

Steve Smith getting actual whiplash, literally wearing a foam neck-brace after whirling around to
see if an edge had carried. Marnus Labuschagne whirling at the crease, acting out his own thoughts
in the air like a tortured pantomime horse, while his career self-suffocates in real time. Here is
Anrich Nortje, the perfect composite of shoulders, glower and vengeful moustache, looking on like
the hand of fate from a deserted balcony, groin-knacked but somehow still profoundly present.

[Temba Bavuma (centre) is embraced as his team enjoy victory in the second Test]
The whole thing heads on to Newlands on Tuesday and the sandpaper reunion game, an impossibly fine
piece of narrative work, slow-cooked over eight years, the kind of storytelling the human brain just
thirsts for.

Although now it is also time to zoom out and feel the whiplash. On the fifth day of the first Test
in Durban, as Australia wrapped up their own victory the TV commentator talked about the fine
sporting spirit of the contest and how "it's credit to the people of South Africa", at which point
the camera panned back with perfect comic timing to show: a completely empty stadium.

So, back to Orwell and being on the right side of history. Because a great thing, a carefully woven
cultural exhibit, is being overtly encouraged to die here. South Africa hadn't played a Test before
this series for 11 months. They played eight last year, two a second-string trip to Zimbabwe.

The hard game, the actual game that still produces the short-form TV offshoot, is being sacrificed
mercilessly. And the thing that is replacing it is not just demonstrably inferior, but self-dooming
in its basic inanity.

In the intervals of the Test, South African TV showed a mouthwatering banter-table chat-along
featuring all of South Africa's craggy modern greats, Polly, Makhaya, AB, the Steyn Gun, sat around
just telling stories, doing everything but hug each other, conjuring up moments of beauty, gold,
legend.

This will not be allowed to happen in the future. T20 cricket can't produce these stories. It's
throwaway content, parasocial celebrity fawning. What are we going to have 20 years from now?
Lhuan-dre Pretorius telling bardically embroidered tales about the time he slash-hoicked 37 off 12
balls in the now defunct North American Mega-Lurch?

There is still a fear of saying this out loud, of making the unarguable observation that a form of
sporting hyper-capitalism is dictating that nothing can matter if it's not monetisable right here
and now. Half-understood arguments about demand and supply will justify an act of cultural
vandalism. Well, yeah, but look at the way this entity in a state of managed decline appears,
believe it or not, to be in a state of managed decline.

The reality is something beautiful is being extinguished. It feels like the sporting arm of a
process that has been called the enshittification of modern life, where the bottom line is the only
line, where everything must be reduced to stupidity and profitable chaff.

Orwell was also good on the "machine worship" of the early 20th century. He was, in the best way, a
luddite. Inefficient, human-scale elements have a right to exist too. The mechanised loom made a few
things better. But mainly it made a few people quite a lot richer.

We can resist this, and not just in the realm of trash sport, but trash life generally. Humans still
know what's good. We crave these things, still hunger for stories like the Bavuma supremacy, which
may never happen again, not in the current timeline, because its structures are being peeled away
even as it plays out.
