

====================================================================================================

Rivals review – this TV joy is so much more than just a romp

====================================================================================================

Tony Baddingham? Tony Worse-ingham, more like! For reasons best known, one suspects, to streaming
giant Disney's revenue-maximisers, the second series of Rivals has been split up into two parts of
six episodes each. We left the rutting folk of Rutshire just after Rupert Campbell-Black (Alex
Hassell, a sex god by way of Hugh Laurie and once you notice this you can't unsee it) had broken the
news to arch-rival Tony (David Tennant, having the time of his life as the unrepentantly villainous
mogul) that his wife, Monica (Claire Rushbrook), had been killed. She drove off into the Great Storm
of 1987 after discovering Tony's affair with Maud (Victoria Smurfit), his bitter enemy's wife, and
demanding – at last – a divorce. One tree later, however, poor Monica's dreams of freedom and
future happiness ended.

Now, after a five-month hiatus for viewers, we arrive at the funeral. Never has there been more
lustful inter-pew side-eyeing. Taggie (Bella Maclean, doing such fine work with what could be a
tremendously boring character) and Rupert Reformed-Bounder-Question-Mark are yearning across the
aisle. Freddie's moustache is quivering at Lizzie and Lizzie's perm is quivering right back
(sported, respectively, by Danny Dyer and Katherine Parkinson). Gerald (Hubert Burton) and Charles
(Gary Lamont) are suffused with the love that dare not speak its name, especially in front of
Gerald's new fiancee, Muffy (Eliot Salt) – but they find a way to express it non-verbally in the
car after the service. And James (Oliver Chris) doubtless is transfixed by the vision of himself in
the nearest reflective surface.

After that, it's sort of business as usual, but … not quite? The Venturer v Corinium shenanigans
continue as the fight for the franchise intensifies, but nobody watching cares about that. We're
here for the other shenanigans, the kind that have made Rivals beloved since the off – emotional
scenes, dramatic reveals, painful confessions, SAUCE, backcombing, betrayals, SAUCE, dastardry and
bastardry, dogs running around huge houses and grounds, a bit more SAUCE and all soundtracked by 80s
bangers or Mozart's Dies Irae, depending on the mood.

[In a red-toned photo, a woman with a fringe glances sideways.]
There is plenty of everything. Without Monica (she was, as a guest at the wake points out, his
conscience) and full of grief and fury, Tony is off the chain. Two shotgun blasts and a brace of
dead peacocks later, Tennant has turned his eyes black and given up blinking for the duration. It's
very unsettling.

The parentage of Sarah's baby is revealed and so, as the series progresses, is the state of the
maternity clothes market in 80s Britain. Freddie and Lizzie's relationship intensifies and he
(possibly also she) discovers a lady part that apparently neither of them knew about before, which
is nice.

There is a mole in the Venturer camp, shovelling information back to Tony. Is it the woman who used
to work for Tony before she attempted to murder him? Or is it the woman sleeping with him now? Will
Declan (Aidan Turner) discover that the latter woman is Maud, in revenge for – _checks notes_ –
him not going to see her in an Ibsen play? Will Gerald tell Muffy she has signed up to a lavender
marriage? Will Paul (Rufus Jones) notice Sarah is pregnant? And if he does, can he count to nine?

[David Tennant, right, in a pinstripe suit as Tony and Nafessa Williams in a red blazer as Cameron.]
At the same time, however, it is as though a dark cloud is gathering over Rutshire – or maybe it
is the shadow of so many chickens coming home to roost. There are more consequences to more actions
in this six episodes than there have been in the entire series and a half that went before.
Sometimes this results in scenes of real poignancy. There is a wonderful and much-deserved (for both
character and actor) death scene for Monica: real heartbreak played out between devastated spouses
and the ones who have betrayed them.

All of these work beautifully, because even though (like the Jilly Cooper books on which it is
based) Rivals has a reputation for being just a romp, it is not. It's a light drama featuring
characters written with real depth, played by actors who can hint at multitudes and plumb those
depths in the moment. But as the crises and breakdowns and evocations of really dreadful grief mount
up, you do sometimes wonder where the fun went.

None of this is helped by the fact that Rupert has been neutered by his love for Taggie. History's
most dashing bounder has mutated into something between village elder and marriage counsellor and,
well – it changes the vibe, you know?

The final two episodes restore some of the original spirit to the overall enterprise. It is still,
taken in the round, a joy – filled with warmth, wit, intelligence and brilliant actors (plus a
touching appearance by the woman without whom none of this would exist, Dame Jilly). But perhaps
Tony could be persuaded to shoot a few of those chickens overhead before the next series arrives and
see if a little more light can be let back in. Let the rolling hills and pumping buttocks shine once
more.
