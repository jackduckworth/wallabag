She’s Gone From Indie Rocker to Unlikely Pop Star. Now She’s Back With Her First Album Since the Big Change.

====================================================================================================

For millennia, women’s voices have borne the burden of lamenting the dead, from the ancient Greek
and Egyptian eras through to modernity, across cultures, including in Gaza today. But on “The
Outside,” the track that opens Phoebe Bridgers’ return album after an extended bereavement leave
from public life, she abjures obvious signs of feminine mourning, the sob or wail many listeners
might expect from this supposed “sad girl” songwriter turned improbable pop star. Instead, she
deploys a vocoder effect—that war-forged vocal filter with its emotionally and sexually neutered
spectrum of drones—to turn herself into her own cybernetic Greek chorus.

These Phoebe-bots step forward in the album’s first moments as if to update the listener on
what’s happened offstage between acts. That is, in the long _Lost Weekend,_ as the album title
teasingly puts it, of the six-year gap since her second solo album, _Punisher__,_ which has become
one of the defining records of the decade. Or at least the three years since her side
“supergroup” Boygenius put out their own multiple-Grammy-winning album, _The Record__._

To begin with, our hero has lost someone.

“Crying in a suit and tie,” the vocoder narrates, calling to mind outfits fans are familiar with
from Bridgers’ stage-wear and photo shoots, but also classic funeral garb. Then comes the
trademark punch line: “But you should see the other guy.” Yeah, the one in the coffin probably
got the worst of it. Mourning can be just the latest stage in a long combat. “One more time, for
old time’s sake / I’ll watch him get carried away.” The deceased here is surely her father,
who died in late 2022, and to whom the album is dedicated. His issues and parental shortcomings have
been a subject of earlier songs. (“I’m gonna kill you, if you don’t beat me to it,” she sang
at him on _Punisher_’s “Kyoto.”) So the double meaning of “carried away” here hits hard,
perhaps as hard as Bridgers apparently drove her “fist through the drywall” in her grief.

But then the voice switches to what listeners know as her “natural” soprano, and she sings in
swelling cadences, “I am on the outside, watching.” That sense of distance and dissociation
explains the need for the cyber-vocals, which return in the next verse to relate the memory of
another ritual—stage-diving into the crowd at one of her own shows, letting fans prop her up, a
third kind of being “carried away.” Her dad was there to see it. Still, she repeats, shifting
back to the smaller human voice, “I was on the outside, watching.”

In those first minutes, Bridgers, who turns 32 next week, sets up a lot of what she faced with this
album. _Lost Weekend_ is a record about grief that thankfully will also turn out to be one about new
love, two forces that can overwhelm the senses and bring on expressive excess or stunned
speechlessness. It’s a work that comes out of that funny, unnamable, Beckettian feeling, “I
can’t go on, I’ll go on.”

Beyond the upheavals in her life, a prime hurdle was her own success. Bridgers’ choice to retreat
came soon after she complained about fans who “bullied” her for attention in an airport on the
way to her father’s funeral. But there was also the dilemma of having so quickly fulfilled so many
ambitions, on a scale she scarcely could have imagined in June 2020, when she released _Punisher_ as
a 25-year-old cult indie songwriter who followed the guiding star of the late Elliott Smith. In
those strange early pandemic-lockdown days, her intimate, often conflicted and depressive but
colorfully poetic songs struck a chord. So did, in her social-media posts and videos, her dry humor,
hipster California drawl, and queer lefty sensibility (though inevitably never queer or lefty enough
for some). Not to mention her platinum-blonde waifish looks, often clad in jokey skeleton costumes,
which got sparklier and fancier as her star continued to rise.

The attention snowballed as society opened back up, via gossip around the movie stars and musicians
she dated; TV appearances, including a spot on _Saturday Night Live_ where she scandalized squares
by smashing her guitar (who did she think she was, a rock star? a _man_?); being picked up by Taylor
Swift to open a leg of the “Eras” tour; making the fabled cover of Rolling Stonealongside her
Boygenius songwriter comrades Julien Baker and Lucy Dacus, with whom she then sold out arenas to
throngs of young female and LGBTQ+ fans singing along with every word; and finding herself echoed
and imitated not only by newcomers like Gracie Abrams and Lizzy McAlpine but peers like Noah Kahan
and that reliable wave-surfer Swift herself, who said of Bridgers in 2022, “she is the reference
now,” and proved it in spots on her subsequent album _The Tortured Poets Department._

Though much of Bridgers’ music is shaped by the sound and skeptical mindset of the 1990s indie
rock that was being made when she was a tot, she never seemed to share its squeamishness about
mainstream fame. This lower-middle-class Pasadena kid looked happy to flirt with Hollywood and high
fashion and attend the Met Gala. But as she sings on _Lost Weekend_’s closing track, “When a
dream comes true, a fantasy dies.” Not only did she have to contend with the invasiveness of fans
and press, but there was a growing risk that the delicate-yet-steely style she’d been honing since
her teens as a precocious soloist and band member gigging around L.A. could become a dead end, a
caricature of itself. Her contributions to the Boygenius album already carried a slight whiff of
repetition.

On an undated voicemail snippet touchingly included on one track on _Lost Weekend_, her father
remarks, sounding a lot like his daughter for a second, “You’re everywhere, dude.” Did
Bridgers get tired of the ubiquity of her own voice, one not always coming out of her own mouth? On
one of the album’s standout tracks, “The Governor’s Waltz,” she shrugs at one would-be
professional and romantic rival: “Being myself wasn’t easy / Do it for me.” (Fans are pretty
sure they know who she means.) And she cautions on “Haunted”—otherwise a rollicking,
hellbent-on-hope jamboree featuring bluegrass superstars Nickel Creek as well as her Boygenius pals
on backup—“If you’re gonna make a wish, then you better be specific / You’re gonna have to
live it every day.” It’s standard to want to slough off some of your own past schtick as you
come out of your 20s, but tougher when that style and persona is one that’s made your fortune, one
that tens of thousands of fans are attached to. As Bridgers says in _Lost Weekend_’s press bio_,_
“I needed a different relationship to the music I was making and to the world in which it was
going to appear.”

Some of that adjustment has been apparent in the album rollout, with its series of pop-up shows in
small venues where fans’ phones were sealed in inaccessible bags and they were asked to keep mum
about the new songs. That plan was followed even with the surprise show at Madison Square Garden in
June, as well as this week’s album listening parties. Those have taken place in selected
planetariums, just as the earlier mini-tour followed a map of UFO-sighting hot spots. There’s
nothing particularly spacey about _Lost Weekend;_ it’s more that while reasserting control over
her social contract with her audience, Bridgers was also renewing her gifts to them, including her
own brand of whimsy.

Musically, she had to reconsider even her literal physical voice, the surface embodiment of the
reductive, sexist “sad girl” category for which Bridgers has so often been made an unwilling
figurehead. Where some people have fetishized or mimicked her high moody hush, others scorned it as
bloodless, or even coquettish. In _Art Monsters_ author Lauren Elkin’s new book, Vocal Break: On
Women, Music, and Power, she writes, “In indie music there’s been a fad for faint female voices.
… The musicmakers and the market and not least the whisper girls themselves have constructed a
sound of fragile white womanhood, and dressed it up as an aesthetic choice. … The whispery girls
sing like they have nothing beneath the neck, no apparatus to produce more than wistful wisps of
song.” She doesn’t mention Bridgers by name, focusing mainly on Billie Eilish (a dubious
candidate for an “indie” singer), but I’ve heard other people similarly charge that Bridgers
and her downbeat sisters’ understated tones are affectations to appeal to the aural equivalent of
the male gaze.

But these young women were coming up when big voices like Katy Perry, Lady Gaga, and Kelly Clarkson
dominated the charts, so perhaps they reacted against that kind of bombastic, escapist recipe for
supposed empowerment. They adopted an attitude that they were not going to lay it all out in bright
colors, but insist through the closeness of their sound that you come to them instead, follow down
into their shadows. Elkin herself cites the poet and classicist Anne Carson writing in “The Gender
of Sound,” her recently reissued 1992 essay, that “high vocal pitch” has for centuries been
used to “characterise a person who is deviant from or deficient in the masculine ideal of
control.” But Elkin declines to quote Carson saying in the same essay: “Very few women in public
life do not worry that their voices are too high or too light or too shrill to command
respect”—or, in more recent terms, too “cursive,” with too much vocal fry, or too whispery.

It’s a catch-22 in which everyone is always the wrong kind of feminine, too much or too little,
too brash or too demure, such that even one as conventionally femme as Taylor Swift can end up
fretting about her younger competition on “Anti-Hero” that “everybody is a sexy baby, and
I’m a monster on the hill.” Granted, as Elkin delves deeper into Eilish’s work, she does
eventually realize that perhaps she has to confront “my own preconceptions about soft female
voices, my own internalised misogyny.” But those reflexive reactions and mixed signals continue to
add up to one cultural message for Carson’s “women, catamites, eunuchs and androgynes”: shut
up and go away.

Bridgers has gone away, and on her return she seems determined to build vocal and musical
soundscapes less apt to be misconstrued, truer to her own fluid sexuality, without surrendering her
aesthetic essence. That begins with the vocoder, which later returns in the song fragment
“Panorama.” Elsewhere she continues something she started on _Punisher_ and with Boygenius,
choosing moments for punk-rock shouts and heavy-metal screams that are all the more bracing given
her usual soft-spokenness—the screech that opens the exuberant “Bobby” on _Lost Weekend_, or
the way she yells repeatedly, in the climax of “As Above,” the words, “You won’t have to
believe in magic / Leave it to me, and I’ll make it happen!” It overturns the tension the song
sustains up till then, swooping it up in a sudden sonic tornado, briefly, before setting it down
peacefully again. These are like the “high-pitched piercing cry” called the _ololyga_ in Ancient
Greek, made only by women, often as part of a ritual sacrifice, a sound confined to the outskirts
and margins of the city, according to Carson: “No proper civic space would contain it
unregulated.” But Bridgers’ music would.

To extend her reach further, Bridgers has brought aboard the contemporary composer Caroline Shaw on
vocal arrangements. Shaw became the youngest-ever winner of the Pulitzer Prize in Music in 2013 for
her a cappella work Partita for Eight Voices_,_ and previously crossed into the pop world working on
Kanye West’s _The Life of Pablo_ (before most of the awfulness) and last year on Rosalía’s
acclaimed _Lux_. Here, where Bridgers in the past might mostly have harmonized with herself and
occasional guests, Shaw helps weave multi-voiced background textures and codas that hint at vistas
beyond the songwriter’s interiority. She’s probably also responsible for the use on the title
track of a sample from composer Paul Lansky’s “Notjustmoreidlechatter,” part of a 1994 project
that probed the overlapping territory between speech and sound.

Occasional male voices slide into the mix, too, including the mysterious “Son-John Johnson” on
“Kill Me” and “I Can’t Wait,” which is widely believed to be a pseudonym for the
unmistakable pipes of Cameron Winter from Geese. That’s an entertaining little puzzle, though
nowhere near as intriguing as why “Kill Me” uses the words “Tamám Shud” from the _Rubaiyat
of Omar Khayyam_ inan apparent reference to a notorious South Australian 1940s murder case. (Maybe
Winter did it.)

Likewise, instrumentally, large ensembles of musicians from across genres join Bridgers to build up
atmospheric interludes between or within songs, and unexpected, unsettling jumps between styles.
Some tracks are just quick sketches or outbursts, like “Never Going Back!”—a kind of campfire
shout-along—and “Other Plans,” a pair that’s cordoned off in a way from the rest of the
album by two instrumental versions of _Punisher’s_ “Graceland Too,” for reasons I haven’t
yet guessed. Many others cut off abruptly, denying the listener a sense of resolution.

These are mostly not storytelling songs like the ones on _Punisher_ or Bridgers’ 2017 debut album
_Stranger in the Alps,_ either lyrically or musically. _Lost Weekend_ is not as eager to be your
companion and comfort in hard times. It comes with guardrails to prevent the listener from
over-identifying with her. It’s made to wrongfoot you, to divulge its depths only gradually with
time, not to be immediately dissected and decoded online. The way the lyrics collage seemingly
unrelated narratives and addressees are evasive maneuvers. As Bridgers sings on “Haunted,”
“There is a difference between lying and deciding what not to say.” It’s as if the kind of
coded allusions queer writers used to conceal taboo truths in the decades before Stonewall were now
being reinvented out of necessity by a comfortably, openly bisexual pop star, merely to preserve her
sanity in the social-media age. But then as now, such tactics also generate new subtexts and
undertones.

What might throw some fans off about this album_,_ then,is the opposite of what they worried about
when Bridgers released second track “Lost Boys” as a single in June and it came with the sort of
standard, obvious pop chorus Bridgers had seldom used before. It also carried in its credits the
name Jack Antonoff, the most overexposed producer today through his work with Swift as well as
Lorde, Lana Del Rey, Sabrina Carpenter, and many others. In fact Antonoff is on all the songs here,
and occasionally you can spot his touch, but only as one among many contributors. Though there are
catchy hooks and pretty passages throughout, something usually comes along to disrupt or undermine
them. And outside of “Lost Boys” itself, there’s nothing straining to be a hit—and even that
single, as I’ve written elsewhere, gradually revealed its generational and gendered complexities
on closer listening.

One of the rewards of being somewhat cryptic in poetry or songs is that it’s all the more electric
when you get direct. And what Bridgers is often wildly, exuberantly open about on _Lost Weekend_ is
being in love. Multiple songs are addressed to “Bobby,” as she calls her reputed partner of
several years, better known to most people as the comedian–filmmaker–performance artist Robert
“Bo” Burnham. I’m not given to tracking down “Easter eggs” about artists’ relationships,
but this one’s not exactly subtle, given that Burnham himself sings on and co-wrote several of
those songs.

First they meet at a costume party in “Kill Me,” where “Bobby, you taught me that I know
nothing.” That’s also the first track to sound a thematic note that arises again and again,
about this love restoring her will to live. “This is gonna kill me,” she sings, but then, with a
pause, “wait.” That redemptive moment would be unlikely in a _Punisher_ song. The track actually
called “Bobby,” meanwhile, is her most straight-ahead love song ever, with a forward-rushing
energy. I tear up near the end when she sings, “Now I don’t know if I can make it tonight / But
what are you doing the rest of my life?” But I laugh when she jokes about killing, if he doesn’t
like them, “either one of my friends” (presumably Baker and Dacus), as well as retiring the
“thing in a box I don’t fuck anymore.” Meanwhile “As Above” begins with shades of bygone
family dramas, including “my mom driving herself to the hospital,” presumably because her dad
had flaked out, maybe when she was in labor, before coming back around to that theme of love defying
mortality: “I don’t want to live forever / But I don’t wanna die before I take you to Coney
Island.”

Speaking of circularity, there are two versions of the title track, each a melodic recitation over a
droning, churchy organ, so near to the model of 2018’s lovely “The Last Great Washington” by
the veteran Seattle songwriter Damien Jurado that he is given a credit. The first version seems to
be an apology to an old friend from early in her career (“playing for no one, on top of the
world”) for having let their bonds decay. But on the “Lost Weekend (Reprise)” that ends the
album, it closes the circle from “The Outside” and returns explicitly to the subject of her
father. “I am my old man’s oldest son,” she sings, no vocoder intervening between her lips and
our ears now, and then makes a callback to that plaster-punching incident: “I fought the wall, and
the wall won.” I love how the initial gender flip leads into the faux machismo of that wry play on
the 1960s garage song turned Clash punk classic.

But she also has tender things to say: “I still live by the things he taught me / Like how to
forgive when they’re not sorry / And the government isn’t trying to kill you / They just don’t
care if they kill you.” Then, in her softest, plainest, girliest Phoebe Bridgers voice, she sings,
“But we’re gonna be alright / Me and you.” One love stands in for another. Change entails
loss, but there are also gains, and important things that stay.
