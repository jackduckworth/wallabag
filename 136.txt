Tarantino’s New Movie Is the Cinematic Equivalent of a Big Mac

====================================================================================================

Two years after the release of the 2019 hit _Once Upon a Time in Hollywood_, Quentin Tarantino
published the novelization of his own movie, a nearly 400-page work of fanfic that went far beyond a
mere prose retelling of the events of the movie’s already incident-packed screenplay. The book
devoted whole chapters to the unexplored backstories of the film’s secondary and tertiary
characters, including Charles Manson—a figure who barely appears in the film, though his cult
followers play a key role in the story. But the novel’s most significant character, beside
past-his-prime actor Rick Dalton (played in the movie by Leonardo DiCaprio), was Cliff Booth (played
by Brad Pitt, who won an Oscar for the role), Rick’s longtime stuntman, driver, and unflappable
gofer.

Indeed, as we learn more about his past and present self over the course of the novel, Cliff begins
to emerge as a rival contender for his buddy Rick’s leading-man slot. In the movie, it’s
suggested that Cliff may have murdered his wife in an ambiguous incident on a boat; in the book, the
truth about this long-rumored act of violence gets spelled out in gruesome detail. At another point
in the film, a sexy hitchhiker comes on to Cliff from the passenger seat of his car; the prose
version gives us the full-on, NSFW lowdown on exactly the show she puts on for him as he drives.

_Once Upon a Time in Hollywood_ the book is not especially successful as a freestanding work of
fiction—it reads at times like a promising screenplay treatment in dire need of an edit—but it
provides a fascinating snapshot of the way its author’s mind has continued to build out a
fictional alt-film-history universe that has by now been obsessing him for years. That description
may understandably seal the deal for Tarantino haters (of which I have been one in the past!)
looking for an excuse not to see _The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth_. But it shouldn’t
necessarily deter Tarantino skeptics, especially those who, like me, found _Once Upon a Time_ to be
among the best and most mature works of the filmmaker’s career.

_The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth_ picks up Cliff’s story in 1977, eight years after the
events of _Once Upon a Time_. The script is by Tarantino, whose fascination for rearranging the
elaborate dollhouse he created continues unabated, but this time the direction has been handed off
to David Fincher. Brad Pitt returns as Cliff Booth—five words that could easily serve as this
movie’s title, given how crucial that star’s particular charisma is to what seems to be less a
sequel than what Pitt has described as an “episode” in the life of the character. Think of it as
a video game side quest, or one of those episodes in a contemporary streaming show that focus
entirely on a previously sidelined cast member. _The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth_ never
really settles on what its genre identity is, a choice that comes off less as a formally bold
gesture than as a laconic unwillingness to commit. Then again, “laconic unwillingness to commit”
is pretty much the default vibe exuded by this movie’s hero, who’s all loopy banter and So-Cal
zen until it comes time—and come time it will—to kick some serious bad-guy ass.

As we first meet 1977 Cliff, he is no longer doubling for Rick Dalton, who has moved to Australia to
star in a Western TV show called _Dingo Dan_. Cliff lives in a modest Marina del Rey apartment with
his elderly but still mentally sharp father (_RoboCop_’s Peter Weller), who runs a pawnshop by day
and spends the rest of his time watching old movies on TV, often accompanied by a running commentary
of behind-the-scenes gossip from his raconteur son. Cliff makes a living in three very Tarantinian
ways: He’s a part-time projectionist at the New Beverly, a venue the director now owns and runs as
an all-celluloid repertory house. He tends bar at a dive called Lucy’s, where he’s known for his
elaborate rum-and-fruit concoctions. And most dangerously and lucratively, Cliff moonlights as a
fixer for Hollywood bigwigs. In one early scene, that includes a closeted film director whose Oscar
statuette has been stolen by a young gay hustler (Corey Fogelmanis), who in turn is apparently being
controlled by an older man acting as his pimp (_Anora_’s Karren Karagulian). It’s an issue Cliff
addresses in a manner that shows us both his softer side (he arranges for the exploited younger man
to get out of the situation safely) and the brutality of which heis capable when he deems it
necessary (the “chicken hawk” pimp doesn’t get off so easily).

When Cliff gets called into a meeting by the agent of TV star Charlie Peters (Scott Caan, the son
of, and near visual double for, James), the veteran stuntman is confused for two reasons. One,
Peters is famous for doing his own stunts, as we witness in a mini-montage of his career as a
small-screen action hero that’s narrated by Tarantino himself, and two, Charlie is far too short
to be doubled by Cliff. Indeed, the diminutive actor is towered over by his agent, Karen Crosby
(Elizabeth Debicki), a lanky beauty who radiates a self-possessed cool to rival Cliff’s own.

As it turns out, Charlie is interested in Cliff’s skills not as a pratfall-taker but as an
all-purpose problem solver. On a party trip to Manila some time back, Charlie made the mistake of
drunkenly bonding with two Filipino hit men, the Verdusco brothers (JB Tadena and Matt Groove), and
offering them money to murder his seemingly quite delightful wife (Carla Gugino), a job he now
realizes was a terrible idea. But the Verduscos are set on collecting their full fee—and, as
established in an early scene where we see them carrying out a particularly ruthless home invasion,
these are dudes who will carry out their contracted missions, not just faithfully, but with casually
sadistic glee.

That setup—lethal hired killers, behind-the-scenes Hollywood intrigue, and a charming slow-boil
flirtation between the quick-witted Karen and the increasingly smitten Cliff—would seem to provide
material enough for a satisfying genre romp, but instead _The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff
Booth_’s two-hour-and-20-minute runtime feels as shaggy as its hero’s feathered tresses. Scene
by scene, the story unfolds nimbly enough, with Tarantino’s trademark banter and plenty of
well-choreographed action scenes, including a shoot-’em-up finale that matches _Once Upon a Time
in Hollywood_’s in go-for-broke bravado. But given this film’s loping, episodic
pacing—basically it’s a handful of violent set pieces separated by long stretches of low-stakes
hanging out—I found myself struggling to invest in either the resolution of the crime plot,
however cleverly laid out, or the final outcome of Cliff and Karen’s flirty badinage, however
skillfully scripted.

Debicki, always a standout, walks away with the movie in her too-rare scenes with Pitt. It’s a
shame we don’t get to see more of what happens in Karen’s life when she _isn’t_ either
parrying Cliff’s well-worn pickup lines or scrambling to stay ahead of the bad choices of her
hotheaded client. And without giving away too much about the ending, I can say that it’s a big
disappointment that we never get another glimpse of Karen after the climactic
dude-on-dude-on-other-dude fight sequence. Why spend so much time developing such a complex female
character only to sideline her like a generic love interest as soon as the punches, bullets, and
hammer blows start flying?

The imaginative production design by Donald Graham Burt and the witty costumes by Trish
Summerville—in particular the glamorously draped all-white wardrobe she creates for the statuesque
Debicki—make _The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth_ a pleasure to look at, even if
Fincher’s underlit digital photography (the cinematographer is Erik Messerschmidt, who also shot
Fincher’s 2020 old-Hollywood-set drama _Mank_) is an odd match with the movie’s sun-drenched
setting. In the end, this unhurried meander through the elaborate fictional universe that exists in
such detail in the screenwriter’s brain achieves what the wry Karen, a former film critic,
describes in one scene as pretty much the highest goal any mainstream movie can aim for: It’s the
equivalent of a decent fast-food hamburger. But that burger might leave behind an oily aftertaste,
given recent revelations about both the star’s attempt to launder his reputation after an ugly
divorce and the director’s apparent use of A.I. Even the movie’s strongest element, the
screwball-worthy chemistry between Pitt and Debicki, takes on a different flavor when you consider
the 26-year gap in the actors’ ages.

Word on the street is that Tarantino has another novel set to release on Dec. 8, right in between
this movie’s brief theatrical release on Thanksgiving weekend and its Dec. 23 streaming debut on
Netflix. The new book will be dedicated entirely to the character of Cliff Booth—who, as it turns
out, may be one of those characters who would do best to preserve an aura of mystery in order to
keep us caring about them.
