A cricketing drama to divide a nation: why the sandpaper scandal is Australia's Bazball | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

Any plans for the weekend? Personally I'm all set. I will be watching the full Pat Cummins sandpaper
denial press conference, filmed live on location in Sydney on Friday morning, and now preserved
uncut and un-redacted on YouTube.

I will not speed it up, or flick through impatiently only to find myself bogged down in endless
adverts for old fat guys doing military calisthenics, and pills that will dissolve the vile writhing
parasites that definitely live in my guts.

This is culturally vital material. As such I will be watching it at least five times, with periods
set aside for reflection, notation and, as necessary, stabbing myself in the eyeballs with a
sharpened kebab skewer just to dull the pain.

The point is, this is not a task to be undertaken lightly. You will need breaks. You will need a
sense of hair-shirt necessity, like forcing yourself to watch a solemnly repetitive arthouse film
where people are just always smoking and talking and looking sad. At one hour and eight minutes, the
Cummins presser is a bit shorter than Three Colours: White. It's almost the same length as
Battleship Potemkin, and nobody actually wants to watch that.

There are similarities though. The opening sections of Battleship Potemkin are called Men and
Maggots, Drama on the Deck and A Dead Man Calls for Justice. So yeah, love that. Elite mate-ship.
Battleship Potemkin is also about a heroic worker's revolt, with the lurking sense that everything
here might just succumb to the tide and the forces of history. So yeah, again. Go Team Pat.

It has been said this is a scandal that will make no sense at all to anyone not pre-converted to
Test Match cricket. But a quick 20-second explainer: Australian players used sandpaper to rough up
the ball to their advantage during a Test Match in Cape town eight years ago, at a time when such
outlaw practices were widespread.

The problem is the plan was enacted on the field in full view of the TV cameras. Cricket is a
ball-fixated team game. But three Australian players took full responsibility and had their careers
derailed. The Cummins press conference was in response to claims in Usman Khawaja's new book that in
fact the entire team was in on it. Subsequent investigations had somehow failed to detect this, but
did make the whole thing go away. And: breathe.

The best bit of the one hour, eight minutes is the start. Cummins looks the same: lean,
super-handsome, eyes sparkling, like the future cyborg-captain of the intergalactic space fleet. But
he is also unusually hyped-up, voice cracking as he gets his prepared lines in at the outset.

Khawaja's book is, he says, a series of "allegations" that he is still "trying to understand".
Although, to be clear, trying to understand without having actually read it in full. Crucially,
Cummins states that if Khawaja is saying "we knew sandpaper was going out on to the field then he's
100% wrong". Note how the middle part of that sentence qualifies the ending. Going out on to the
field has always been key. Note also the way this replicates the statement by Australia's bowlers in
2021. Execute. Discipline. Stick to the process.

Cummins shifts on to more general notes. We were part of a team. Usman is a friend. Usman has also
betrayed not just me but all of Australian cricket. He then opens up some distance by pointing out
he was 24 and playing his 13th Test at the time. Which is true. But then, the entire Cummins persona
is also built on presenting himself as the opposite of that moment.

Clearly, this is an attempt at containment. Cummins says he will not be talking about it again. But
like it or not, these moments capture you. What we have here is a man trying desperately not to
become a meme, while visibly turning into one before our eyes.

What is happening here, really? It is hard to overstate how massive this story is in Australia. At
the same time it feels like nothing at all, an entity without content beyond its own emotional hot
air. On TalkSport on Friday morning, Alan Brazil asked the editor of Wisden, Lawrence Booth, if the
Australian team was ripping itself apart over this, if England might take advantage. Er, actually
no. That same day an Australian team with different names was gleefully tearing England apart in
Perth, with Mahli Beardman – nickname suggestions: Bob, Jacob, "And Me" – taking six debut
wickets.

But then Beardman was 14 years old when this happened and would surely be quite happy if the old
lags faded from view. This is key to the sage, and to the sport. In cricket you basically hate your
teammates. This is an individual pursuit masquerading as a team sport. You win together. But also,
you don't really. This is hard numbers, independent value, a series of conjoined duels.

Sandpapergate is a public airing of that strange internal dynamic. But it also offers a perfect
mini-anatomy not just of Australian cricket, but of Australia itself. A key fact: nobody else
actually cares about Sandpapergate. Initially the ICC handed out one-match bans, which seemed fair
enough. Meanwhile Australia frothed and blamed, imposed ludicrously one-year exiles from all
cricket, and banned David Warner from ever captaining anyone ever again.

At moments like these you remember what a culturally sui generis place Australia is, beautiful as a
stage set, cities ranged warily around the coastline, a place that is wealthy, free, unspoilt, and
still embracing something while also rejecting it.

It might come as a surprise, but English people love Australians. They really do, to the extent it
is often a shock to realise this is not always entirely reciprocated. There is an edge there. These
are not in fact your fun country cousins, but a former settlement with its own set of deep
structural recalibrations still in train.

And of course cricket, that absurd scratchy formal dance, is the perfect sporting vessel. In
Australia cricket has been defined by a terse but fraternal conservatism. Cricket is about
toughness, resilience, the content of your character. Crucially it is about playing correctly, about
being more resolute, more disciplined, more worthy (let us say it: than the English), about
presenting yourself to the room, while also being better and more authentic than the room.

One reason Australians hate the sandpaper affair, the reason it hits so hard, is because it's
sneaky. It's embarrassing. It's mean, small, pigeon-chested. It's basically quite English. And
cricket is supposed to tell you that you're the opposite of this, to offer hard, sunlit legitimacy,
to answer the questions: are we fine now, are we good, out here still facing westwards from
thousands of miles away, still trying to be better, straighter, tougher?

In a way Sandpapergate is Australia's Bazball, another bizarre cricketing contortion, whose energy
was almost entirely inward-facing. Bazball was a simple tactic, the self-congratulatory urge to bash
a cricket ball around in a fast-forward, attacking manner. It was only in England that this seemed
vital and seductive and profound, in those few giddy months before it dissolved in its own cult-like
amour propre. The reason it hit that sweet spot, why it both enraged and delighted, is that cricket
in England is about class, privilege, hierarchy, the question of who owns the space, who gets to
decide the mood.

And the key quality of Bazball was its deep cultural mnemonic, the gentleman-amateur style, the idea
of winning from above the fray, winning while saying, oh you didn't actually think any of that stuff
really mattered, did you, old boy. Even the body language was maddening, Ben Stokes on the balcony,
vest, shades, jawline, muscular insouciance, Brendan McCullum with his feet up, oozing careless
alpha-dog energy. Even when we lose we win. Because we are us, doing this.

Nobody else out there really gave a toss about all this, beyond the fact it was undeniably annoying
to play against, and deeply satisfying to beat it. This was English cricket speaking to itself, and
to the way it wants to be seen. Bazball was unearned exceptionalism, faux amateur nobility. Bazball
was _honi soit qui mal y pense_ in a daring reverse scoop new-ball hoick. It was also doomed and
phoney, unable to progress because it wasn't really anything but a style.

But that was English cricket's own psychodrama, in the time of shrinkage and lost edge. And now
Australia have this. It is obvious enough what should happen. No more bans. No more hand-wringing.
An apology to the players who were over-punished. A rooting out of the administrators who mismanaged
this farrago of weirdness when it all went down. Then let's get on with the game.

Yeah, good luck with that. In reality this can never resolve itself with logic or cold compromise,
because it's about change, identity, what remains and what matters, about seeing a world in a grain
of sandpaper. One hour and eight minutes. Frankly, we're just getting started.
