An Acclaimed Novelist’s New Book Is Supposedly a “Literary Assassination” of Natalie Portman. I’ve Read It. It’s Something Much Weirder.

====================================================================================================

Cusk is an experimental novelist, one known for incorporating elements from her own life into her
fiction, and _Life of M_, which comes out on Aug. 25, is particularly tricksterish piece of work in
that respect. From the beginning of the novel, Cusk hints broadly at the dubious veracity of her
(unnamed) narrator’s account of M’s life. In the first sentence, the narrator recalls telling M
that “I was thinking of writing her autobiography.” An autobiography is, of course, written by
its own subject, and since the narrator isn’t offering to work as M’s ghostwriter but to produce
the book entirely on her own, the statement is nonsensical. M finds it funny. “Would you make it
all up?” she asks her friend. That’s the first clue that the novel’s initial, scandal-greedy
readers have failed to pick up on.

The first chapter of _Life of M_ provides some background on the friendship between M and the
narrator, in which M relates some of the bizarre and lamentable aspects of her fame and profession,
including a starring role in a film at the age of 13 that “ended her childhood.” (This scans
with Portman’s feature-film debut in the 1994 movie _Léon: The Professional_, directed by the
notorious creep Luc Besson.) M tells the narrator that she prefers to use what power her fame has
given her to make her life as “normal” as possible. Nevertheless, when the pair arrange to meet
for coffee in a hotel garden, only to find the garden closed for the winter, the staff readily
reopens it just for them. “This was how people and things behaved around her,” the narrator
observes. “Reality became malleable, was always giving way and changing its rules.”

Until the final chapter of the novel, M and the narrator won’t meet again. Instead, _Life of
M_ alternates between scenes from the lives of both the narrator and “M.” That’s “M” in
quotes because by the time the reader gets to that last chapter, it has become increasingly apparent
that the passages describing M’s life are, for the most part, mere speculation on the narrator’s
part. After the first chapter, the narration switches from first-person singular to first-person
plural, as the narrator moves through obsessions familiar to readers of Cusk’s previous novels:
frustration over the inauthenticity of contemporary life, the instability of identity, and a sort of
neurasthenic aestheticism that makes simple activities like going to a museum distressing if the
other visitors respond to the art in a too-boisterous manner.

At first, the “we” in these chapters seems to be the narrator and her partner, who has a health
crisis at the beginning of the book. Later, the “we” apparently refers to a solitary woman, or
perhaps two women—namely the narrator and the imaginary version of M she has concocted for the M
passages in the novel. These passages almost always begin with the words “It is a” followed by
the name of a setting: a brasserie, a posh members-only spa, a Mediterranean villa, a wooden
schooner, a corporate conference room, an acting class. These settings are described in minute
detail, descriptions that occasionally dip into clichés right out of the most voyeuristic travel
writing or promotional brochure: “richly embroidered curtains” in a stately home or a
minimalist interior design that “asserts simplicity as the ultimate luxury.”

Compared to the attention the novel lavishes on these settings, the scenes that play out in them
tend to be sketched rather than dramatized. M, for unknown reasons, submits to being scolded by some
guy for unspecified shortcomings. She gets a massage with an actress friend. A journalist interviews
her in a trendy restaurant in the unnamed European city where she lives. She attends an art opening
for an artist she once patronized. None of these scenes have anything to do with or even mention
M’s intimate relationships. Her children are referred to glancingly, and no marriage or romantic
partnership ever comes up. As gossip goes, even if these passages were really about Portman, who
tends to keep her personal life private, they’re pretty thin gruel.

The narrator of _Life of M_ mostly seems interested in M’s hopeless desire to make contact
with “reality,” her unsureness about who she truly is, the impossibility of “sincerity,”
and her abiding sense that she is mere performing herself. Anyone who prides herself on the
refinement of her taste as much as Cusk does would know that making observations like these about an
actor is banal, as is a line comparing M’s home in the city to a “big doll house, with her as
the doll inside it.” And anyone who has read even a little of Cusk’s previous work will
recognize that these concerns map suspiciously closely to Cusk’s own preoccupations with
artificiality.

The novel’s final chapter confirms those suspicions. Suddenly, the narration reverts to first
person, as if the narrator were snapping out of a reverie in which her own identity had merged with
M’s. M has read the “project” and declares it “hurtful,” complaining that not only had
she never been to any of the places so painstakingly described in the chapters we have just read,
but she has also never done any of the things the narrator has reported. The narrator seems
surprised that anyone would think otherwise. “The character was invented by me,” she explains.
So if that character has any faults, “then it’s me that it’s true of.”

“I don’t know anything about your private life,” the narrator goes on. “I don’t know
anything about your character.” She hadn’t even googled M, just “watched your films, like
anyone can,” a statement that, upon reflecting on the preceding chapters, seems perfectly
accurate. Fantasizing a version of M based on her movie roles allowed the narrator to “go and
live in someone else,” the way she imagines an actor like M does in her own work. Ironically, the
character she created to live in had exactly the same problems Cusk’s main characters usually
have, only with more money and power.

The final sentence in _Life of M_ is so bizarre and disorienting it throws even these revelations
into chaos. As the writer departs, M stops her at the door, announcing there’s something she’s
always wanted to ask her. Then M smiles “warmly and almost goofily,” and says, “This might
sound stupid, but I’ve been wondering. Are you a guy or a girl?” Since Rachel Cusk is quite
obviously the latter, what can this possibly mean? Only that Cusk is screwing with us and perhaps
suggesting that not only is M not Natalie Portman, but the narrator isn’t even Cusk.

_Life of M_ is a narrative hall of mirrors that only a fool would confuse with a thinly veiled
roman à clef about a celebrity. To my mind, only one true mystery remains: Was Portman in on it? Is
she just pretending to be offended for a lark, or in a bid to drive interest in her friend’s book?
As of this writing, neither woman has made a public statement on the matter, but I’m not sure if
we can believe either one of them when they do.
