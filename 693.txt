

====================================================================================================

It Might Be the Most Popular TV Show in the World. It Keeps Getting Worse and Worse.

====================================================================================================

Greetings, it is I, a traveler from the year 2016. I have come to your strange time not to see how
things worked out with that Donald Trump character or even whether everyone is still losing their
heads over _Hamilton_, but to remind you of something you may have forgotten: _Stranger Things_ used
to be good.

Back in my time, you see, ’80s nostalgia was a relatively new phenomenon, and Matt and Ross Duffer
approached the period and the pop culture that defined it—Steven Spielberg’s suburban
mythmaking, Stephen King’s tales of small-town horror—with such reverence and attention to
detail that it felt at times as if someone had discovered the episodes in the back of an abandoned
Blockbuster. Poised midway between their fresh-faced tween protagonists and their worse-for-wear
parents, the then-32-year-old Duffer twins connected with the crumbling innocence of children who
are just starting to learn about the terrors and disappointments of adulthood, the monsters that
hide in the dark and the moms and dads who have forgotten how they ever loved each other.

Watching _Stranger Things_’ fifth and final season, it’s hard to remember that this earlier show
ever existed. The series’ massive success has given the Duffers practically unlimited
resources—the new season, by some reports, cost as much as $480 million, 10 times the cost of the
first, and more than any _Avengers_ movie—but they’ve increased its scale while narrowing its
scope. We learn within the first few minutes of the new season that Hawkins, Indiana, is under
military quarantine, with some story about unexplained phenomena covering up for the Vecna-spawned
earthquakes that tore a giant red rift down the middle of the sleepy town. But it’s not just
Hawkins that feels cut off from the world. It’s _Stranger Things_ itself, a show now sealed in an
airless, impenetrable bubble of stagnant characters and snarled lore.

After a quick flashback to the night of Will Byers’ abduction from the very first episode, the
fifth season jumps forward four years to the end of 1987, which puts its principal characters—Will
(Noah Schnapp), Mike (Finn Wolfhard), Dustin (Gaten Matarazzo), Lucas (Caleb McLaughlin), and Eleven
(Millie Bobby Brown)—into their midteens. Given that it’s been 10 years since _Stranger Things_
started filming, and more than three since the last episode of Season 4 was released, the show had
to choose between acknowledging the passage of time or looking like Ben Platt in _Dear Evan Hansen_.
(McLaughlin, the eldest of the young actors, is now 24, eight years older than his character.) For
one thing, it’s not nearly long enough to account for the actors’ fully adult appearances:
Though it’s been a decade since the show went into production, the characters have aged, in total,
less than half that. (Schnapp in particular seems to have been draped in layers of baggy clothes in
a vain attempt to make him resemble a scrawny 16-year-old.) But it also creates narrative issues the
show seems to have no interest in addressing, namely what would happen to a nondescript Midwestern
town if the government cut it off from the rest of the country for the better part of a year.

The end of _Stranger Things_’ fourth season seemed to promise, even mandate, that the existence of
the demonic alternate dimension known as the Upside Down would become a matter of public record,
rather than a secret shared by a handful of heartland nerds and the governments of several
superpowers. But the Duffers hand-wave that possibility with a few brute-force sentences about the
“giant metal Band-Aid” the military has used to cover the fissures that literally split Hawkins
in two, a cataclysm the community evidently now considers so humdrum that the kids use those metal
plates for warm-weather sledding.

Given the way the show’s early seasons tapped into Cold War paranoia and the Satanic Panic of the
1980s, affirmative proof of government conspiracies and supernatural goings-on ought to send Hawkins
into a wide-scale panic. Instead, life appears to have continued on much as usual: Mike’s parents
still hate each other, Dustin is still getting bullied (although he’s now more inclined to fight
back), and Will is still, obviously, gay, despite the fact that no one seems ready to admit it.
Granted, the 1980s were a less enlightened, more openly homophobic time, but assuming that the show
is saving its big reveal for its final episodes—after this batch of four, three more arrive
Christmas Day, with the series finale on Dec. 31—the show will have spent something on the order
of 40 hours building up to the bombshell that one of its male characters likes boys.

You’d think his mother, Joyce (Winona Ryder), would at least have figured out by now. But the show
that restarted Ryder’s career nearly a decade ago no longer seems to know what to do with her.
Joyce’s determination to find her son no matter how crazy her attempts might look drove _Stranger
Things_’ earliest episodes; she hammered Christmas lights to the wall with as much manic intensity
as Richard Dreyfuss brought to sculpting mashed potatoes in _Close Encounters of the Third Kind_.
Now, though, she merely seems frantic, as the show’s kids have become more self-sufficient (and
more handy with firearms) and as Jim Hopper (David Harbour) has transformed from a potbellied police
chief into a sword-wielding action hero. What’s a mom to do, except remind her son not to go off
and fight Vecna without first putting on a hat?

About Vecna—at one point in the season, a new character requires quick instruction in the nature
of _Stranger Things_’ big bad, and after one person refers to him as Vecna, another as Henry
Creel, and a third as One, the newcomer cries out in exasperation, “That’s too many names!” If
nothing else, the feeble attempt at transferring the franchise to the Broadway stage—and
especially the documentary about the chaos behind its creation—revealed that the Duffers have
never had either a master plan or, more fatally, the skill to write themselves out of the corners in
which their ad hoc storytelling leaves them stranded. So they fall back on fan service and
pop-culture scavenging, with the fifth season’s major innovation being that the reference points
shift from _Dungeons & Dragons_ to _A Wrinkle in Time_.

One of TV’s most particular pleasures is getting to watch a show’s cast grow up in something
resembling real time. It’s like living with the characters in a way that movies have to go to
extraordinary lengths to emulate. And _Stranger Things_’ cast has grown, maturing into actors more
than capable of carrying their own projects. But the show hasn’t matured with them. Like
Netflix’s other reigning auteurs—the Russos, Zack Snyder—the Duffers have been given free rein
to indulge, and have used that freedom to repeat themselves, over and over again.
