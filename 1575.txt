One of the Biggest Questions of the World Cup Comes From a Country With a Unique Name

====================================================================================================

Herzegovina is having a moment. Despite its accounting for half of the name of the Balkan nation
Bosnia and Herzegovina, its residents have long suffered the indignity of Westerners using _Bosnia_
as shorthand for the country. But something is briefly changing: Thanks to FIFA’s insistence on a
strict style guide—TV announcers are stating the full name, not just _Bosnia_—Herzegovina is
finally getting its due. Worldwide search interest in _Bosnia_ will always far outpace
_Herzegovina_, but the 2026 World Cup has proved to be Herzegovina’s search breakout. There’s a
good chance that’s why you’re on this page. If so, welcome! Hopefully, the United States men’s
national team will be leading our Balkan friends by the time you read this.

One of my best friends is a Bosnian immigrant, so I’ve had the pleasure of meeting lots of
Bosnians. Herzegovina has remained a mystery, though. The area is roughly a quarter of the
country’s landmass. Exactly how many people live in Herzegovina? It’d be guesswork, as this part
of Bosnia and Herzegovina has no defined border. The region’s biggest city, Mostar, is only the
sixth-most-populous nationwide. There’s no authoritative data on how many Herzegovinians live in
the U.S., only a rational conclusion that there are far fewer of them than those from elsewhere in
the country. Some might identify themselves as Croatian in surveys or even use the international
shorthand “Bosnian.”

What, then, is this region’s deal? It’s located in the south of the nation of Bosnia and
Herzegovina, and the area almost reaches the Adriatic Sea before the long southern sliver of
Croatia, including Dubrovnik, interrupts it. It’s not defined by one ethnic identity or a
different system of governance. Given that, why are the words _and Herzegovina_ attached to the most
commonly uttered name of the country? These are fair questions, and in fact they were _my_ questions
when I called a leading Balkan historian, a Sarajevo native, this week. Now I think these are the
wrong questions. The right question may in fact be: Why don’t _most_ countries have names like
Bosnia and Herzegovina?

“If I had my perspective to convey to the larger public, it would be to start from not considering
_Bosnia-Herzegovina_ as an anomaly,” said Edin Hajdarpašić, an associate professor who teaches
about the Balkans at Loyola University Chicago. “It’s a perfectly normal name for a country. If
there is a strange name for a country, it would be ‘the United States.’ ‘The United States of
America’? Why are there three parts?”

Hajdarpašić is not merely being edgy. Every country larger than Vatican City is an amalgamation of
places that may or may not have much in common with one another or possess one neat, shared
identity. And yet, on the United Nations’ list of members, as close a reference as you’ll find
anywhere to “the world’s agreed-upon names for all the countries,” you can spot efforts to
tidy things up. _United States_ is one way to do it. (United by what? Not everything!) _Solomon
Islands_ is another. (Are we to presume they’re all the same?) Only six countries are willing to
name themselves thusly, though: Antigua and Barbuda, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Saint Kitts and Nevis,
Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Trinidad and Tobago. _The United
Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland_ nominally counts but is rarely enunciated that way,
even in the most formal settings.

“I would say a lot of misconceptions start with a starting point that views joint-compound names
as somehow requiring explanation,” Hajdarpašić said. Compound political names used to be quite
normal, including for some of the world’s most powerful nations (hello, Austria-Hungary!).
Hajdarpašić points next door to Croatia, whose political identity was long discussed through
Croatia, Slavonia, and Dalmatia, before _Croatia_ became the dominant name and the others remained
regional identities. Bosnia and Herzegovina took the road less traveled, and didn’t need the name
to roll off the tongue.

That the longer name endures feels poetic, given the region’s history. Bosnia and Herzegovina was
one of the six Yugoslav socialist republics, and its demography was a big mixture: Bosniaks
(historically Muslim), Croats (mostly Catholic), Serbs (mostly Orthodox Christian), and more.
“When Yugoslavia was dismembered by nationalist politicians starting in the late 1980s, they
really focused on Bosnia as the place where communities were most diverse,” Hajdarpašić said.
“What they wanted to do is separate them. They quite literally spoke of ethnic cleansing as a
means of accomplishing that.”

The ensuing Bosnian war was not exactly Bosnia against Herzegovina, so the long-form name was not
about everyone getting a piece of a country that came to peace after a violent, drawn-out slog. The
war pitted the internationally recognized government (Bosnia and Herzegovina, based in Sarajevo)
against divisive political projects that wanted to carve the country up based on religion and kill
or expel anyone who didn’t fit into their vision. This led to mass displacement and the genocide
at Srebrenica, for which several perpetrators were convicted at the International Criminal Tribunal
for the former Yugoslavia. The country that lives on to this day, Hajdarpašić said, is “a much
more divided country than it was at any time before in its history.” There was no good reason the
name needed to fracture too.

Thus far in this World Cup, the big story about American attitudes toward Bosnia and Herzegovina was
that of a TV reporter’s bad joke. Seemingly trying for some lighthearted sports patriotism but
overshooting her target, the announcer noted that she couldn’t find Bosnia on a map. “I don’t
know the first thing about Bosnia, and I don’t want to know. That’s because, Team USA, we’re
back! We’re better than ever.” She apologized, hopefully ending the saga of the bad joke and
maybe even providing us the occasion to remember that we too are not immediately intelligible and
understandable to the rest of the world. Hajdarpašić says he found much about America
“baffling” when he arrived as a 16-year-old during the war.

“It’s an occasion for Americans, but also for everyone, to reflect on their habits or their
place in the world,” he said. In our moment of reflection, we might take one lesson from Bosnia
and Herzegovina: A country cannot cohesively name its way to national unity, and it may well be best
not to try.
