

====================================================================================================

The Witch Farm review – this creepy tale is laugh-out-loud funny … without meaning to be

====================================================================================================

A lot of strange and scary stuff happened in 1989 at Heol Fanog, a lonely farmhouse at the foot of
Bannau Brycheiniog (formerly known as the Brecon Beacons). English artist Bill Rich and his pregnant
Welsh partner, Liz, moved there with Bill's teenage son to begin again, replacing the complexity of
urban life with the still silence of the remote countryside. But their new start became a protracted
nightmare when they experienced what seemed to be supernatural phenomena – and the Heol Fanog
ghosts would keep appearing to the Rich family for another seven years.

The case – nicknamed the "Welsh Amityville" for its similarities to the US's most famous instance
of reported paranormal activity – has long haunted Danny Robins, presenter of the podcast and BBC
Two TV series Uncanny, in which he investigates claims of interactions with the undead. Heol Fanog
stands out because of the sheer number of eerie events, the length of time across which they took
place, and the multiple witnesses: if you can explain this, what about that? Or that? Or, oh my God,
THAT?

In 2022, Robins made a nine-part BBC audio documentary about Heol Fanog, with Joseph Fiennes and
Alexandra Roach playing the Riches in dramatised inserts, but the itch persists and so here is a
full-blown drama, written by Robins, telling the tale again. Michael Socha and Gabrielle Creevy play
the doomed couple, with Lewin Lloyd as Bill's son, Jonathan.

[A man and his teenage son embrace tightly in a dimly lit room, a painting of a horse behind them]
Robins's factual programmes can be frustrating for those of us who are sure that death is final,
that the departed cannot communicate with us, and that if they did they probably wouldn't choose
annoyingly obscure and ambiguous methods such as slamming doors, knocking ornaments off shelves and
making noises in the night. He comes across as a sceptic at heart, and Uncanny's format includes two
warring "experts", one of whom is a parapsychologist who puts forward earthly explanations for
supposedly spooky goings-on. But too much reason would spoil the fun and Robins, a skilled showman,
typically lets the science guy's objections be overwhelmed by a tide of intoxicating hogwash.

Repackaging the saga of Heol Fanog as fiction allows Robins, and us, to loosen up. Yes, it's based
on fact, but the writer is under no obligation to stick precisely to what is known to be true.
Ghosts can just be real, and viewers who disagree in everyday life can – at night, in front of a
screen – still suspend disbelief and enjoy a good yarn.

The Witch Farm has classic ghost-story elements in abundance. Bill, Liz and Jonathan are a unit
riddled with vulnerabilities, from Jonathan's abiding resentment at Bill's role in the end of his
marriage to Jonathan's mother, to Bill's currently dormant alcoholism and stalled career as an
artist. Liz is about to have a baby with a man we sense is a risky choice for her – Socha's skill
in portraying matey charmers in denial about the consequences of their own weakness means he's very
well cast here – and the trio are, by buying such a gnarly property, messing with things they
don't understand, possibly including Bill the Englishman having rashly moved to rural Wales. The
house is rough and rickety with odd, dusty junk in the attic; to ram home how unsettling the place
is, there's a derelict stone building at the bottom of the garden, its old cellar now a seemingly
bottomless pond, the surface of which is a pitiless black mirror. The farmer who lives up the road
(played by Robert Pugh) is a bit frosty as well.

But because the story of Heol Fanog has so much in it, even a four-episode series has to move
uncomfortably fast. The Witch Farm can't hang about if it's going to cover the plentiful aberrations
turned up by the original podcast series, which touched on historical murders, ancient curses and
even ley lines in addition to regular visions of the dead. Consequently, it breaks the rule that
horror stories have to wait as long as possible before showing us the monster.

There's no slowly accumulating dread here: it's bedlam in that house from day one and, although the
show makes some use of claustrophobic closeups, sudden switches to shaking handheld cameras and
liminal glimpses of the atrocious – there's one shot of a swiftly retreating spectre that does
chill the hackles – it soon abandons the effort to build atmosphere and shows its hand instead.
Our first clear sighting of what the Riches are haunted by arrives far too early and is so unsubtle,
it's laugh-out-loud funny.

Liz's new-mum insecurities, Jonathan's turbulent adolescence and Bill's mental fragility give the
drama all it needs to be a powerful ghost story. But the best ones exploit the idea of malevolent
phantoms as allegories for human frailties, and The Witch Farm's genesis means it hasn't time for
that. This is one for true believers.
