====================================================================================================

The arrival of a straight-to-Netflix movie based on a TV show that ended five years ago might seem
like a less-than-festive occasion, another example of the entertainment industry’s grim
determination to milk every piece of successful IP down to the last flavorless drop. But for fans of
the French comedy series _Call My Agent!_, which ran for four effervescent seasons before coming to
a confusingly abrupt halt, _Call My Agent! The Movie_ comes as a long-awaited chance to find out
what happened to the show’s characters, the frazzled staff of a Paris-based talent agency.

_Call My Agent!_ was a show-business satire of a sort. It poked endless fun at its petty, conniving,
yet lovable main characters, and in a running conceit, every episode featured a real-life movie star
playing him or herself. These were not cameos but central roles as the problem client of the week,
the one whose whims or neuroses threatened to scotch their projects with the agency. This sent the
stars’ agents scurrying to placate, dissuade, chasten, or threaten them as needed, even as those
agents squabbled and backstabbed among themselves, all while Frenchly having affairs in the
workplace and everywhere else.

For all its tart humor and eye for human weakness, _Call My Agent!_ believed deep down in the power
of a good movie. Most of the show’s ongoing conflicts had to do with the tug-of-war between art
and commerce, between integrity (however comically misunderstood) and greed (however
self-delusionally denied). The ASK agency, an independent shop that is eventually absorbed by a
corporate giant, was easy to side with, despite its dysfunction. The work the agents
did—shepherding ambitious projects to completion, protecting artists from exploitation or from
their own worst impulses—was at heart worthwhile. My theory as to why _Call My Agent!_ took off
during the pandemic, when it had been streaming on Netflix since 2016, is that the show’s vision
of a stressful yet glamorous workplace was frothy enough to make for fun viewing, while being
emotionally satisfying enough to provide some of the productive interpersonal tension we were all
missing.

In the years since _Call My Agent!_ ended, there has been confusion about whether a fifth season, a
movie, or both were in development, and the existence of this feature-length installment does not
preclude the possibility that an episodic reboot is still in the works. Creator Fanny Herrero, who
wrote or co-wrote every episode until she exited the show at the end of the third season, makes a
welcome return as co-screenwriter (with Lison Daniel), while Émilie Noblet directs. All the
show’s main characters are back: Andréa (Camille Cottin) is now a would-be indie director trying
to cast and finance her first movie. Andréa’s former best friend and colleague Gabriel (Grégory
Montel) is now an agent at StarMédia, the soulless conglomerate that was ASK’s longtime rival
before taking them over. Noémie (Laure Calamy), who started her career as the executive assistant
to senior agent Mathias (Thibault de Montalembert), is now his dissatisfied live-in girlfriend and
the producer of a successful but vapid reality show. Meanwhile, Mathias has launched his own talent
agency with his illegitimate daughter Camille (Fanny Sidney), who is still angry about her father
hiding her existence from his other family for two decades. (It’s a Season 1 thing.)

Since this movie is about twice the length of a _Call My Agent!_ episode, it’s fitting that there
are not one but two guest stars playing themselves: Vincent Macaigne, an actor, theater director,
and playwright, spoofs his own hyper-sincere persona as a tortured artist whose guilt over his
unearned privilege is sabotaging his ability to work. Laetitia Casta also plays a slightly tweaked
version of herself, a model turned actress whose past romance with Vincent remains unresolved. (In
real life Casta recently divorced her high-profile husband, the actor and filmmaker Louis Garrel.)
When the lead actor in Andréa’s debut film (Laurent Lafitte) deserts the project to take a
supporting role in a Wes Anderson movie (sadly, that director does not appear as his corduroy-clad
self), she has an ill-fated meeting with George Clooney (who does play himself, with wry humor).
Eventually Andréa winds up casting Vincent in the lead despite her doubts as to his emotional
stability. Similarly unwise is her choice to bring Noémie on as producer, given their clashing
personality styles: The dryly understated Andréa dreams of artistic seriousness, while the bubbly,
volatile Noémie has a keen business sense but tastes that run to the conventional.

As Andréa’s independently financed film threatens to fall apart in every way imaginable, from the
delay caused by an on-set injury to the intractability of a wild boar who arrives on set with a
handler and agent of her own, the former staff of ASK begins to reconstitute itself around the
project, unsettled grievances and all. Gabriel represents one of the two leads, Camille represents
the other, and Mathias, at a low point in his personal and professional life, represents the boar.
When things get really tough, Andréa puts in a call to her mentor Arlette (Liliane Rovère), a
retired agent with the wisdom of a hardened old-timer. And Hervé (Nicolas Maury), a junior agent
who left ASK to try for a career as an actor, does his best to bring Daniel Day-Lewis–style
authenticity to his one-line part as a boar hunter in the film within a film.

Like the series, _Call My Agent! The Movie_ owes more to the tradition of French boulevard comedy
than it does to the American sitcom, with crisscrossing romantic intrigues and farcical slamming
doors. The laughs come less from the dialogue—though that is spiky, overlapping, and rapid-fire,
worth experiencing in the subtitled rather than the dubbed version—than from the gradual pileup of
bad luck and even worse decisions on the characters’ parts. For all the film’s charm, its final
conflict gets resolved a tad too easily; after making choices that placed her cast and crew in
legitimate danger, Andréa is forgiven pretty hastily by both her colleagues and the screenplay. It
would be overpraising the movie to say that it aims for or accomplishes anything beyond what the
average episode of the series did, but given the height of the bar the show set, that’s not
nothing.

Do you have to have seen _Call My Agent! The Series_ to enjoy _The Movie_? No, but in all honesty,
it would surely enrich the experience. Some of the film’s character arcs, especially the broken
friendship between Andréa and Gabriel, would feel thin without knowing the characters’ long
history as workmates and rivals. But watching your way through all four of this show’s six-episode
seasons would feel less like homework than like getting 24 consecutive deliveries of perfectly baked
Parisian pastry, each with a different-flavored filling. If you haven’t yet given yourself the
pleasure, _régalez-vous_.
