The Hottest Lunch Trend in America Just Ran Into a Very Unpleasant Problem

====================================================================================================

delivered to your inbox daily._

We still don’t know what exactly is driving our national outbreak of cyclosporiasis, the parasitic
infection that has stricken thousands of Americans with explosive diarrhea. In part, you can thank
the Trump administration’s self-sabotaged public health apparatus. But we do know, based on what
history and science tell us, that _some_ vegetables are likely to blame—specifically, the bagged
and mass-processed salad mixes that supply lettuce and mixed greens to big-box supermarkets and
ubiquitous fast-food joints across these United States.

That likely includes Taco Bell—which has now “voluntarily and temporarily” removed some
unnamed ingredients from restaurants located within various Midwestern and Appalachian states—but
none of this is a mere consequence of living _más_. The sheer reach of the _Cyclospora_ parasite
indicates other contributors at play, and despite expert recommendations to the contrary, it’s
already fomenting a mild panic over produce. Shares of publicly traded fast-casual staples like
Sweetgreen, Cava, and Chipotle took steady hits this week, as did T-Bell parent company Yum! Brands.
And sheer uncertainty abounds over _how_ to handle raw fruits and veggies while this parasite crawls
about.

It made me wonder: Are Americans changing up their daily lunch habits and routines, whether it comes
to the daily sandwiches they slap together at home or the “Mediterranean diet” bowls they grab
near the office? Are they buying fewer types of green ingredients in particular or just forgoing
certain places and brands altogether? I found, unsurprisingly, that the steps people have taken vary
quite significantly, but they all have one common denominator: The greens are cooked—in more ways
than one.

I conducted an informal lunchtime field study on Thursday near Slate’s office in Downtown
Brooklyn, an area absolutely silly with “slop bowl” joints, to see how those spots were faring
amid the cyclosporiasis anxiety (and the wildfire smoke). Cava, despite the broader company’s
stock decline, was still packed with salad-hungry customers; so were Naya and Dig Inn. Others
weren’t doing so hot. The cashier at the local Chopt admitted to me that foot traffic had slowed
significantly this week, even before the smoggy conditions set in. Chipotle, as always happens
whenever there’s a _Cyclospora_ or _E. coli_ outbreak, almost seemed as if it had been cleared
out.

Things also looked a little empty at Honeygrow, though the staffers gamely insisted to me that
“we’re good” since the salad chain sources produce from tristate farms instead of the
megacompany that investigators have linked to the Taco Bell fiasco, Taylor Farms. A worker at
Sweetgreen, meanwhile, seemed well prepared for my question, attributing that day’s slowdown to
the weather and informing me that the chain not only gets its vegetables delivered from upstate
farms but “washes them around four times” before adding them to the line.

“Now, the place you _really_ need to watch out for is the supermarkets,” she concluded.
“Because even if you get it in a bag, you can’t just wash the parasite off.”

David Berger, a reader based in Los Angeles, said he and other shoppers were indeed acting
cautiously at the grocery store, even though California has (so far) reported no cyclosporiasis
cases. “I was at a Ralphs yesterday and reaching for my usual prepackaged lettuce for my salads
when I said, ‘Nah, let’s wait.’ Got stuff to make a Greek salad instead, since those are
easily washed and peeled,” Berger wrote to me. “I talked to a guy in the produce department who
said customers are starting to ask him about it, and lettuce products in particular are staying on
shelves longer than usual.”

For those still getting their workday salads from their usual spots, _hold the lettuce_ appears to
be the ethos. In southeastern Michigan, an area particularly rampant with _Cyclospora_ reports, a
friend who unfortunately caught the bug told me that his co-workers are avoiding boxed salads at the
supermarket and steering clear of regional staples like Beyond Juicery. Still, they’re putting in
their usual Qdoba bowl orders—these days, sans the lettuce and even the cilantro. Katie Krzaczek,
a journalist and former Slatester based in Philadelphia, did the same on Thursday with her Chipotle
order, following a hard think over whether she wanted to stick with her beloved chicken Caesar wraps
from a local sandwich shop.

That points to another conundrum for the health-conscious white-collar urban workforce: Greens are
_everywhere_ and in _everything_. “Everything is salad!!” Vox Media’s Danielle Hewitt wrote to
me. “I thought I got something with no salad greens in it yesterday. FILLED with kale. Even the
sandwiches are salad!!”

So, for now, Hewitt’s solution will be to bring in her own home-cooked lunches, emphasis on
_cooked_. She’s not alone there. Others I spoke with will be getting their nutrition from cooked
veggies and bringing those in, as opposed to ordering from a slop or salad bowl service. After all,
high temperatures do kill the parasite.

“I’ve cooked arugula, spinach, and kale recently that was supposed to be served raw when it was
packaged, by either a local ‘grain bowl’ place or CookUnity meal delivery service,” Erin
Hoffman, a data scientist based in Harlem, wrote to me. “This started about a week ago, when I saw
New York was one of the states with the most infections.” She sautés her veggies to make
“basically a stir-fry,” adding, “It’s less refreshing in this hot weather than a regular raw
salad, but the tradeoff feels worth it.”

Nearly everyone else I spoke to said the same thing: Even when blessed with the lush output of the
summer harvest, it seems far safer to sizzle everything instead of eating from the back or fresh
from the farmers market.

For now, it seems reasonable to expect that while cyclosporiasis reports stay elevated across the
country, everyday lunch habits will be overhauled in the short term, hitting the very health-food
spots the affluent classes normally flock to. This, alas, includes me: After freaking out a little
about my salad order from earlier this week, I switched to steaming-hot vegetarian noodles from a
neighborhood Chinese shop. Not your typical work lunch, to be sure, but in these atypical times, it
ain’t easy staying green.
