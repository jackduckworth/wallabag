The Etiquette Tip That Helps Me Keep My Cool—and Get What I Want—in Tense Email Exchanges

====================================================================================================

_This is_ _One Thing__, a column with tips on how to live._

It started when I was writing an angry email to my insurance company. It seemed like they were
going to deny coverage for some medical tests that I was sure they were supposed to pay for. When I
complained to my therapist about it, I explained that I was going to send them a response along the
lines of: _Listen you corrupt clowns, I already sent you the forms you requested five times so this
is obviously just a typical ruse to get out of paying._

He reminded me that I can be, well, a little bit of an impatient hothead. His advice was to draft a
response, and then wait eight hours before sending it. Waiting would allow me to process the actual
information in the insurance company’s message, and to calm down. Most importantly, being in a
less reactive state would give me a better shot at attaining my actual goal—getting medical
coverage—instead of simply insulting a customer service person. Since my longtime therapist’s
behavioral approach had already helped rein in my personality enough to quit smoking and drinking,
and marry a great guy, I decided to try his suggestion. The email I ended up sending began: “Thank
you so much for your help with this complicated matter.” Insurance ended up reimbursing me
completely.

It’s useful in situations that elicit any kind of strong emotions, not just anger. More recently,
an ex-boyfriend I hadn’t seen in years emailed me a request to loan him $4,000. He’d been sick,
so I feared he might need the money for something serious. Could he not afford medical care? We’d
dated after college; I’d always felt guilty I’d ended the relationship. I wondered if I could
afford to lose the $4,000. What if he died before repaying?

Recalling my therapist’s mandate, I didn’t answer right away. Just because someone contacted me
didn’t obligate me to reply instantly. Hours later my ex shared more info unprompted, revealing he
needed money because a Russian woman he was involved with couldn’t afford the tuition for her son
(who lived in Moscow!). What a scam. I told him so, refusing to comply and letting him know that he
shouldn’t be giving away money he needed to borrow.

I was glad I kept my rule. Now I try to slow down all complicated or heated electronic
correspondence for at least a day, sometimes a weekend. If you’re like me, eight hours can feel
like a long time, but it isn’t, and waiting can be like giving a great present to yourself.
