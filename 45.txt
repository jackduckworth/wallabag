Rise of the Rockstar Chef review – Gordon Ramsay and Marco Pierre White serve up the most sizzling TV of the year

====================================================================================================

Never mind the scallops, here's the Chef Pistols. Rise of the Rockstar Chef portrays the fine-dining
dons of the 90s and 00s in the manner of a band biopic. In place of musical differences is a stolen
reservations book and an argument about red mullet that reduced Gordon Ramsay to tears. How's that
for a kitchen nightmare?

As Ramsay, Marco Pierre White and Heston Blumenthal look back at their storied careers, it's a
culinary willy-waving contest. "We were bigger and better than the Beatles," brags White. "And we
fed more people than they did." It's hard to argue with the latter claim, unless you count Linda
McCartney's veggie sausages.

The mouthwatering question looming over proceedings is whether Ramsay and his estranged father
figure, White, can be in the same room together, decades after their bitter bust-up, without taking
copper-bottomed saucepans to each other's heads. Their infamous feud, picked over in chewy detail,
didn't just involve allegations of diary pilfering and piscine punch-ups; there were further ones of
fake table bookings, gatecrashed weddings and restaurant ejections. There is, in a very real sense,
beef. It's been braised in a rivalry reduction and slow-simmered in a testosterone jus.

[A man with curly hair and glasses wearing a suit looks at the camera.]
The docuseries chronicles how the gifted-but-dysfunctional duo drove the British restaurant scene
from global laughing stock to Michelin-starred powerhouse. It's a tasty tale of pan-throwing,
precision plating and the obsessive pursuit of perfection. If you enjoy watching angry men grating
truffles and tweezing micro-herbs, Christmas has come early.

The third wheel, their former protege Blumenthal, often feels like a gooseberry. The Fat Duck
trailblazer – inspired by "a Hungarian nuclear physicist playing around with egg whites" – is
treated as a palate cleanser between proper courses. This is a Studio Ramsay production and neither
the boss nor his Musketeer-haired mentor are willing to share the limelight.

Jamie Oliver was also considered to take part. Word is that White refused to sign up unless the
bish-bash-boshing Essex geezer was scraped into the kitchen bin. After all, he's never won a
Michelin star.

White, quite rightly, is the headline act. We see how he shot to fame in the 80s, winning his first
Michelin star at 26. Soubriquets soon pile up: the great White, Byron of the backburner. Mick Jagger
of the Aga, Jesus in a chef's jacket. With sniggering relish, Ramsay recalls him having sex with a
starstruck diner in the toilet upstairs, then climbing back through the kitchen skylight to continue
cooking her lunch. Hope hygiene inspectors weren't in the house.

When Ramsay goes solo, the young Padawan turns on his Jedi master and their power struggle begins.
White is a soul-searching romantic who views cookery as "gastronomic therapy for misfits" and talks
misty-eyed of cultural revolution. "Revolution?" scoffs Ramsay, who wouldn't know poetry if it
blow-torched his balls. "It was a shit-fight." The air goes out of the narrative when these rutting
stags aren't on screen.

Their frequently conflicting accounts are punctuated by talking heads who are no slouch with a
spatula themselves – the likes of Tom Kerridge, Michel Roux Jr and Raymond Blanc. Stanley Tucci
adds a sprinkle of stardust. We could probably live without Jodie Kidd and Max Beesley. Maybe they
wandered in by mistake from a Channel 5 clip show in the adjacent studio.

Social class is a running theme. All three hail from humble backgrounds – "council-house rebels
with a fucking cause", as Ramsay puts it in typically potty-mouthed style. They broke the rules and
rattled the establishment, before ultimately becoming the establishment. En route they created
eye-wateringly toxic work environments – all shouting, aggressive knife-sharpening and comparing
of burn scars.

[A man in a black T-shirt sits and clasps his hands]
"I guarantee a helluva lot of dick-swinging," says Marcus Wareing at the prospect of a peace summit.
Indeed, the entire series is an artisanal sausage fest. The alpha female is revered restaurant
critic Fay Maschler, whose reviews could fill or empty a restaurant. She purrs about White
"communing with a piece of wild salmon" and how "his intensity could glaze a creme brulee at 10
yards" but notes: "I admired his passion but he was actually quite dull, endlessly telling you about
ingredients." Food bores clearly haven't changed. When Maschler gave his restaurant Harveys four
stars out of five, legend has it that she knocked one star off because Marco refused to sleep with
her. (White says: "I can honestly put my hand on my heart and say that's not true." Maschler says:
"No, I didn't want to go to bed with him.") .

The heavily trailed reunion happens in the finale. It's both as fascinating and infuriating as you
might expect. White truly is the master(chef) of mind games. Prickly and unrepentant, he calls
Ramsay "boy" to make him feel like an apprentice again. For all his conciliatory gestures, Ramsay
still has a huge triple-cooked chip on his shoulder. At least White and Blumenthal are touchingly
affectionate together.

What's ultimately dished up is a flavoursome study of three gamechanging gastro geniuses. A sort of
foodie Mount Rushmore, with their craggy heads carved from fondant potato rather than granite. It's
both an exercise in Cool Britannia nostalgia and an elegy for a lost era. Does it make for delicious
entertainment? Yes, chef! Service please.
