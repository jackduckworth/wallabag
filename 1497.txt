That Crisp, Clean Beer You Love Has Long Depended on a Strange, Very Old Ingredient. Brewers Are Dropping It in the Name of Progress.

====================================================================================================

As I listened to the calm cadence of Morgan Freeman’s voice narrating yet another cataclysmic
extinction event in Netflix’s recent miniseries _The Dinosaurs_, my mind was necessarily on the
Jurassic period. But rather than focusing on the famous land-before-time megafauna and their demise
at the hands of the 2-million-year rainstorm of the Carnian pluvial episode, I was admiring the
impact of the prehistoric microbes that were their contemporaries. The Steven Spielberg–produced
simulation had inconsiderately left them off-screen, but the light Pilsner I nursed had likely been
poured over their fossilized remains.

Diatoms—a variety of tiny, single-celled algae that first emerged during the age of the
dinosaurs—are a key component of the modern brewing industry, and a not-insignificant part of why
your favorite beer looks and tastes the way it does. But that may be about to change. Clouds are
forming in beer across the world, and brewers are increasingly abandoning the ancient algae that can
clear them up.

Perhaps you know how beer is made, or you’ve heard about German beer-purity laws,
_Reinheitsgebot_, which allow brewers to use only water, barley, hops, and yeast. It is less common
knowledge, though, that much of the beer we are drinking (even that pure German stuff) filters
through the fossilized remains of the prehistoric plankton that lived alongside our favorite dinos.
Which is why even the casual beer drinker should take note of a white powder called diatomaceous
earth. It has become one of the most common filtration substrates in industrial beer-making.

“It has a lot of characteristics that make it applicable in filtration,” explains Roland Pahl,
beer production expert at Pall Corporation. “It has a big inner surface—the particles are shaped
very bizarrely. The reason for that is that DE is basically fossilized algae. In ancient seas and
ancient water reserves, algae would grow, die off, form a sediment on the ground, and fossilize over
millions of years. It kept that shape intact but changed the structure to silicon dioxide, so it is
almost inert.”

Every year, breweries across the world collectively buy hundreds of thousands of tons of fossilized
diatoms from mining companies in order to filter their beers. The global market for DE was valued at
$1.45 billion in 2025 (though many other sectors, including the dairy, edible oil, and petroleum
industries, use it for filtration). The U.S. is the leading global producer of the substance, and
about 60 percent of all DE ends up being used for filtration. But when you ask around in the
industry today, it sounds as if Morgan Freeman is narrating another hapless species on the brink of
extinction. The beer market is slowly turning its back on its fossilized fodder for good.

That doesn’t mean, however, that modern consumers are about to give up their filtered suds. Up to
92 percent of all beer consumed across the globe today is lager, that crisp, light, clear style we
recognize in Corona, Heineken, Budweiser, and Coors, among others. Filtration is key to making every
bottle, every pint, and every keg so clean and crushable.

“There’s that old saying ‘People drink with their eyes,’ ” says Chuck Skypeck, technical
brewing projects director at the Brewers Association. “The image of the crystal-clear beer with a
nice head of foam on top is so inviting. A bacterial infection in a beverage is going to produce
cloudiness, so there is probably something ingrained in our consciousness to view cloudiness with a
doubtful eye.”

Drinkers should not take beer clarity for granted. A clear, crystalline Corona bottle would hardly
be attractive if it were filled with a gruel-like mash of malt and water or the resulting unfiltered
wort, each of which precedes beer in the brewing process. Fermented drinks like beer and wine are
intentionally clarified once alcohol has been produced by yeast. There are both technical and
psychological motivations for that clarity.

Pahl explains: “As soon as people know that a product can be clear, when they see it’s hazy,
they suspect spoilage. It is easy for people to recognize turbidity.” Brewers who want to send
consistent, stable beers out into the market are bound to rely on some filtration technique prior to
bottling their product. It helps keep the beer tasting fresh. And they have tried their hand at many
strange filters over the years.

“In the late 17th and into the 18th century, brewers were trying numerous techniques and
substances to clarify their beers, even dark and heavier beers like the porter,” Malcolm Purinton,
a food historian and author of _Globalization in a Glass: The Rise of Pilsner Beer Through
Technology, Taste and Empire_, wrote me via email. “Among the options that were attempted were the
use of flannel strainers attached to the exit pipes from fermenting vessels and a, thankfully brief,
attempt at using asbestos as a filter bed.”

Shortly thereafter, a substance known as isinglass would become the norm for English brewers.
Appearing in the swim bladder of fish, especially sturgeon, isinglass makes yeast precipitate out of
the beer, clarifying it. Major breweries were still filtering their beer with these gelatinous fish
organs until relatively recently—Guinness finally altered its process in 2015 to make its dry
stout vegan.

“By the end of the 18th century and early 19th century we see the additional use of _kieselguhr_,
or diatomaceous earth as a filter bed,” Purinton continues. “Perlite also came to be used as
well—siliceous glass made up of mostly aluminum silicate and volcanic in origin.”

For industrial brewers, though, DE has been the only viable option to filter hundreds of millions of
liters of beer annually. “For the last four to five decades,” Pahl says, “DE filtration has
really dominated the market. The major reason for filtration is stability. If you would leave the
natural yeast in the product, the yeast would die off. And yeast does a nasty thing called autolysis
that creates a lot of unpleasant flavor loads and other impacts on the quality—it changes the
color of the product, it destroys foam, it enhances oxidation reaction.”

Under a microscope, DE looks like a chalky, primordial Chex Mix. Brewers rely on the nooks and
crannies and pores that once existed on prehistoric single-cell organisms to capture their own
single-cell yeast alongside other yucky brewing sediment. The problem is that this kind of
“dead-end” filtration clogs up DE’s pores. (And no one, not even an extinct microbe, wants
clogged pores.) The stuff cannot be used again, meaning that it causes a lot of waste. The fossil
filter also presents some major issues for breweries, including increased water usage, a relatively
high CO₂ footprint, and generation of hundreds of tons of waste. In an age when breweries are
under heightened scrutiny from educated, discerning consumers, sustainability is top of mind.
Accordingly, consumers can expect to start drinking beers that rely on other, more renewable
filtration techniques.

Pahl, whose company has moved away from producing and selling DE equipment to breweries, notes its
drawbacks. “You cannot practically reuse it after it has been used in filtration,” he says.
“Big breweries produce millions of hectoliters of beer a year—so hundreds of millions of liters.
That makes the use of DE in the hundreds of tons. And once it has been used, it has been soaking up
the product. It makes it wet, and it’s holding all the organics that you want to filter it out, so
it multiplies its mass by a factor of three. A hundred tons going in makes 300 tons of waste going
out.”

Where brewers did not previously have other options, technology is catching up to what is literally
the dinosaur technique of the beer industry. Cartridge filters, smaller-scale centrifuges, and
cross-flow membranes (which sweep debris away during filtration rather than clogging a filter with
particles) are all increasingly common options for craft brewers. “I kind of look at DE as the
grandfather filter medium that is starting to phase out,” explains Skypeck, of the Brewers
Association. “It’s fairly difficult to work with, and it’s time-consuming. Working with that
powder, it’s messy and it’s not easy to dispose of. You don’t want to put it down the
drain.” Meanwhile, Pahl tells me, “cross-flow membrane filtration is, at this point in time,
unanimously seen as the only alternative to DE filtration for large-scale breweries.” The wine
industry has already largely moved away from diatoms, and beer is quickly following suit.

One final factor is hastening the fossil’s fall from favor—the rise of hazy beer. Across the
craft-brewing industry, brewers are forgoing filtration altogether. New England IPAs, German
Hefeweizens, and Kellerbiers are all unfiltered and hazy by design. These are beers still cloudy
with the residual particles of the brewing process, and they generally deliver a full mouthfeel and
a soft, equally cloudy texture on the tongue.

This haze is not so rampant as to be a Pliensbachian-Toarcian Extinction Event–level threat to the
already-dead diatoms, but there’s no question that brewers are turning away from the Jurassic
filtration substrate for lots of good reasons. It’s heavy, messy, water-intensive, time-consuming,
and single-use. Well, it’s single-use as far as brewers are concerned. We cannot be sure what
those same silica diatom skeletons will be doing in another 100 million years. We thank them,
though, for their service to crisp, clear beer everywhere.
