Tech Billionaires Really Do Think We’re All Idiots, Don’t They?

====================================================================================================

One week after the blue wave swept at the midterm elections, Palantir CEO Alex Karp announced that
he was actually super liberal. Possibly the best liberal that you have ever seen. Certainly a better
liberal than you.

“I’ve been a Democrat my whole life,” he said in an interview with Politico. “I grew up in a
far-left environment. I know more about being a progressive than almost anybody on the planet, and I
absolutely believe Palantir—all of our products are essentially progressive.”

He isn’t the only billionaire who’s seemed to change his mind. Elon Musk, who is most famous for
aligning himself with far-right politicians and also Looking Like That, and who spent $277 million
on Donald Trump’s campaign and $474 million to try to stop Democratic Sen. James Talarico from
getting into office, logged onto a video call at a New York Times summit this week and said the
following words: “I’m not MAGA.” Yes, the guy who maybe Nazi-saluted, the guy who called
himself “dark MAGA” last year, the guy who hit the _High School Musical_ jump at a Trump rally
is not—not!—a Republican. You’re the one with that silly idea!

“I’m not sure what MAGA means,” Musk said. “If that means sort of blind adherence to a set
of policies by the Republican Party, then I certainly don’t have that.”

Meta has also tried to go purple, deepening ties with Democratic Washington veterans during the week
of the election after CEO Mark Zuckerberg spent so long cozying up to the Trump administration.
(Here’s some photos of them laughing and smiling and leaning their foreheads into each other.)
Microsoft and Apple have also hired Democratic lobbyists to brace for the inevitable probing and
questioning that they face after so flagrantly and openly playing in voters’ faces for the past
four years as they so belligerently played a role in the politics that impacted everyone’s lives.
Big Tech is hitting Uno reverse on the past two years—but I have some questions about what,
besides their proximity to the people signing the checks, these billionaires actually intend to
change.

Take Karp, whose progressive credentials appear to include everything other than what he does to
make his money. One could make an argument that Karp could be considered liberal, in some twisted
sense of the word. There’s evidence to support this. In 2015, he was caught on tape criticizing
Trump in a Palantir staff meeting, saying, “I respect nothing about the dude” and that “it
would be hard to make up someone that I find less appealing.” He publicly backed Kamala Harris’
campaign in 2024. There’s a picture of him wearing a keffiyeh in his college yearbook that
sometimes gets circulated like an urban myth (Palantir posted in support of Israel in 2024). Sure,
some could make the argument that Karp is liberal, perhaps, in the same way that putting a _Coexist_
sticker on the back of a Cadillac Escalade is liberal, or wearing Temu pussy hats in this day and
age could be considered liberal, or putting “moderate” in your Hinge profile can be convincing
as potentially liberal if you turn off all the lights and squint. Throw a rock in any major American
city and you will hit someone working for an A.I. defense startup who wants to claim the same
political affiliation, simply because they’ve read Marx and Engels before.

It’s easy to make fun of these statements because any claims of leftism are quickly eclipsed by
the human impact that these corporations have had on people living in the country. Zuckerberg also
started off criticizing Trump’s first term, and the two have feuded as Trump threatened to ban
Facebook, but years later, they’re getting dinner together, and Zuckerberg’s shaping A.I. policy
in this country.

Palantir’s public positioning is not unlike Karp’s politics. The company is officially known for
its management, data analysis, and visualization software. Its workers have called themselves
digital “plumbers,” joining leaky or disconnected pipes of information together with A.I. tools
so scattered datasets can be analyzed with ease. But much of its internal work has remained largely
shielded from the public, which the company claims is for client confidentiality.

Here’s what we know: A bunch of smaller companies have user data—shopping habits, demographics,
the random subscriptions that are pooling in inboxes, everyone’s geolocations used for picking up
food or reselling furniture or whatever. Palantir aggregates all of this, and then helps its clients
access these different databases, synthesizing all this scattered information into one clean channel
of analysis for whatever purpose. They can help a marketing company figure out at what price point
you’re most likely to buy their products, based on your abandoned shopping carts across all your
favorite stores. They can also help ICE figure out when you’re most likely to be at home or at
work or at church, so they know how to pick where to raid. (Palantir denies that they are making
predictive policing tools, although arrests have been made in cities across America with
Palantir-partnered predictive policing tools.)

This is the cultural switch-up that Palantir constantly plays: They have established a reputation as
being mysterious and all-powerful, because they have all our data, but then people involved with the
company have spent an astounding amount of airtime trying to shirk accountability around the impact
of their work, or worse, not being able to articulate what it is they do at all. They know
everything, and also nothing. Alex Karp and co-founder Peter Thiel have denied that Palantir ushered
in an era of predictive policing, overreaching government surveillance, and mining sensitive user
data, although the bulk of their public-facing partnerships are with the military and police forces,
as well as private defense corporations like Anduril and Lockheed Martin, so all the arrows kind of
point in one direction. Karp claims he is actually progressive, so how could someone who grew up in
a “far-left” environment with granola and stuff possibly be manufacturing a back-end national
surveillance dataset and selling it to the military?

To be clear, what Karp actually politically identifies as is a sycophant. His financial donations
have been scattered between Republicans and Democrats alike, and he’s spent most of his time in
politics aggressively glazing whatever party in power holds the coin purse that re-signs the
Department of Defense contracts with Palantir. It’s a level of dick-riding that many might not
have believed possible: In December 2024, just one month after Kamala Harris lost the election that
Karp himself endorsed her in, he got on stage and started dunking on Democrats for their “woke
pagan ideology”; the next year, he said that any claims that Trump is a fascist are “stupid”
and “idiotic” in a panel with the New York Times. Earlier this year, he also published an
“anti-woke” manifesto in a 22-part Twitter thread that sparked huge amounts of discourse. No one
throws it back like Karp does.

Getting involved in politics is not part of his job description, but it has worked out. Karp has won
the company hundreds of millions of dollars under the Trump administration. Palantir has played a
pivotal role in the president’s mass deportation campaign and widespread policing across this
country. And for all these jovial lamentations that Karp thinks Trump went “too far” in the
violent detainment of immigrants and citizens alike, Palantir still has a $377 billion market cap.

I do not think all hope is lost, though. I think there is a bulletproof solution to Karp’s desire
to pivot left. This is a solution for any tech billionaire looking to convince the public that
they’re actually, seriously—no, seriously—progressive: Give me all the money.

All the money you have.

Give it away, and give it to me.

Give it to schools to buy free school lunches and hospitals to pay off medical debt and libraries
for lots of smutty romance books.

Give it to Cardi B, who can set up funds for people to get food assistance and all-expenses-paid
girls’ trips to Miami or something for overworked women and also women who are going through
breakups.

And mostly, give it to me. I have a bunch of stuff I want to buy, including paying down my
friends’ student loans and medical debt and then also getting some matching shoes.

My demands do not end there. Once you give me the money, dissolve Palantir and dye your hair blue
for good measure, and start experimenting with your pronouns. If you’re really about the
superprogressive leftist life, start arguing with your co-organizers in a shared Google Doc about
logistics for the upcoming local government sit-in. I think that would be very progressive. I think
if you all did that, I would say, “Wow, Alex Karp knows more about being progressive than anyone
on the planet.” (No, I still probably wouldn’t say that, because what’s past is prologue. But
I’d be far more likely to say that.)

Until then, no dice! Alex Karp and associates want to glaze Democratic senators for taxpayer money
without giving anything first. That’s my money and your money that they’re currently prancing
around on podcasts for. The smallest sin that Karp has to pay for is the fact that Palantir is
possibly the most criminally incorrect LARP of _Lord of the Rings_ in history (the company, named
after the series’ omnipresent “seeing stone,” has been dragged online for the fact that the
entire moral of J.R.R. Tolkien’s Palantír was that it was an unreliable guide, calling into
question if these tech bros have critical thinking abilities), and those restitutions are already
starting at a pretty steep price. As more tech billionaires have to figure out how to align
themselves with a Democratic-majority Congress, I expect direct deposits in the process. There’s
no other way out. If they really want to be the best liberals we’ve ever seen.
