Squid Game Is Full of Deaths. But One Is Truly Devastating.

====================================================================================================

_THIS ARTICLE CONTAINS SPOILERS FOR_ SQUID GAME _SEASON 3._

_Squid Game_, Netflix’s bloody Korean series that became a global phenomenon when it first
premiered in 2021, has never pulled its punches. The show—a gruesome and disturbing exploration of
how capitalism undermines humanity and morality—found success using intense violence and clever
storytelling, turning the concepts of childhood games into fatal events as part of a dystopian
competition for survival and money. But _Squid Game_’s true strength lies in its ability to create
unlikely heroes (and captivating villains) out of the most unassuming of characters, like Ali in
Season 1, the nefarious Recruiter in Season 2, and, now, Hyun-ju, a transgender female character
played by the actor Park Sung-hoon. Despite _Squid Game_ centering around the resilient, yet
somewhat idiotic, main character of Seong Gi-hun (Lee Jung-jae), it’s Hyun-ju who has become the
most captivating character in this final season, which premiered on Friday. Unfortunately, these
unlikely _Squid Game_ heroes and villains that define the show are tragic ones, and, as is typical
for this series, Hyun-ju’s story ends too soon, resulting in the most devastating death this
season.

Cho Hyun-ju first arrives on _Squid Game_ in Season 2—Seasons 2 and 3 tell one continuous story;
you can think of them as two parts of the same season—as Player 120, a shy transgender woman who
initially receives questionable looks from other players. But in the very first game of the deadly
survival competition—the infamous Red Light, Green Light game—Hyun-ju proves herself to be of
incredible character, risking her life to help Gi-hun carry an injured player over the line to
safety. She does the same (though not necessarily by choice) when she agrees to team up with the
anxiety-ridden Young-mi (Kim Si-eun) in the subsequent Six-Legged Pentathlon game. It’s thanks to
Hyun-ju’s coaching and leadership that her team—which includes a group of misfits, many of whom
go on to become Hyun-ju’s trusted allies as the games continue—survive the Six-Legged
Pentathlon.

We find out that Hyun-ju entered the life-or-death competition because her decision to transition
got her fired from her job and ostracized by her friends and family. The lack of income and absence
of a support system in the middle of her transition caused Hyun-ju to fall into debt, so she joined
the games so she could pay off her debts and finish getting the surgeries needed to complete her
transition. Hyun-ju quickly comes to represent a hero for the underdogs, someone who always stands
up for and protects the weak. But she doesn’t simply protect those players: She earnestly believes
in their abilities to overcome challenges or bring something useful to the table. Hyun-ju doesn’t
see the world, or the games, in black-and-white terms of weakest links. Instead, because of her
experiences,she believes in the value of life, perhaps more than any other player does.

Kindness and compassion aside, the other part of Hyun-ju’s appeal is her physical and intellectual
dominance. Not only does she formulate and execute good game plans to survive the trials, but she
also proves herself to be the player most adept at combat. When Gi-hun attempts to lead a revolt
against the workers overseeing the games at the end of Season 2, Hyun-ju is the only woman who
volunteers to join the fight. When the other rebels are shocked at her strong capabilities and
battle and weapon knowledge, she reveals that before she transitioned, she was a highly trained and
incredibly skilled sergeant in the ROK Special Forces. This, of course, takes the men who had judged
or underestimated her by surprise. Even the fact that she had carried herself and her teammates to
safety so far seemed to defy the prejudiced expectations of her fellow players.

Where Season 1 of _Squid Game_ had a whole cast of equally interesting characters, making the
show’s stakes skyrocket as you realize they can’t all survive, Seasons 2 and 3 have struggled to
achieve that same level of depth among the mostly new slate of characters, whether because of
lackluster acting or poor writing. There were some standouts: a mother-son duo, and Thanos, a
struggling rapper with a drug addiction and an unfortunate brush with a failed cryptocurrency,
played by K-pop rapper T.O.P. But none hold a candle to the emotional stakes that Hyun-ju embodies,
easily making her a fan-favorite character in Season 2. Of course, Park Sung-hoon’s phenomenal
acting, approaching Hyun-ju with such depth and vulnerability, has much to do with this as well.
Though this casting decision originally generated some controversy from viewers who were
disappointed that Hwang Dong-hyuk, the show’s creator, didn’t cast a transgender woman for the
role, Hwang addressed these complaints in an interview with TV Guide, saying that “it was near
impossible” to cast a trans actor, as he found that “there are close to no actors that are
openly trans, let alone openly gay” in South Korea, given the marginalization that members of the
LGBTQ+ community still face in the country.

But, as any _Squid Game_ viewer knows, the good can’t survive, because no one can (unless you’re
Gi-hun, the show’s foolhardy main character). In Hyun-ju’s case, she dies the way we would come
to expect: while trying to save other people. Only two episodes into the final season, during a
fatal game of Hide-and-Seek—in which the knife-armed seekers must murder at least one hider before
the time is up, and hiders must avoid death by remaining hidden or by finding the exit
door—Hyun-ju is randomly assigned a hider role. She vows to stick with the pregnant contestant
Jun-hee (Jo Yu-ri) and Geum-ja (Kang Ae-shim), the mother of the mother-son duo—two vulnerable
players who would otherwise be unlikely to survive the game. When Jun-hee fractures her ankle and
then goes into labor while hiding, Hyun-ju stays on the lookout for seekers looking to murder them.
She gets into a struggle with one, and their fight pushes them down the hallway into another room.
After Hyun-ju manages to kill the man, she looks up and realizes she’s inadvertently found the
exit. Instead of stepping through the door and winning the game with her life intact, she selflessly
runs back to the room to tell the ladies—where she gets stabbed in the back, ultimately dying.

There are layers to Hyun-ju’s death. The man who killed her is Myung-gi (Im Si-wan), Jun-hee’s
former boyfriend and father to the newborn. He has spent the game murdering a number of hiders,
after having switched positions with Jun-hee so that he could ostensibly protect her after doing his
requisite murder. But instead, he has ruthlessly killed scores of people to grow the ultimate prize
pot and increase his chances of surviving the competition, illustrating how power is a slippery
slope and violence can be brutally indiscriminate. And, irony of all ironies, he kills the one
person who has protected the mother of his child—and the main reason that his child was even able
to make it into this world. Hyun-ju was his opposite, someone whose selflessness cost her her life
in a world built for the hard-hearted to survive.

There’s also the layer of what Hyun-ju’s death represents because of her presence _in_ the
games. Hwang intended for the character to open viewers’ eyes to the marginalization of LGBT
characters, and Hyun-ju’s presence in the story does just that. _Squid Game_ was first known for
highlighting how devastating it is that people without money are so desperate to survive that they
feel the need to risk their lives for basic human rights or a higher standard of living, whether
their misfortunes are a direct result of their actions or not. Hyun-ju’s presence in the games
showcases the grim reality of a trans person without the means to procure gender-affirming care, or
even solidarity and understanding from the people in her life. Hyun-ju doesn’t just battle for
money in the arena—she also battles against discrimination every time she proves the judgmental
men around her wrong, just as she battles for visibility and understanding when her allied players
in the game validate her existence and her identity. Like everyone else, she’s battling for her
life—but then again, she was already doing that outside the games.
