The World Cup’s Ticketing Nightmare Is Finally Here

====================================================================================================

Last Christmas, Jeremy Wright gave his soccer-loving wife a monumental present: tickets to the
Netherlands–Japan World Cup matchup on June 14 at AT&T Stadium in Arlington, Texas. Purchased in
September via StubHub, with taxes and platform fees included, the two passes nearly amounted to
$1,400—all before factoring in logistical expenses like gas for the drive from Austin, along with
lodgings at a nearby hotel. Still, when game day arrived, Wright and his wife were all set to go,
except for one major wrinkle: They never got the tickets.

Five hours before kickoff, after spending the morning celebrating with fellow fans before attending
the team parade, Wright received an email from StubHub informing him that his tickets couldn’t be
transferred. When he opened the app, he was prompted to select “alternative tickets,” only to
find that none were available. He then requested a refund but encountered a confusing series of
prompts that required him to enter his credit card information again. When he called customer
service, he said, a representative repeatedly told him that because he had selected the refund
option, they could no longer assist him. “I kept asking to talk to a manager,” Wright told me.
“To which they said, ‘We can put it in a queue and someone will call you back in 24
hours.’ ”

By the time Wright and I spoke last week, he still hadn’t gotten a callback. As for how he and his
wife ultimately spent game day, “we were so disappointed that we drove four hours home in the rain
and didn’t watch the match.” (A spokesperson for StubHub informed me, and Wright confirmed, that
company reps offered him free hospitality-suite tickets to a semifinals game in Dallas–Fort Worth
on July 14, after my request for comment from the company.)

Throughout this euphoric, dramatic World Cup series, which has brought domestic and international
tourists to North American cities from Vancouver all the way down to Monterrey, there has been one
unfortunate motif: fans unable to access the pricey tickets that they purchased in advance, whether
through StubHub or SeatGeek or FIFA’s official ticketing platform. Many of the aggrieved fans I
spoke with had chosen StubHub in particular, as it commands a larger share of the
customer-to-customer resale market and represents a _chance_, at least, to get something cheaper
than what FIFA would allow. Or so it seemed, until they finally tried it for themselves and saw
their purchases disappear altogether.

“I get claustrophobic in large gatherings, so I chose StubHub over SeatGeek because they let you
pick seats,” Thom Fountain, a Twin Cities–based sporting enthusiast, told me of his experience
buying tickets for Thursday’s Netherlands–Tunisia match in Kansas City. “But they sent me a
completely different seat in the middle of the section, and when I reached out they told me my only
recourse was to resell the tickets or keep them.” The replacement tickets ended up cutting
Fountain off from his refund opportunity, as per the terms of StubHub’s “FanProtect
Guarantee,” which leaves decisions on resolutions to the platform’s “sole discretion.”

There are countless other soccer spectators with stories similar to Wright’s and Fountain’s—of
getting secondhand tickets not worth the price initially paid, or of not receiving tickets at all
thanks to seller cancellations or transfer glitches, while seeing no relief from customer-service
inquiries. (In fact, many unhappy fans are now talking to neighborhood reporters instead of sales
reps.) The volume of complaints across Facebook, LinkedIn, and local news desks has swelled rapidly
over the past month. While there is no hard data available yet on just how many tickets have been
“sold” on one resale platform over another—such as Vivid Seats, which has also fielded World
Cup ticketing issues—it makes sense that StubHub is getting the most public attention: It’s the
most used resale app in the world, and it has historically been the subject of far more Better
Business Bureau complaints than any of its competitors. (StubHub’s profile page on the bureau’s
website, which gives it an “F” rating, has been flooded with thousands of negative user comments
since June 2023—far exceeding SeatGeek and Vivid Seats.)

On Reddit, where Wright first shared his tale of woe, communities like r/StubHub and r/WorldCup2026
are saturated with antipathy toward ticket resellers; when you type _StubHub_ into the Reddit search
box, an A.I.-generated description sums up the subreddit’s chatter as “World Cup ticket failures
and calls to stop using StubHub.” Sure, some attendees may be splurging thousands on a single
ticket, but others are getting nothing at all, even after traveling across international borders for
games.

“When sellers don’t deliver tickets on time, StubHub investigates and takes swift action in line
with our terms & conditions, including permanent bans and financial penalties,” a spokesperson for
StubHub wrote in a statement to Slate. “The transfer failures fans have encountered stem from
performance issues with the event organizer’s own app infrastructure—a challenge that affects
all resale platforms operating at this tournament—and we are trying to engage with the event
organizer directly to address it.” According to the spokesperson, “the vast majority of World
Cup orders on StubHub have been fulfilled successfully.”

Other buyers would say, however, that such issues long predate the current soccer bonanza. StubHub,
which merged with the controversial British ticket exchange Viagogo in 2020, has been racked with
various scandals over the years: paying out a $10 million federal settlement over deceptive pricing
practices, allowing “speculative tickets” (listings from resellers who do _not_ actually hold
the relevant tickets) to run rampant, and retracting high-level sports and concert tickets from
consumers in order to gouge their prices and re-resell them. But the World Cup in particular seems
to be exposing the full extent of a problem StubHub has faced during other large-scale events, like
Zach Bryan and Taylor Swift tours: It’s too easy for the system to get overwhelmed with
potentially deceitful or inconsistent sellers, along with desperate deal-seekers who don’t really
know what they’re buying, and one company cannot handle all those complaints efficiently—even
after automating much of that support system.

“Nobody’s really solved how to get tickets to clients faster on the secondary side, and it seems
like StubHub hasn’t made too much progress, which is disappointing to see,” Taylor Hagstrom, an
Atlanta-based customer experience representative who worked with StubHub’s Global Fan Experience
team in 2024 and 2025, told me. On that team, Hagstrom took messages (calls, emails, chats) from
ticket buyers and collaborated with the company’s sellers division to resolve reports. “All
those escalations showed _clearly_ that ticket delivery is one of the biggest points of pain for
customers, so why not put as much energy behind solving that as possible?”

Hagstrom had to handle consumer escalations for “tough” events, like the Masters and the NFL
playoffs, so he had a clear view of where StubHub needed to improve: accelerating the
ticket-transfer process and leaving more time to handle potential disputes before an event begins.
There are measures the company has adopted over the years to bolster its platform against myriad
vulnerabilities: adopting cybersecurity programs to preempt automated bot accounts, and expanding
protections for music festival mishaps. But the list of issues is still long (lack of thorough user
verification, susceptibility to hacks, nontransparency around fees), and the fixes are slow, per
Hagstrom. “We would work with our various technology teams to try to improve the site. I know
they’re trying to make these timelines tighter,” he said, “but the whole place needs to put
consumer insights back in their focus.” (After publication, a StubHub spokesperson wrote to Slate
that “Primary ticketing companies and event organizers control when tickets are released, directly
impacting when they can be transferred. For high-profile events like the World Cup, some tickets are
only made available hours before a match starts, which makes transfer more difficult and creates
confusion for fans.”)

The World Cup goes until July 19, so unless something drastically changes, and soon, more consumer
insights will inevitably pile up for StubHub—in the form of endless and increasingly prominent
complaints from buyers who trusted the platform and its sellers with their money. After all, nearly
every source I spoke to for this story told me they’ll never use StubHub again.
