

====================================================================================================

A New Netflix Show Combines Two of TV’s Most Popular Showrunners and a Dream Cast. It Does Not Go Well.

====================================================================================================

Given that they’ve built a career on emulating Steven Spielberg’s direction, it makes sense that
Matt and Ross Duffer would mark the end of _Stranger Things_ by following him into the realm of
executive producing. They’ve already stuck their imprimatur on the Netflix series _Something Bad
Is Going to Happen_ and the animated spinoff _Stranger Things: Tales From ’85_, and now comes _The
Boroughs_, a slowly unwinding creeper set in a desert retirement community. Creators Jeffrey Addiss
and Will Matthews, who created the _Dark Crystal_ series _Age of Resistance_ and co-wrote the
animated movie _The Lord of the Rings: The War of the Rohirrim_, are aiming less for Spielberg than
for his derivatives, especially the movies produced under his Amblin banner in the 1980s: Think of
it as _Gremlins_ meets _The Goonies_, with a sizable helping of _Cocoon_.(That last one wasn’t
made by Amblin, but like _The Boroughs_, it plays as if it wishes it were.)

There’s also, inevitably, a lot of _Stranger Things_, if you aged up the characters about 60
years. Like Hawkins, Indiana, the Boroughs presents as an idyllic, tight-knit community, a place
where, as the slogan plastered on every flat surface reminds us, “you’ll have the time of your
life.” But underneath its facade is—would you believe it?—a dark and deadly secret that the
people who guard it will stop at nothing to keep hidden. And a ragtag group of friends, in this case
thrown together more by circumstance than by inclination, are the only ones who can uncover the
truth.

While the Duffers’ own show relied on finding fresh faces, _The Boroughs_’ cast is a dream team
of screen veterans: Alfred Molina as Sam, a retired scientist who moves in after the death of his
wife (Jane Kaczmarek, seen in flashbacks); Denis O’Hare as his quirky neighbor Wally; Alfre
Woodard and Clarke Peters as Judy and Art, a once idealistic couple whose open marriage has allowed
them to drift further and further apart; and Geena Davis as Renee, who hasn’t let age or
circumstances dull her appetite for life, or sex. It’s a particular joy to see Woodard, who of
late has been wasted in too many things, like the Z-grade remake of _Salem’s Lot_, get a chance to
stretch out a character over eight full episodes. But the series’ writing is rarely up to the
level of its actors, and Woodard in particular gets shortchanged by a character arc that runs all
the way from morose to glum.

I can’t give away the Boroughs’ secret here, but thanks to the obligatory scare that opens the
show, the one that’s meant to convince streaming viewers they shouldn’t immediately scroll
onward, I can say there’s a monster involved, a leathery creature that plucks old people from
their beds and suggests that “the time of your life” might not be that long after all. Sam’s
already having cognitive issues, moments when his wife’s death seems to be happening in the
present instead of the past, over and over again. But as one of the Boroughs’ employees chirpily
informs him, getting older isn’t trauma. Your life isn’t supposed to be a full or happy one at
this point: Just play some pickleball, maybe take an exercise class, and wait for the end.

The showmeans, I guess, to show us there’s still life in these old folks: They still smoke weed,
get laid, and remain a lot more aware of their surroundings than the unnervingly buoyant couple who
run the Boroughs, Blaine (Seth Numrich) and Anneliese (Alice Kremelberg), are counting on. But its
vision of life’s final chapter feels thin, as if the people making the show didn’t talk to any
older people in the process. (Call your parents, folks.) Among the writing staff, the closest to the
characters’ ages is James Schamus, who pops in for a single credit on the sixth episode but leaves
no trace of the Oscar-nominated screenwriter behind _Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon_. There’s no
reason you have to have been through the aging process to tell stories about it, but it requires a
curiosity and compassion the series rarely employs, interested more in its unfolding mystery and the
occasional joke about fried food.

That might be more forgivable if _The Boroughs_’ central mystery were more engaging or less
predictable—or even vaguely coherent. But it’s a mess, constantly shifting what it seems to be
about—a magic tree? An alien egg?—in ways that create confusion rather than a sense of intrigue.
There are ways to leave the door open for future seasons that aren’t just playing hide-the-ball,
but this feels less like calculated suggestion and more like simple vagueness; if anyone knows where
this is going, there’s no sign of it. The show does occasionally, if not for long, capture the
vibe of some of those Spielberg-produced ’80s classics (although a lot of those, to be frank,
struggle to retain that status when viewed through adult eyes). But that’s in no small part
because the show so often treats its elderly protagonists like children, motivated only by the parts
of their characters it’s chosen to show us thus far. It’s not interested in the lives they lived
before, and they’re not interesting enough for us to wonder.
