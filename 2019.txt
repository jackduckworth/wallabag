20 Years Ago, We Were Introduced to One of the Finest Performances on TV. It Feels Impossible Today.

====================================================================================================

_This article is part of_ _20 Years of_ 30 Rock_, Slate’s celebration of the iconic sitcom two
decades after its premiere._

In a profile of Alec Baldwin that ran in the New Yorker in 2008, two years after the premiere of _30
Rock_ on NBC, writer Ian Parker described an actor at a crossroads—convinced his movie career was
permanently stalled out, still angsty over his acrimonious 2002 divorce from Kim Basinger, and not
totally sure what he would do with the rest of his life. Onscreen, in the show’s first couple of
seasons, Baldwin’s network suit, Jack Donaghy, was establishing a working relationship with
_TGS_’s showrunner Liz Lemon (Tina Fey), delivering backhanded compliments like “I like you. You
have the boldness of a much younger woman,” and burns studded with snobby, memorable turns of
phrase. (After Jack’s Season 1 “gentleman from Fairfield” rant, pizza will forever be
“greasy peasant food” to me.) But offscreen, Baldwin was, Parker found, adrift, less like the
confident Jack of earlier seasons, who baldly lies to Lemon’s face about dyeing his hair, and more
akin to the “unsharkulated,” softer Jack Donaghy we would later see in the 2013 series finale,
who mourns spending “Christmas in the Hamptons drinking Scotch and throwing firecrackers at Billy
Joel’s dog.”

Baldwin, it seems, initially had to be convinced to commit completely to _30 Rock_, because the role
of Jack, a really good character in a really good sitcom, seemed too easy. Was it even acting for
Alec Baldwin, a man with a turbulent inner life, conventionally handsome looks, and a big slabby
head on a big slabby body, to play Jack, an executive passionately committed to advancing in
business and in New York society who is also secretly a freak with a hidden passion for ceramic
cookie jars, a hate–love relationship with his overbearing mother, and an uncanny knack for
mimicry? The role of Jack depended on Baldwin’s sense of comedic timing and presence in a way that
his big-screen movie work in _Beetlejuice_ or _The Hunt for Red October_ had not. Over the next few
years, Jack and Baldwin merged into the perfect symbiont, each one of them amplifying the other. The
result was one of the finest performances on television—one that, even though it won Baldwin a
handful of Emmys, Golden Globes, and other awards, still feels like it never got the recognition it
deserved.

The Jack role, as beloved as it remains among _30 Rock_ die-hards, could never be written in the
activated, partisan Trump years, because Jack is a corporate, Reagan-loving Republican who is
managing creatives, and sometimes reins them in and makes them insert GE microwaves into sketches,
and other times is kind of, well, Daddy—a plainspoken, mean type of Daddy, doling out reality
checks in Baldwin’s trademark husky, pointed delivery. In a 2013 Rolling Stone postmortem on the
show, co-showrunner Robert Carlock said of the character, “It’s a lot more fun to let the
conservative guy be right more. … It’s fun to let Liz be a little less well-informed, but maybe
a little more morally right, and Jack to be a lot more sophisticated.” Case in point: “Where do
you invest your money?” Jack asks Liz in the Season 2 episode “Rosemary’s Baby,” one of the
show’s pound-for-pound most quotable, which also gave us Jack’s advice to “never go with a
hippie to a second location.” “I have, like, 12 grand in checking,” Liz says, to which Jack
replies sardonically: “Are you an immigrant?”

Often, the things Jack said were offensive, in a way that would never work for a 2026 landscape of
fans habituated to demand solid red lines between heroes and villains, but felt, at the time, like
just another way to draw a contrast between the New York City of Liz Lemon—creative, hapless,
well-intentioned, Obama-era woke—and Jack’s New York City, a world full of boldface names where
the point of life is to meet famous people and make bank. In that world, Jack’s developed habits
of fat-shaming, age-shaming (“I’m 50. To put it in perspective that’s like 32 for ladies”),
and misogyny (“Don’t gloat. It makes you seem mannish”) are as insulting as they are
ruthlessly pragmatic.

Why doesn’t all of this make Jack, a character I’d dread being trapped with at a party if he
were real, too grating to watch? Because Baldwin knew how to make Jack just enough of a stock
character, inches to the left of reality, that his statements don’t really hurt Liz Lemon—or, by
extension, me. Baldwin told Rolling Stone when the show concluded that he was inspired by Gene
Hackman in _The Royal Tenenbaums_,“this kind of Hemingway manqué masculinity … a blend where
you’re alternately Teddy Roosevelt, and the next minute you’re Dustin Hoffman in _The Graduate_:
You’re a neurotic mess, psychosexually, and then you’re just charging up the hill with a sword
in your hand.” Jack is the kind of guy who spends New Year’s with his famous friend, “the
deep-sea explorer Bob Ballard” (classic Jack name-drop), who brings him “a 2,000-year-old
amphora of wine from a sunken Phoenician trading vessel,” but they end up tripping out in the
backyard, high on the toxic fumes, “having vivid hallucinations of Astarte, the Phoenician goddess
of sex and war.” Baldwin delivered those packed-full _30 Rock_ lines with a professional’s
aplomb, and Jack’s bluster always stays just on the interesting side of unbearable. Baldwin also
never feared appearing ridiculous, embracing fully those gags where he played dream versions of
Harriet Tubman or Thomas Jefferson, or pulled off stereotype-shaped impressions of Tracy Jordan’s
parents (and young Tracy, and their Latina neighbor, and Tracy’s mom’s white paramour) in one of
the show’s most impressive displays of Baldwin’s versatility.

No, “Jackie D.” (as Tracy called him) wasn’t a part that just anyone could play, nor was it a
part that would come around often. By the time the show was over, Baldwin had figured out that the
role of Jack was a gift. “I miss that show terribly. … I’ll never have it that good again,
professionally,” Baldwin wrote in “Good-Bye, Public Life,” a 2014 New York magazine essay in
which he declared his intention to do only his work and participate in none of the extra parts of
celebrity that involve “communicating with an audience playfully like we’re friends.” The
years after this essay brought the very public debacle of the fatal shooting on the set of his movie
_Rust_, and the different public debacle of his family’s unbeloved and short-lived reality show,
_The Baldwins_. When all is said and done, will Jack be the role Baldwin is remembered for? I’ve
got a rabbit hole of YouTube highlight reels that makes a pretty darn good case.
