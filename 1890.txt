

====================================================================================================

Arteta embraces perfect storm to play the victim in game of his own making | Barney Ronay

====================================================================================================

A gentleman is never rude unintentionally, only by design. Nobody really knows who said this first.
But it could easily be a line from the Elite Football Manager's Guide to Press Conferences (Premier
League edition). Because nothing happens by accident in these spaces.

Every word spoken in front of the board covered in adverts is metered and planned. A manager
is never angry, or concerned, or even charming unintentionally. The game _is_ the game, but so are
all the other bits spilling out either side of it.

It was hard not to think about this while listening to Mikel Arteta talk about the penalty awarded
to Sunderland at the Stadium of Light for a perceived act of grappling on Dan Ballard by Ezri Konsa.
This is said with genuine professional respect. As everyone who has ever done a podcast knows –
and this is, let's be clear, everyone – there are lost Amazonian tribes discussing Girls season
three in their own dark corner of Spotify – there are moments when you just have to flip the
switch, up the energy and turn on full clip‑this‑bit‑up‑please‑Dave sincerity mode.

Arteta did this beautifully in the moment, appearing genuinely struck by what he was saying,
radiating wounded integrity, like a disappointed junior headmaster appealing for the return of the
school minibus keys.

Let's be clear though. Arteta is not "very worried" about all this. He's not "sad" or indeed, as he
later emphasised for good measure, "really sad". He's not concerned solely with "protecting the
game", speaking here for all of us, just a fan of football.

Come off it. This is a manager we know to be obsessed with the most marginal of gains, to micro-plan
the micro-details. As is all elite sport now. There has been talk of rowing coaches installing
fibreglass toilet seats at their training hubs having calculated the average athlete spends 20
minutes in there and this is all key glute management time. Arteta loves this stuff. There's no way
Ben White isn't sat fiddling with his phone at the training ground on a gold-standard Easy‑Cup
Mega-Soft Evacu-Throne.

So it is with his public statements. This will have all been gamed and contingency‑planned in
advance. And for hard, rational, tactical reasons, because this is how the game is played and run
now. So what does it mean?

[Ezri Konsa holds Dan Ballard.]
There are three things we know for sure. First, Arteta was right. This was a bad refereeing
decision. Most obviously it was factually wrong. Ballard very skilfully pulled Konsa down on top of
him, while simultaneously feigning shock that such a thing could happen.

It was doubly bad because the referee's response was pre-planned, flagged by the early-season chat
about refereeing in a more interventionist fashion. Go into any situation with a voice in your head,
a vaguely worded mission to fulfil: this will skew your judgment.

The second thing is that referees are not biased. They're just not very good. Two things have
happened to them. First, the barrage of information to be absorbed in real time during a game is now
utterly mind-boggling.

Most referees are not from a high-pressure data-processing background. These are not military pilots
or traders on a currency floor. Less than a quarter have an academic degree. The task is beyond
them. This is not a slur or an insult. It's a simple description of what is happening, as
supported by the evidence.

This is doubly annoying because referees have become fundamentally confused about their role.
Referees are not ringmasters, or curators of the spectacle. They don't get to decide what our sport
should look like. Pro Ref is a private company with no _locus standi_ to rule on the aesthetics of
football. A referee should exist at the very edge of the spectacle. You're not a local government
planning officer with a mandate to decide which double-glazed French doors are or aren't in
character with the neighbourhood. Stop getting football not just wrong, but _wrong_.

[Mikel Arteta has a word with the fourth official, Elliot Bell]
But Arteta was also ready for this. Arsenal's coaches will have known this would happen at some
point. That press conference response was already in the chamber. Arteta may even have practised his
sad-not-angry face, his key lines.

And this would be the correct thing to do. None of this is breaking the rules, just as Arsenal are
not a dirty team or a bunch of cheats. They play the game that is being presented to us, a sport
that is being made to operate at the edge of its own ludicrously micro‑analysed rules on small
issues of physical contact.

Arteta's Arsenal have been masterful at finding advantages in this. Defenders are very good at
playing with the ball at their feet now. Defenders are not selected for their ability to resist
physical challenges. It is logical to exploit this, just as it is logical to seek to protect your
ability to do so by speaking in public about how the rules are applied. This is why Arteta is good,
and why he wins.

It is also true that this has created a strange environment. The tone of the wider Arsenal fanbase
is another element here. There is no one type of football fan. All human life is here. But the fact
remains, the loudest voices in the online Arsenal hive mind, even the atmosphere in the stadium is
unique in its absolute conviction of victimhood, of conspiracy, of a galvanising sense of adversity.

There is no other ground where every single refereeing decision, even the who-knows 50-50s, is so
violently and eloquently disputed. There are supporters who seem to spend most of the game up on
their feet in a state of performative, weaponised indignation, whose entire interaction with the
spectacle is an attitude of aggrieved injustice.

This makes sense, too. It speaks directly to the game we have created. Aggrieved middle-aged rule
pedantry. This is the new cauldron of hate.

[Dan Ballard fouls Gabriel.]
Is it based in reality? Not perhaps on the outline numbers. Arsenal have been awarded more fouls
than any other team this season. Only six teams last season had more fouls awarded against them, and
the same the year before. They had the fewest yellow cards last season. They haven't had a single
red since they had six of them in 2024‑25.

Still, certain key incidents continue to grate and fester. There was the incorrectly denied penalty
claim for Bruno Guimarães at Aston Villa. The balls-up with VAR lines against Brentford in 2023,
which, if it really was deliberate conspiracy-cheating, was the worst, most public attempt at
deliberate conspiracy-cheating ever conceived.

There is discontent at the way some rules seem to be applied more severely to Arsenal, the silly
little stuff like delaying or fiddling about with restarts. But then again, if you make the game
about that, if you plan to play on the margins, and successfully so, more stuff might happen in that
place while you're doing it.

Arteta has also played the role of arms-length rage conjurer expertly, has gone from rarely
commenting on referees to heartfelt and carefully targeted critic. The Sunderland penalty award was
a first opportunity to unload on the refereeing backlash to Arsenal's tactics, to launch his
pre-prepared defence; and to point out something demonstrably true. Referees have lost control of
this, because the game has got beyond them.

In many ways this was a perfect storm for Arteta. Not just a decision that didn't matter, because
the penalty was saved. But a decision that was clearly incorrect too, a chance to get your lobbying
in while very obviously in the right.

There will be more of this as the season plays out, as Arsenal work to preserve the advantages they
have made for themselves in close quarters, tactical attack and defence. Factor in that masterful
turn at a press conference _j'accuse_, and Saturday was a significant win in more ways than one.
